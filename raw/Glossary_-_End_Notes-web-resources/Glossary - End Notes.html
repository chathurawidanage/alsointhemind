Glossary

The terms listed below are from the Pāli language—the language of the early Buddhist scriptures—and provide the English meaning and context for essential concepts found in this work.

Ācariya: A teacher or master.

Adhiṭṭhāna: Determination or Resolution. It refers to the unwavering commitment to a course of practice, a virtue, or a specific spiritual goal.

Ādibrahmacāriya: The beginning of the holy life; the foundational training that establishes the conditions for deeper practice and liberation.

Akusala: Unwholesome, unskillful, or demeritorious actions or thoughts.

Anāgāmi: A “Non-returner”; the third of the four stages of enlightenment, a being who will not be reborn in the human realm but in a pure abode.

Anāryan: “Ignoble” or “worldling” (puthujjana). A person who has not yet attained any of the four stages of enlightenment, characterized by the persistent view of a permanent “self.”

Anatta: “Not-self”; the core Buddhist doctrine that there is no permanent,
unchanging soul or self in any phenomenon.

Anicca: Impermanence; the doctrine that all conditioned things are in a constant state of flux and subject to change.

Arahant: A “worthy one”; a fully enlightened being who has attained Nibbāna and ended the cycle of rebirth. An arahant, having uprooted all delusion, no longer creates new kamma; their actions are instead termed kiriya (inoperative) and do not produce future results, thus ending the cycle of rebirth.

Aranya: A forest hermitage or monastery.

Aryan (Sanskrit: Ārya; Pāli: Ariya): Noble; a “Noble One.” This term carries three distinct meanings depending on context. In the Buddha’s teaching, it refers to a lineage of virtue—one who has reached a transformative realization of the Dhamma and entered the path to liberation. Historically, it also refers to the ancient Indo-Aryan ethno-linguistic group of the Indian subcontinent. In modern history, the term was bastardized by the Nazi regime, who misappropriated the Indian term to promote a “superior race” ideology. In the scriptural context, however, true nobility is strictly spiritual; it is earned through the internal work of shedding defilements—greed, hatred, and delusion—rather than by birth or ancestry.

Asubha (saññā): Contemplation on the 32 components the body is made up of: head-hairs, body-hairs, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinews, bones, marrow, kidneys, heart, liver, pleura, spleen, lungs, intestines, intestinal tract, stomach, faeces, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, grease, saliva, nasal mucus, synovium [oil lubricating the joints], urine and brain. There is nothing attractive in nature found in these, hence one sees how unworthy the body is of being identified with. It is used as an antidote to lust.

Aṭapirikara: (Sinhala) The traditional offering of the eight basic requisites for a Buddhist monk, including robes, an alms bowl, a razor, etc.

Avijjā: Fundamental ignorance or delusion. This is not a lack of worldly information, but a deep-seated misperception of reality. Specifically, it is the ignorance of the Four Noble Truths, which causes one to see what is impermanent as permanent, what is suffering as pleasure, and what is not-self as a self. It is considered the root cause that drives the entire cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra)

Bhikkhu/Bhikkhunī: A fully ordained Buddhist monk (bhikkhu) or nun
(bhikkhunī).

Brahmā-loka / Brahma World: A very high and long-lived celestial being (Brahma) or realm (loka) attained through deep meditation. Despite its peace, it is still part of the impermanent cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra).

Buddhānussati: Recollection or mindfulness of the qualities of the Buddha; one of the protective meditations.

Cārikā: A journey on foot, especially the traditional ascetic practice of a wandering monk who does not stay in one place for long.

Dāna: The act of giving, generosity, or charity; an offering, especially of food, to monastics. Dāna hall is the place where people offer.

Deva/Devatā: A deity or celestial being, often compared to an angel. However, they are still mortal, unenlightened, and bound to the cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra). While the terms are often used interchangeably, deva is the general term for a god, whereas devatā can refer more specifically to a localized spirit or guardian divinity.

Dhamma/Dhammas: A term with two main meanings depending on context. Dhamma (capitalized) is the Buddha’s Teaching as a whole, or the ultimate Truth. In meditation, mindfulness of dhammas (lowercase) refers to contemplating mental objects or phenomena, often grouped into categories like the Five Hindrances or the Four Noble Truths.

Dhammapada: “Verses of the Dhamma”; a beloved collection of the Buddha’s sayings in verse form.

Dhātu: An element or natural property. Refers to the four great elements (‘solidity’ or earth, ‘fluidity’ or water, ‘heat’ or fire, and ‘motion’ or air) that are the fundamental components of all physical matter. By seeing that one’s own body is composed of the same elements as the world outside, this contemplation helps deconstruct the idea of a solid ‘self.’

Dosa: Aversion, hatred, or ill will; one of the three root defilements. Sometimes means fault(s).

Dukkha: Suffering, stress, unsatisfactoriness; the first of the Four Noble Truths.

Indriya: Faculties or controlling principles. In the context of meditation, this most often refers to the five spiritual faculties: faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā). The practice involves developing and balancing these five qualities to lead the mind toward liberation.

Jhāna: A state of deep meditative absorption characterized by profound stillness and concentration.

Kālaṁ āgameyya: “Awaiting one’s time.” A phrase used to describe the final stage of a practitioner’s path, referring to the practice of mindfully and equanimously awaiting death. It is a contemplation of the body’s inevitable decay, done with an unagitated mind.

Kamma (Karma): (Pāli/Sanskrit) The universal law of cause and effect where intentional actions of body (kāya), speech (vacī), and mind (mano) determine one’s future experiences. These actions are primarily categorized by their nature and results:

• Wholesome (kusala) kamma refers to actions rooted in skillful qualities like generosity and kindness. While skillful, they are performed by one who has not yet eradicated the fundamental ignorance (avijjā), and thus lead to favorable rebirth.

• Unwholesome (akusala) kamma refers to actions rooted in greed, hatred, and delusion (lobha, dosa, moha), which lead to painful results.

• Imperturbable (āneñja) kamma is a specific type of wholesome kamma related to deep meditative states, leading to rebirth in a formless realm.

Khandha Paritta: A protective chant from the Suttas, recited to generate loving-kindness towards snakes and other creatures.

Kuṭi: A small hut or simple dwelling for a monk or nun.

Lobha: Greed, desire, or attachment; one of the three root defilements.

Magga-phala: “Path and Fruition.” A term for the specific, supramundane moments of consciousness that mark the attainment of the four stages of enlightenment. The Path (magga) is a single moment of insight that cuts through specific defilements, while the Fruition (phala) is the immediate result—the direct experience of the peace of Nibbāna.

Mahāthera: “Great Elder”; a title of respect for a senior monk, typically with over twenty years of ordination.

Māra: The “Evil One” or “Great Deceiver”; a deity who personifies temptation, illusion, and the forces that bind beings to the cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra). While depicted as a being who tempted the Buddha, Māra can also be understood as a metaphor for the internal defilements—such as greed, hatred, and fear—that a practitioner must overcome in their own mind.

Maraṇānussati: Recollection or mindfulness of death, used to cultivate urgency and reduce attachment.

Mettā (meditation): The meditative practice of cultivating boundless goodwill towards all beings.

Moha: Delusion or ignorance; one of the three root defilements.

Nibbāna (Nirvana): (Pāli/Sanskrit) The ultimate goal of Buddhism; the complete cessation of suffering and the extinguishing of the “fires” of greed, hatred, and delusion.

Nīvaraṇa: The Five Hindrances; a group of five unwholesome mental states that obstruct the mind during meditation and block the development of wisdom. They are: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, and doubt.

Pacceka Buddha: Commonly referred to as a “silent” or “private” Buddha. An individual who attains full enlightenment on their own, without a teacher, during an age when the Buddha’s teachings are not available in the world. Paccekabuddhas are, in reality, neither silent nor private. Additionally, they do teach in a conventional way, but not the ultimate analysis. They also do not establish a Sāsana.

Pāli Canon: The collection of primary scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism, written in the Pāli language and consisting of three “baskets”: the Sutta Piṭaka (discourses), the Vinaya Piṭaka (monastic discipline), and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka (philosophy).

Pañcupādānakkhandhā: The “five aggregates subject to clinging.” This is the Buddha’s analysis of a person’s experience into five components: form (the body), feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. According to the teaching, suffering arises from our clinging to or identifying with these constantly changing processes as a permanent “self.”

Paṭiccasamuppāda: Dependent Origination; the doctrine that all phenomena arise in dependence on other phenomena, explaining the process that perpetuates suffering.

Piṇḍapāta: The monastic practice of collecting alms food by walking with a bowl. (More strictly, this is called piṇḍacāra; piṇḍapāta is what is dropped into the bowl.)

Pirith/Paritta: (Sinhala/Pāli) A collective term for a set of protective chants or discourses from the Pāli Canon.

Poya/Uposatha: (Sinhala/Pāli) A day of religious observance in Sri Lanka, typically falling on the full moon, when devout lay Buddhists often observe eight precepts.

Puthujjana: A “worldling” or ordinary person who has not yet attained any of the four stages of enlightenment and thus remains bound to the cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra). The opposite of a Noble One (ariya).

Rishi/Isi: (Sanskrit/Pāli) A title meaning a sage, seer, or revered teacher. It is used in Hindu, Jain, and other Indian spiritual traditions.

Rūpa: Form, matter, or physical phenomena. It is the first of the Five Aggregates of Clinging (pañcupādānakkhandhā). According to the Suttas, all rūpa is composed of the four great elements (mahābhūta): the properties of earth (solidity), water (cohesion), fire (heat), and air (motion). It also includes derived form (upādāya-rūpa), which consists of the five physical sense faculties (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body) and their corresponding sense objects (sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and tangible things).

Samādhi: Meditative concentration. A key part of the Noble Eightfold Path, it is the practice of unifying the mind to serve as the foundation for wisdom (paññā).

Samatha: “Calm-abiding” or tranquility. A category of meditative practices designed to quiet the mind and develop deep concentration (samādhi). Along with insight (Vipassanā), it is one of the two pillars of Buddhist meditation; a calm mind developed through samatha provides a powerful foundation for seeing reality clearly.

Saṁsāra: The endless cycle of birth, death, and rebirth, characterized by suffering and driven by ignorance and craving.

Saṁvega Vatthu: A sense of spiritual urgency or holy dread that arises from contemplating the dangers of saṁsāra (e.g., old age, sickness, and death). This feeling is a powerful catalyst for dedicated practice.

Saṅgha: The community of Buddhist monastics (bhikkhus and bhikkhunīs); can also refer to the community of all awakened or noble disciples. The distinction is between Ariyasaṅgha (community of awakened ones) and sammutisaṅgha (ordained disciples aiming at becoming ariya).

Saṅkhāra: A Buddhist term with two main meanings depending on context; its essential characteristic is that it relates to conditioned phenomena.

1. As the fourth of the Five Aggregates, the saṅkhāra-khandha is defined as the six classes of volition: volition regarding forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tactile objects, and ideas.

2. As the second link in Dependent Origination (avijjā-paccayā saṅkhārā), it refers to kamma-forming volitional actions—the wholesome and unwholesome actions of body, speech, and mind, born from ignorance, that propel the cycle of rebirth.

Sāsana: The dispensation, teachings, and legacy of a Buddha.

Sati: Mindfulness; awareness of the present moment.

Sīla: Morality, virtue, or ethical conduct, often undertaken by observing precepts.

1. Ājīvaṭṭhamaka Sīla: The “eight precepts with right livelihood.” This includes the standard five precepts (no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, or intoxicants) plus three additional rules focused on wholesome speech and ethical livelihood.

2. Aṭṭhaṅgika Sīla: The “eightfold morality” typically observed on special retreat or Uposatha days. It includes the basic precepts (with full celibacy) plus ascetic rules such as not eating after noon and avoiding entertainment or luxuries.

3. Ariyakanta Sīla: Virtue that is “dear to the Noble Ones.” This refers to flawless, unbroken morality that has become natural to the practitioner. It is no longer just following rules, but a state of integrity that directly supports deep meditation.

Sīmā: Boundary or consecrated area. In monastic law (Vinaya), the sīmā is the specific, legally established ground where the Saṅgha (community of monks) conducts formal legal ceremonies like ordinations (Upasampadā) and the recitation of the Pātimokkha (monastic rules).

Sutra/Sutta: (Sanskrit/Pāli) A discourse or sermon of the Buddha.

Swami: (Sanskrit) An honorific title from the Indian subcontinent, meaning “master” or “one who is master of oneself.” While it is most commonly used for Hindu spiritual teachers, in Sri Lanka, it is also used colloquially as a term of deep respect for Buddhist monks in some regions.

Thero/Thera: Honorific title used for a Buddhist monk who has been fully ordained (upasampadā) for ten or more Vassa (rainy season retreats). It denotes seniority and respect.

Upekkhā: Equanimity; a state of balanced, unshakable peace of mind that is not swayed by pleasure or pain.

Vinaya: The code of monastic discipline for the Saṅgha.

Vipassanā: Insight; meditation that cultivates a direct seeing into the true nature of reality, especially into the three characteristics of impermanence, suffering, and not-self.

Yoniso manasikāra: A right means of attending that is in accordance with the truth. That is attention to the way things really are: impermanent as impermanent etc. Engaging in yoniso manasikāra leads to the awakening of the eye of wisdom.

A Note on Sources and
Methodology

This book was carefully reviewed by a team of over ten people, including the joint editors of Pure Inspiration: Ven. Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera—Recollections of His Life, Practice and Teachings and who both knew Ven. Ñāṇavimala personally—together with other monastic practitioners and lay practitioners, some of whom also knew Ven. Ñāṇavimala personally.

The process involved extensive research: reviewing hundreds of hours of Ven. Gnanaloka's public talks in Sinhala and English, studying the Suttas referenced in the notes, cross-checking dates and details, and consulting existing records such as Pure Inspiration and Life of Nyanatiloka Thera—The Biography of a Western Buddhist Pioneer.

As for the writing team, between us we brought nearly fifty years of meditation practice—including backgrounds in social research and conducting interviews, psychology and medical science—deeply influenced by time in Saddhammadhara Sutta retreats with Ven. Gnanaloka, as well as experience in various traditions such as the Thai Forest, Mahāsi, and Goenka.

The original notes are Ven. Gnanaloka's, written between 1994 and 2001 during his training under Ven. Ñāṇavimala. The Reflections were created by our writing team through:

• Over twenty extended interviews with Ven. Gnanaloka over two years (2022–2024).

• Sessions typically lasting 3–4 hours, often beginning near midnight.

• Review of hundreds of hours of his public Dhamma talks in both Sinhala and English.

• Cross-referencing with existing biographical resources such as Pure Inspiration.

• Multiple rounds of review by Ven. Gnanaloka himself.

A challenge for the writers was piecing together the patchwork of stories available about such a monk shrouded in mystery and verifying its credibility. While Ven. Gnanaloka could personally verify the vast majority of these accounts, a few instances, perhaps three in total, rely on other credible sources. We intentionally excluded a few lay accounts that veered into the downright mystical; though Ven. Gnanaloka was aware of some of these stories, they remained unverifiable and were thus omitted to maintain the integrity of the record.

Acquiring time for an interview could be incredibly difficult, sometimes waiting months just to have a chance to talk with Ven. Gnanaloka. The writers had to get creative, taking every opportunity, and sometimes inventing them, to gather information. At times, our persistence may have bordered on the intrusive, yet Ven. Gnanaloka always welcomed our dedication to the task. On one occasion during a car trip while Ven. Gnanaloka was giving retreats, one writer attempted handing him printed sheets to review from the back seat. Ven. Gnanaloka was recovering from minor health issues at the time. As the sheets were passed forward, he commented in an amused way, “When I am dying, will you also be handing me sheets to review?”

Ven. Gnanaloka insists this story be included in the book. It was likely his way of highlighting the great lengths to which we devotees went to complete this work. We are grateful for his patience, and ask forgiveness if we at times overstepped.

Much of the writing work was done at Sri Sambuddha
Ramaneeya Ashramaya Forest Hermitage during retreats—an environment of practice that shaped our reflections.

Our role was to preserve and transmit—to provide context, narrative, and accessibility while remaining faithful to Ven. Gnanaloka's accounts and his teacher's approach.

In developing these narrative sections, we chose to include
specific accounts of Ven. Gnanaloka’s training and experiences to illustrate the practical application of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s instructions. We have included these at the editors' insistence, as we believe these moments provide essential illustration of how the Dhamma functions as a protective and transformative force—and as a final testimony to the transformative power of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s presence and guidance. Any errors or shortcomings in this transmission are ours alone.

This book represents a living Dhamma transmission in the oral tradition. The teachings passed from Ven. Ñāṇavimala to Ven. Gnanaloka through direct encounter and practice (1990–2005). Ven. Gnanaloka preserved them in handwritten notes (1994–2001). We received them through interview and study (2023–2026), and now transmit them in written form to readers.

In this sense, the book continues the lineage of direct transmission—from teacher to student to student—that has preserved the Buddha's teachings for 2,600 years.

Acknowledgements

This book has been a journey of faith and perseverance, made possible only through the collective goodwill of many individuals. It was an entirely volunteer-based effort that spanned almost three years, from November 2023 to August 2026.

Our deepest gratitude goes, first and foremost, to the memory of Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera, whose profound life of renunciation is the very heart of this work. His ascetic, unwavering example continues to be a source of immense inspiration.

Though we have called it three years, this book has in truth been thirty-two years in the making. It began not in 2023, but in February 1994, when a young man named Shanaka first began to jot down the words of his teacher—brief notes, taken in the moment, without any thought of a book. That he wrote them at all, and kept them safe across three decades of a wandering, ascetic life, is the reason these pages exist. What was preserved for one student's practice has become an inheritance for many.

We are profoundly indebted to Venerable Bambalapitiye Gnanaloka Thera. He not only gave his permission for this project but
generously shared the private, handwritten notes of his teacher, entrusting them to us. Given his ascetic nature and deep com-mitment to solitude, meeting with him was a rare and precious opportunity. Over the course of this project, a small team of writers was granted several extensive interviews—some lasting many hours—both in Sri Lanka and during his travels abroad. During these invaluable sessions, he patiently answered our questions, clarified ambiguities in the decades-old notes, and reviewed the reflections to ensure they faithfully preserved the spirit and meaning of his encounters with his master. We hope this book does justice to his trust.

This work would not exist without the tireless efforts of a dedicated team of volunteer editors and reviewers, both lay and monastic, whose insightful feedback helped to shape and refine this
manuscript.

The monastery regularly hosts Saddhammadhara Meditation Retreats for both monastics and lay visitors. Following a framework inspired by Ven. Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera, these retreats emphasize a quiet, self-guided exploration of the Buddha’s teachings through direct engagement with the Suttas. As Ven. Ñāṇavimala envisioned, the ultimate intention is to inspire suitable individuals toward the renunciant path.
