Chapter 05

Always Be with Thoughts of Mettā
(April 1995 – July 1995)

14 April 1995

x Sutta No. 107—Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna)—where, when doing other work one must also practice. Not just to always stay in seated meditation.

x When not well, keep the mind calm, practise Buddhānussati and make the mind happy. Calm the mind with a meditation object.

x Daily reflect on the three ways of conduct and, before sleeping, practice mettā bhāvanā. That is kamma which will give vipāka (change the situation).

x Daily reflect on the 5 contemplations (Maharagama Vath Pirith).1

x First Sīla (three ways of conduct), Right View (Word of the Buddha), Satipaṭṭhāna (Overcoming the hindrances), Jhāna, vipassanā.

x When going on caṅkamana (walking meditation), if a worldly thought arises, consider it as “it is not mine.”

x This; three ways of conduct [tīṇi sucaritāni], is it difficult?2

15 April 1995

x Know the way you should try to attain Jhāna—(the way is described in Suttas; Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna) and Dantabhūmi Sutta (MN125; the grade of the tamed) of Majjhima Nikāya).

x When meditating, certain pleasant, unpleasant or sensual thoughts connected to the past may arise. What is past is not important. The present is important.

x You can notice how moment to moment objects are always changing.

x The present moment or situation due to the past way of conduct is something we cannot brush aside, but we may act in such a manner that no greed, hate or delusion may arise and that is kamma.

x In the past for many births, we have depended on feelings for our happiness.

x Death is nothing but breaking up of this present body which never belonged to us and the mind getting a new base to get on.

x The art of living in the present; it takes time to get established in that; not by days or weeks, but years.

17 April 1995

x Control of talking and thinking, Suttas—Kāyagatāsati Sutta (MN119; mindfulness of the body), Mahāsuññata Sutta (MN122; the greater discourse on voidness) and Cūḷasuññata Sutta (MN121; the shorter discourse on voidness) of [Majjhima Nikāya].

x Talk only on Dhamma, otherwise be quiet.

x Thinking and reasoning should stop a bit.

x No point knowing, preaching and so forth many Suttas. If one is not meditating, then one is not Dhammavihāri— [Paṭhamadhammavihārī Sutta (AN5.73; one who dwells in the Dhamma, first)].

x It is good to know many Suttas thoroughly and meanwhile meditate.

x There is no point looking for external support. The whole practice is dwindling away but make sure what is inside—the state of mind is kept undisturbed and pure.

x When you are old you have to depend on others, more bondages. Therefore, more suffering.

x In the library, you may find the Suttas in Pāli and an English translation. You may read both.

20 April 1995

x Whenever there are inclinations (in the sensual world) don’t try to fight them. It is impossible. But divert the mind to thoughts of Dhamma or recite certain verses.

x Watch the mind and keep it purified.

x Anyone who delights in feelings, delights in suffering.

x In the beginning read certain Suttas, but as you practice and go on you may read more accordingly. Don't just go [straight] to reading more Suttas, practice is important.

x Generally, a teacher should be one who has entered the path.

13 May 1995

x Start with Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) [by] read[ing] the Word of the Buddha [by Venerable Nyanatiloka Mahāthera] etc.

x Tradition is so dangerous. That’s why you should stick to the Suttas.3

x When going on [cārikā—wandering on foot] as a Bhikkhu, you have to go on piṇḍapāta (alms-round). All the difficulty. When no illusion.

14 May 1995

x Read Suttas; Sallekha Sutta (MN8; effacement), Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta (MN9; right view), Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty).

x When you see Paṭiccasamuppāda (Dependent Origination) you see Dhamma.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).

x Read Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).

x Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases) is an expansion of Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) through the six sense doors. The Suttas and so on are similar.

x You may talk on the Dhamma to whoever shows interest, but you must do this with your example. You may do with means that are suitable to them tactfully.

x Whether someone has entered the path and how far he has progressed one cannot say. Only an Arahant (Fully Enlightened Being) knows for certain when he comes to that (that is his arahantship). This sort of questioning is meaningless.

x You must look after your welfare first. This is done by Satipaṭṭhāna (foundation of mindfulness).

x Our teacher is the All Enlightened One

x Aṅguttara Nikāya 6.85—Sītibhāva Sutta (AN6.85; coolness)

x You must deal with the different nīvaraṇā [hindrances] with suitable objects of meditation.

x Reading of new Suttas should be done with practice.

x Working toward a certain worldly position etc. they are all to do with feelings. But all that ultimately comes to an end by old age and death.

x Always health is taken over by sickness, youth by old age and life by death.

x Sickness may come suddenly; we do not know what kamma we have done. The sickness of the body cannot be overcome but the sickness of the mind can be. It is very important to purify the mind from lobha (greed), dosa (hate), moha (delusion). The best conditions for recovery from physical sickness is mettā bhāvanā, high puñña kamma and upekkhā etc.

x Even if difficult situations come, you mustn’t let the mind get disturbed. [The] mind should abide... should abide blissfully every time.

15 May 1995

x All your efforts are not in vain. They will follow you like your shadow.

17 May 1995

x You must learn to accommodate with the place
or environment to create harmony but not at the expense of akusala kamma (unwholesome actions). That you should know.

x There is happiness in married life and in renounced life. There is a difference. What is this married life? It is an illusion, an intoxication. It is due to not knowing things as they are. Take the example of Venerable Mahākassapa. How both stayed together and departed in separate ways. That is possible.4

x Even if someone is kind and restrained, but it is an obstacle to stop this saṁsāra (the cycle of suffering and rebirth).

x Do your studies also properly. You may retire from time to time as you do now.5

x Simile of the sailor, very important, crossing the river [Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s own simile, detailed in the 27 April 1994 entry].

x You may feel this is right and that is right.

x You mustn’t enforce on others. But show with your example.

x Always [be] with thoughts of mettā etc.

18 May 1995

x Arahants look at all things with upekkhā (equanimity).

x Some arahants with abhiññā (supernormal powers) and the Buddha had the ability to see the attainments of others.

x Until you come to arahantship (full enlightenment) one doesn’t know. So it is good to have or continue to develop saddhā.

x You would not always have [quiet] suitable surroundings. I had it only twice in life.6

x You must make use of all situations to come to your goal. Obstacles should be made use of. You have to get involved in certain activities, that is unavoidable. Therefore, when you are engaged in such activity, direct your full concentration to that.

x Regarding the Suttas, certain things omitted by the Buddha means the person the Buddha talks to has already cultivated what is omitted. Sometimes things are omitted because certain preliminary things have to be cultivated [first].

x Your studies; they will also come to an end. It might come to an end suddenly.

x Though we have lived in a good way this life, we don’t know what we have done in the past births. So certain painful kamma vipāka (ripening of past actions), we should learn to bear it up with equanimity.

x Mettā bhāvanā and Buddhānussati.

x You must do dāna, sīla and bhāvanā. Never neglect bhāvanā. [Do it] daily.

x Due to past kamma certain urges may come. That you must take care [by not acting on those urges].

x Not letting the mind go to the future and the past, sticking to the presently arisen Dhammas with perfect knowledge.

19 May 1995

x Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases)—Very important.

x Attadatthaṁ paratthena (look after your welfare etc.)—
Dhp 166.

x His last words: Dhamma and the Vinaya is our teacher.7

x Always this path is against the stream. So, you should cut across like the sailor. You must sometimes accommodate, but direct it slowly to the goal.8

x Don’t get upset by any situation. Our kamma vipāka has to take place.

x Don’t have a fixed idea about the future or how you get about. So just get on peacefully moment to moment.

x All your efforts are not in vain.

x The highest way you can respect the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha is through the practice.

28 May 1995

x You may concentrate on the foundation—not thinking of jhāna etc. nor to be a scholar, but as the practice goes on you may refer further to Suttas and could categorize them under ‘sīla’ (virtue), ‘samādhi’ (concentration) and ‘paññā’ (wisdom).

x Iṣṭa Devatā [guardian celestial being] is there so you have nothing to worry.

x Studies: You may do your duty with maximum effort on your part, so that you would have no inner remorse or conflict concerning your duty, but the result should be expected with equanimity.

x You may learn Pāli [language] when you see the necessity
for it.

x Certain verses you may recite and memorise in Pāli, [those] which appeal to you.

x Mirror of the Dhamma,9 daily reflection on death etc.

x May you put brick by brick etc.

x Mustn’t be negligent and also mustn’t be too eager, but the middle way.

x Hope your effort and understanding will continue.

x Not letting the mind go to the future and the past, sticking to the presently arisen Dhammas with perfect insight as to its true nature of impermanence, suffering and therefore not mine.
And at that time no lobha, dosa and moha (no craving, clinging
—paṭiccasamuppāda), then the mind is directed to Nibbāna.

x “Nibbānaṁ paramaṁ sukhaṁ” [“Nibbāna is the ultimate happiness/bliss”].

26 June 1995

x Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna), Dantabhūmi Sutta (MN125; the grade of the tamed), Sekha Sutta (MN53; the disciple in higher training), Ariyapariyesanā Sutta (MN26; the noble search).

x Saṁyutta Nikāya, Khandhasaṁyutta and Saḷāyatanasaṁ- yutta—some you may memorise.

x Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta (DN22; the greater discourse on the foundations of mindfulness).

x We are always going to either the past or the future. But if we stick to the present, you would then experience anicca. That which is anicca is dukkha. That which is anicca and dukkha is not mine.

x A bhikkhu has no attendant. When you go on cārikā you are quite alone. If you are not well, the hospital is there free of charge.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).

x Don’t think of this personal services, but think of [the]highest form of merit.10

x Buddhānussati (Recollection of the Buddha).

x Maraṇasati (Mindfulness of Death).

x Asubha bhāvanā (Contemplation of Impurity/Foulness) (already in Satipaṭṭhāna, when you are practicing there is no ignorance, no craving, no lobha, dosa, and moha—greed, hatred, delusion.).

x Saṁyutta Nikāya (Read Khandha and Saḷāyatana Vagga).

29 June 1995

x Saṁyutta 22 and 35 onwards—Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).

x No avijjā no saṅkhāra, no saṅkhāra no bhava, no jāti no old age, no sickness, no death. What doesn’t arise doesn’t pass away.11

x Read Satipaṭṭhāna. Practice it. The nimittas [mental signs/objects] are there for samādhi in Satipaṭṭhāna.

x Must attain jhāna. It is the suitable condition for entering the paths.

x You may read Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days). Where it is mentioned, how you respect the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha in the
highest way, is only through the practice. The other thing is, though you hear that a certain teacher is famous or popular, compare it with the Dhamma and the Vinaya. So the Dhamma and the Vinaya is our teacher.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā) is very important. After the vandanā [worship].

x Nobody can make another practice or attain. They can only show like the Buddha. They are only Dhamma kāya.12

x Read certain passages, verses and ones that appeal to you, memorise them or recall them.

8 July 1995

x Solitude is most necessary for the understanding to arise. As when there are distractions through the sense doors, the required concentration used to investigate the Dhamma is disturbed.

11 July 1995

x Formations will never be complete and perfect. So you must learn not to be dissatisfied, but to get on with inner peace and upekkhā [equanimity]. And that upekkhā too you
should reflect its true nature. It is consciousness. It arises and passes too. Seeing it in this way you continue up until arahantship. This inner peace (upekkhā) you should see as it is, without delusion.

x [Whether] I am there or not make sure you retire for the poya.13

x And before you sleep, make sure you reflect and do mettā bhāvanā, or in other words go to sleep with a raised or elevated mind.

x When you have attained the path, try your best to establish others who are first connected to you by birth, and more beings as possible.

x Make sure while you’re doing your studies that you retire from time to time.

12 July 1995

x Face facts with confidence (in general in all situations).

x Saḷāyatana, Khandha, Ānāpānasati, Bojjhaṅga—[Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases), Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates), Ānāpānasati Sutta (MN118; mindfulness of breathing), Bojjhaṅga Saṁyutta (SN46; connected discourses on the factors of enlightenment)].

x When you are with other people, don’t be separated, but with compassion direct them by your example.

x Always associate and be close to people and places that have an understanding for this Dhamma.

  1. 1 The five contemplations, as found in the Abhiṇhapaccavekkhitabbaṭhāna Sutta (AN5.57; themes), are five facts the Buddha taught for daily reflection: that one is subject to aging, illness, and death and has not gone beyond them; that one will be separated from all that is dear; and that one is the owner and heir of one’s own actions (kamma). This is also commonly found in Sinhala chanting texts, such as the Maharagama Pirith Book (the English equivalent being ‘The Mirror of the Dhamma’ compiled by Venerable Nārada Mahāthera and Venerable Kassapa Thera).

  2. 2 Ven. Ñāṇavimala stresses this reflection of bodily, verbal and mental actions,
    reflecting before, during and after, as a foundational practice that should be done always. Ven. Gnanaloka comments “I’ve noticed that those who have developed     samādhi, they have that developed in their mind (also).” Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice about doing things “slowly, mindfully, carefully” goes with this.

  3. 3 Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s emphasis here is on rejecting practices that deviate from the teachings found in the Suttas, such as rituals. He, however, cautioned against being openly critical, as noted on 21 April 1994 about the danger in doing that—“even what is left will disappear.” The best approach is not to encourage such practices.

  4. 4 Venerable Mahākassapa and Venerable Bhaddā Kapilānī, both Arahants, were once husband and wife who lived in a celibate marriage and later renounced household life by mutual consent.

  5. 5 At that time Shanaka was pursuing his higher studies, and Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s comment “do your studies properly” is regarding that, seeing that Shanaka was perhaps neglecting this aspect.

  6. 6 Ven. Ñāṇavimala would also add that being in solitude is itself a result of kamma. In other words, do not attach to solitude or continuously long for it. Such periods arise and cease depending on conditions.

  7. 7 Referring to the Buddha’s last words before his passing—Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days).

  8. 8 “You must sometimes accommodate”—means one must learn to be flexible, sometimes it may seem like one is even going with the current, but slowly gradually directing it according to the conditions, without trying to cut across directly. In a practical real world sense, this may appear as they have to take a decision such as taking a job or duty in marriage, in order to fulfill a certain worldly responsibility. However even in the face of those decisions, one must gradually steer it tactfully towards the goal.

  9. 9 Recommended by Ven. Ñāṇavimala was ‘The Mirror of the Dhamma: A Manual of Buddhist Chanting and Devotional Texts’, compiled by Nārada Thera and Bhikkhu Kassapa. This English-language manual served a similar function to the traditional Sinhala Vath Pirith Potha, published by the Siri Vajiragnana Dharmayathanaya Temple at Maharagama.

  10. 10 Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice was directly related to Shanaka’s service to him during his final years. He taught that while physical assistance creates merit, the highest form of merit is gained through the disciple’s own sustained purification of mind in meditation.

  11. 11 This statement reflects the reverse sequence of paṭiccasamuppāda (dependent origination), showing the cessation aspect (paṭiloma-paṭiccasamuppāda). When avijjā (ignorance) ceases, saṅkhārā (volitional formations) cease; with the cessation of formations, there is no bhava (becoming), and consequently no jāti (birth), jarā (aging), vyādhi (sickness), or maraṇa (death). The phrase “What doesn’t arise doesn’t pass away” expresses the realization of nibbāna—the unconditioned state, free from arising and ceasing.

  12. 12 In the Theravāda tradition, Dhamma kāya means “embodiment of Dhamma.” Like the Buddha, even realized teachers cannot make another person practice or attain—they can only point out the way through the Dhamma they have realized.

  13. 13 Poya (full moon day) is a public holiday in Sri Lanka, and this instruction emphasizes dedicating the full day to intensified practice.
