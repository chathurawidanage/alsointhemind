Reflection 02

Returning to the Source

When Shanaka first met Ven. Ñāṇavimala, he was swept up in the charisma of his presence. There was nothing overt in his manner; if anything, it was understated and austere. Even in his eighties, body worn and frail, extraordinary alertness marked each step. Every movement seemed to arise from a deep inner silence.

One day at Polgasduwa Island Hermitage, Shanaka stood watching Ven. Ñāṇavimala walk toward the dining hall. A sense of awe filled him. The elder monk’s stillness felt majestic. A wave of devotion arose in Shanaka.

In that very instant, as if the thought had been spoken aloud, Ven. Ñāṇavimala stopped.

He turned.

His compassionate calm, piercing gaze met Shanaka's eyes directly:

“Your teacher and my teacher is the Dhamma—Vinaya.”

It was a direct response to his unspoken thoughts. How had he known? The words were familiar. Ven. Ñāṇavimala had said them before. But this time, spoken as if in direct answer to what he'd just been feeling, they settled deeply within him.

In time, Shanaka came to understand what Ven. Ñāṇavimala was pointing to. In the world, even the most sincere teachers can mislead, however unintentionally. Devotion can become entangled with personality, lineage, or charisma. But the Buddha, the true refuge, never lets one down. The Buddha offered a path grounded in direct experience. He asked for no blind faith, only careful and honest inquiry.

This very inquiry led Shanaka to appreciate the difficulty many modern seekers face. He began to understand why the Buddha’s words, though timeless, could feel so distant to modern seekers and why contemporary voices often seemed to reach them more easily.

The gap wasn’t only about words that didn’t quite fit in with the times. It was about resonance. And beneath that, something even subtler.

The unadorned power of the ancient words holds a subtle vibration that minds conditioned by constant noise struggle to detect. Truly penetrating the Suttas required stilling and re-attuning the mind. He found that first in the forests, where its primordial silence uncovered an intuition attuned to the Buddha's frequency.

He read a discourse given in the Great Wood near Vesālī, where a visiting crowd of Licchavis, the city’s ruling clan, had arrived in a long procession of fine carriages. As their noise filled the forest, a group of elder monks then withdrew to a secluded grove. The Buddha, praising their retreat, declared that even sound was a thorn, an obstacle to attaining the first of the sublime unified states of mind known in the Suttas as jhāna.1 Jhānas are states free from the five mental hindrances (nīvaraṇas): sensual desire (kāmacchanda), ill-will (vyāpāda), sloth and torpor (thīna-middha), restlessness and worry (uddhacca-kukkucca), and doubt (vicikicchā). He reflects that this still quality of mind free from hindrances like a jhāna is conducive to the arising of intuitive insight.

He now sees that modern spiritual practitioners face disturbance beyond just sensory noise. Today seekers are contending with an even louder noise—cognitive noise—the internal disturbances that drive one away from deeper cognitive powers needed to reach these states of mind.

Attention, manasikāra in Pāli, is a mental resource that, if used wisely, can bring insight toward liberation. This fundamental resource has been cultivated by meditative sages for millennia. Yet today, he notices a pattern. Attention has now become a commodity, captured and engineered to be profited from, rather than cultivated.

Technology now advances and gets adopted at an ever-
quickening pace. Brains do not evolve and update like technology does. What has become normal is a habituated chase for fleeting rewards—status updates, likes, addictive content and news
snippets, whilst juggling six different things at once—leading to what modern culture has termed ‘brain rot.’

That is the silent but destructive consequence: information overload, restless distraction and a rewiring toward shallow rewards. The very faculties of sustained attention and inner stillness a contemplative must cultivate are being worn away.

The problem stems from people reaching for technology not only for their necessary use, but also as an escape and a way to find a temporary release. This is a kind of ‘intoxication,’ he observes, where the craving within only grows. If craving grows, so does suffering. He quotes the Buddha: taṇhā paccayā dukkhaṁ uppajjati, taṇhā nirodhā dukkhaṁ na uppajjati2—with craving as a condition, suffering arises; with the cessation of craving, suffering does not arise.

“Going on not recognising the problem, is itself the big problem,” he says. “To practically begin any meditation, we have to recognise the thing that deteriorates the mind, and resolve not to do it.”

Research shows that the mere presence of one's own mobile phone impairs cognitive capacity for attention, even without actually checking the phone.3 This is because the brain has only so much capacity for attention, and it is being constantly consumed by simply resisting the urge to check it, even when the device is turned off. On top of this, the anxiety many feel when their phone is just kept away in another room reveals how deep the problem runs.4 This is the modern-day thorn to settling into stillness.

The brain isn't broken. It's under chronic strain. These conditions reshape not just behavior, but the brain's very structure and chemistry.

From his own experience, he had come to know the area behind the forehead as significant for discriminative wisdom—the seat of what he calls the wisdom eye (paññācakkhu).5 He says: “If you're really properly focusing, you will feel a kind of a tingling, or some activity, in the forehead. That eye is sleeping, but very subtle; the more you relax, you can open it. You have to relax as well as think.” In this part of the head, science locates what is said to be the most evolved region of the brain—the prefrontal cortex, central to managing thought, emotion and behaviour. Its outermost part is what manages distractions. When this regulatory system functions well, the mind can resist reactivity.

Deep within the brain sit the amygdala, twin almond-shaped structures that tag sensory objects with emotional value, creating the automatic pull towards what we crave and away from what we fear. When overstimulated and poorly regulated by the prefrontal cortex, reactive patterns dominate: fear when threatened, craving when enticed, aversion when confronted with the unpleasant. He saw in this a parallel to how the hindrances dominate an untrained mind.

But the brain can change. Meditation practices that cultivate the repeated return of attention to the present moment—mindfulness-based practice—have been shown to physically alter brain
structure. Studies of eight-week mindfulness programs found that
as stress diminished, grey matter density within the amygdala reduced while a key region of the frontal cortex involved in attention control increased.6 In other words, the structure that drives emotional reactivity was physically changing while the capacity for sustained focus was growing. In those who had practised for years, the prefrontal region was measurably larger.7

He now reflects: “If the prefrontal cortex is developed in a balanced way, it has the ability where it develops a certain power. We have to develop that to a certain extent where we are not distracted. Focus is under our control. It is a repeated practice.”

To guard that focus, he advises those on the spiritual path not to use devices like computers or phones for anything other than profession, studies or for spiritual activities. “Boredom,” he says, is a “fundamental requirement for one to settle down and start generating creative ideas.” This facilitates the inclination of mind to reflect and seek out the truths of life. He reflects on what he sees: “From the time you wake up till the moment you close your eyes, till the time you drop dead, people are continuously being on the phone, reacting to every notification. This keeps you engaged with the karmic identity of ‘you.’ By not doing so, you’re letting the hell you built around you to melt through mindfulness, contemplation and liberating wisdom.”

Through this discipline, a different kind of happiness is found. Not the dulling kind that comes from intoxication, but one arising from virtue, generosity and meditation. “There is an ease that comes from the nirāmisa,” he says—nirāmisa sukha, a happiness that does not depend on the senses. The Buddha spoke of such a happiness to the wanderer Māgandiya, who had accused him of being a “destroyer of growth” for teaching restraint of the senses. “There is, Māgandiya,” he replied, “a delight apart from sensual pleasures, apart from unwholesome states, which surpasses even divine bliss. Since I delight in that, I do not envy what is inferior, nor do I delight therein.” The Buddha named it as the happiness of jhāna: the rapture and ease born of seclusion, of a mind gathered and withdrawn from the pull of the senses. “Always, the world is there to attract you,” Ven. Ñāṇavimala would remind Shanaka.

To step away from that attraction is to enter the current the Buddha described: from rain falling on a mountaintop, the water fills the clefts and gullies, which fill the streams, which fill the rivers, until at last the great ocean is filled. Given the slope, the water simply flows. So it is that each state becomes the supporting condition for the next—from this happiness it flows into concentration, concentration into the seeing of things as they are, and this seeing into disenchantment, disenchantment onward into dispassion and then liberation.8

Shanaka found this nirāmisa sukha with the support of nature. It was born from the intuition he had carried since those solitary walks by the Caledon River, when the resolve first arose: I must stay in silent places like this. These silent places were the types of environments his teacher had practised in, and many great masters before him.

The movement of branches, the calls of birds, the empty caves—these engage the senses without provoking reactivity. Ancient masters understood: this is why the Suttas describe them practicing beneath trees and in forest groves. In the modern world, it offers a space where that cognitive noise can quieten.

Now the science points the same way. Studies comparing walks in nature versus busy urban settings found that nature reduced amygdala activity,9 lowered cognitive strain,10 and decreased blood flow to regions involved in rumination11—those looping thoughts focused on the self. Busy urban settings kept these patterns hyperactive; nature calmed them.

This rumination—the perpetual self-story, the endless loops of “me” that one wishes they'd stop thinking about, hinders present moment awareness and the clear seeing required for insight.
Together with reduced reactivity, nature provides an environmental support for calming the hindrances, especially restlessness and worry.

Through first noticing how loud the cognitive noise has become, one can then allow discipline, quietude and seclusion to turn it down. He finds that only then do the Suttas begin to sound fresh.

Just like the waves crashing on the ocean’s shore stir up the sand, so when those waves become still the sand settles. Then, through the clear undisturbed water, the sandy bottom is seen. So it is when the disturbances subside, what was always there can finally be seen. In that state, the mind could easily resonate with the ancient words, uncovering that intuitive spark.

Samāhito, bhikkhave, bhikkhu yathābhūtaṁ pajānāti—a bhikkhu who is concentrated sees things as they are.12

He would further reflect on the sacred support of places he practised in Sri Lanka, noting: “where enlightened beings practiced...we get that faith and the mind inclines towards that.” Yet he came to see that within its nature lay only a training support, not the path itself. Ven. Ñāṇavimala had once cautioned him against romanticizing this. Observing Shanaka’s idealization of the forest recluse, he remarked: “Even animals stay in the jungle.” The true seclusion the Buddha taught was internal. It is a quieting of the mind and a guarding of the senses that could be accessed anywhere. Ven. Ñāṇavimala used to tell him that even while you are in a crowded place you should be practicing; that then helps you when you are in a silent place. Following on from his teacher’s advice, he now reflects: “It’s not easy to be in solitude either. That is if you're not prepared before to some extent to handle solitude. Solitude also has an overwhelming effect on the mind. It can stir a lot of inner disturbances. But it can be a catalyst for spiritual wisdom if you have the proper foundation.” That foundation was never a particular environment. It was the inner development, in spite of whatever the outer conditions may be. He would later experience this clarity amid a crowd of grieving family members after his cousin went missing. Even there, the stillness of a guarded mind allowed the Dhamma to be his refuge.

He further came to see how the untrained mind constructs its own reality. One forms an idea, and whatever evidence one finds has already been shaped by it. “You have an idea, and that creates the facts,” he says. Truth, by contrast, is something that is revealed; it is not found by searching for evidence to confirm a view one already holds. Much of what passes for history and settled theory is formed in the opposite way—not by discovering the truth, but by beginning with an idea and then gathering supporting evidence around it. Once that idea takes hold, it quietly shapes what one notices, what one accepts, and even what one believes one sees. As he says, “Micchā diṭṭhi (wrong view) goes on,” each wrong view reinforcing the next.

Modern civilisation is built on constructed ideas. The ancient cultures that turned toward the inner life grew rich in consciousness. The modern world turned outward and grew rich in material things, and called it progress. But he came to measure advancement differently. “We are not evolved, actually,” he reflects. “Our evolution can be known by our ability to have more attention power. That leads to more samādhi. That leads to more intuition.” By this measure, the one faculty that marks a civilisation's inner depth is the most threatened. “Inwardly we don't know an inch about our being, our existence. We are taking only the shell, we are not taking the life behind the shell.” The law of impermanence shines a further light. “When you die, the things you take as gems, it’s all a perception,” he says.

This habit of borrowed ideas extends even into how the Dhamma is received. Ideas about Nibbāna are taken second-hand, from this teacher or that, held and repeated, while the meditative aspect—the direct seeing that alone gives them insight—remains absent. “When you have a lot of ideas, you never really go deep into the Dhamma. You’re always on a superficial theoretical level,” he says. People gather the words, holding them as views, rather than approaching the Dhamma as the Buddha instructed: ehipassiko—“come and see,” to truly investigate and see.

This was the subtler root of the distance between the Suttas and the modern seeker. Beneath the noise lies a mind already crowded with borrowed ideas and inherited certainties, encountering
spiritual ideas only to fit them into views it already holds. The attention needed to receive them has been worn thin. The Suttas ask to be met by another kind of seeing altogether.

That seeing does not manufacture a new idea. It investigates, it observes, and in that observing the truth discloses itself. He describes how it occurs: “washed completely from history, from the conditioning of the past, cognizing—like this you see the fresh truths.”

It was with this openness that he approached the Suttas—the openness Ven. Ñāṇavimala had guided him toward. This is how attention turns, in receiving the Suttas, into yoniso manasikāra—wise attention—to see things as they are.

From his own experience, he came to understand that the wisdom eye is covered by layers of conditioning, even by tensions held in the body, particularly behind the forehead, where tension accumulates like deposits covering those nerves. Reading the Suttas gradually removes those coverings, awakening the wisdom eye like a path being cleared in a tangled forest, revealing truths of how the enlightened beings saw the world. This is the context behind his words, “you have to relax as well as think”—simple words that hold more than they yet reveal.

Through his reading of the Suttas, he came to see the critical difference. While the words of modern commentators could be like a reflection of the moon, the Suttas were the moon itself. As he would later affirm, the Suttas “may look simple, but they point clearly to that insight.”

That moment Ven. Ñāṇavimala said “your teacher and my
teacher is the Dhamma—Vinaya,” had not been just a passing remark. It was his way of pointing Shanaka away from himself and back toward the source. Don’t cling to me, or to anyone. Go to the Dhamma. Trust the Buddha.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala had innovated a way to keep those words with him, his constant companion, even while walking thousands of miles on cārikā. The entire collection of the Buddha's discourses (Sutta Piṭaka) and the monastic code (Vinaya) he condensed into two small exercise books he could carry. Their pages were dense with German shorthand, compressed even further through his own system of abbreviations. This condensed what would normally require row upon row of heavy, hardbound volumes—an entire dedicated library shelf of scripture—into two portable booklets. He could locate any passage in moments.

A work of remarkable sophistication for a life kept radically simple.

What he'd also committed to memory was a feat in itself: the Pātimokkha13—all 227 monastic training rules—the entire Dhammapada14 with its 423 verses, as well as key Suttas. The rest lived in those two booklets, small enough to fit in his shoulder bag.

Toward the end of his life, this capacity for recall began to fade. The passages once instantly accessible and the verses held in memory for decades became harder to recall. Yet his awareness remained undimmed. His insight into the Dhamma and his presence had never depended on the ability to quote texts. What he had truly realized lived beyond memory.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala never claimed to have students. Yet many were drawn to him. They even came from schools of thought that did not always agree. Yet they all recognized what he had—a quality they lacked or a depth they aspired to.

Ven. Gnanaloka would time and time again notice two distinct types of devotees. The first were the dedicated practitioners of
specific techniques, seeing in Ven. Ñāṇavimala an ascetic meditator who had reached the depths. Ven. Gnanaloka noticed in them a notable stillness and poise when in meditation, the kind of bodily calm that comes with regular practice. Yet, they expressed the same doubts: that they felt adrift, unsure of where they stood on the path or where to go next. They were, as Ven. Gnanaloka points out, like travelers hiking without a map. The second group were the intellectuals: those steeped in the scriptures, seeing in Ven. Ñāṇavimala someone who understood what the words truly meant. They possessed vast theoretical knowledge but lacked basic experience and were paralyzed by doubt in another way. They held a map with exquisite detail but had never stepped onto the trail.

Observing the two, Ven. Gnanaloka playfully suggests that if these two groups were to “marry,” things would be “truly good.” He was pointing to the essential union of Suttas and practice which Ven. Ñāṇavimala embodied.

This unique capacity to bridge study and practice made him a source of inspiration for prominent lay teachers. During his time at Polgasduwa, Shanaka met individuals such as Godwin Samararatne, founder of the Nilambe Meditation Centre, and Brindley Ratwatte, the key figure in establishing Dhamma Kuta, the first Sri Lankan meditation centre in the Sayagyi U Ba Khin / Goenka tradition. Both had come seeking Ven. Ñāṇavimala for guidance. Years later, Brindley Ratwatte was ordained as Venerable Siddhartha. Ven. Ñāṇavimala asked Ven. Gnanaloka to support the newly ordained monk during his time at Polgasduwa.

Many monastics who met Ven. Ñāṇavimala as young monks would describe their encounters with a mix of admiration, but also intimidation. To some, he seemed fiercely unbending in his expectations, even narrow in his ways. However, there seemed to be a marked “softening in him” over the years as Ven. Bhikkhu Bodhi would write. Ven. Gnanaloka acknowledges that he got to know him in that later period, which was characterised by more gentleness.

Whether they were monastics or laypeople, Ven. Ñāṇavimala never allowed himself to be elevated or revered as a “guru” or spiri-tual figurehead. Though his presence was undeniably compelling, he refused to let personal devotion obstruct the path. Again and again, he directed others not to himself, but to the Buddha.

“If anyone is sincere, they will guide you to the Suttas.”

The lessons of the encounters were clear. Even the most gifted teachers may offer invaluable guidance and inspiration, but they can also falter, for charisma fades, institutions shift, and personalities fail. The only ultimate refuge, as Ven. Ñāṇavimala himself would say, is the timeless Dhamma, for “the Buddha never lets you down.”
