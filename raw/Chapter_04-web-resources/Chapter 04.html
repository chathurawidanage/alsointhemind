Chapter 04

More Pleasure More Danger
(Jan 1995 – Mar 1995)

28 January 1995

x To overcome these [sensual] thoughts, asubha saññā [perception of foulness]

How to spend the week:

x Read the verses in Pāli as if the Buddha is speaking to you, then the mind will be directed to that. Always the translations would not be adequate.7

x You must choose the Suttas that would help you to get on in the lay life. Read and practice loving kindness as [outlined] by Ñāṇamoli Thera [The Practice of Loving-Kindness], and in Vandana Vathpirith Potha [The Mirror of the Dhamma compiled by Ven. Nārada and Ven. Kassapa] there are 4 important things to be reflect on: mettā (loving-kindness), buddhānussati (recollection of the Buddha), asubha (foul nature of body), maraṇānussati (mindfulness of death).

x Concentrate on sīla and bhāvanā (vipassanā).

x Try to elevate the state of mind—that is the greatest service. Because what follows depends on it.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).

x Don’t study with the intention of accumulating knowledge.8

x You can earn a living even by sweeping.

x Learn to be content with little.

x Sensual things are the most dangerous.

x More Pleasure More Danger.

x Like moths who fly into the flames. So be careful.

x Know that the Saṅgha is in decline.

x Because when living near towns, going to pirivenas [monastic colleges that educate monks], there are more
sense impressions arising that arouse inner worldly inclinations, so they disrobe.

x So, one should retire to the forest, follow the instructions of the teacher.

x Satipaṭṭhāna should be practiced with Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi).

x Jhāna (meditative absorption) and then Satipaṭṭhāna (foundations of mindfulness).

x For jhāna to arise, there needs to be a foundation of sīla (virtue).

Regarding sensual desire:

x When elephants are in rut, or other animals, or lovers—they are fully absorbed and intoxicated by that; no sense is seen.

x People work just to earn money, to support their body. All the activities are centered on that. No time for the practice.

x When on retreat food and sleep or negligence are the hindrances one must watch, not too much effort either, but one should balance.

x First you should raise the mind or consciousness to higher deva worlds. Then after that you direct the mind to the Dhamma.9

x Saṅkhāra (Volitional Formations) brings about birth.

31 January 1995

x Yoniso-manasikāra (wise attention)—is these three ways of conduct.

x Satipaṭṭhāna should be practiced with the right understanding.

x The five faculties (indriya), saddhā, viriya, sati, samādhi (four jhānas) and paññā.10

x No hate, no greed should arise.

x If hate arises, you should control it with mettā (loving-kindness).

x When greed arises, practice asubha (contemplation of the foul nature of the human body), when delusion arises, read the Suttas.

x Refer to the Suttas Majjhima 117, 25, 50—Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty); Nivāpa Sutta (MN25; the bait); Māratajjanīya Sutta (MN50; the rebuke to Māra).

x It is good to be with the teacher in the jungle.

x It is very difficult to practice otherwise with unnecessary impressions.

x Also read Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).

x This life is full of suffering: old age, sickness, and the trouble of always searching for food. Even bhikkhus are not free from that; all others are working day and night only for this. [Also], the weakness of the body due to sickness. The food you put in is only for the purpose of supporting the body, [which is] constituted of the 32 parts of impurity. If you vomit it back, it is so repulsive. When it goes [past] the throat it is all foul, especially if you keep it for some days. When you reduce the craving or attraction to these things—though there [was] initial pleasure, [but] more suffering [is] connected to it. [By] cultivat[ing] the happiness in the absence of deluded pleasure, you turn away from the cause of this suffering (paṭicca-samuppāda). No illusion, no craving, no clinging, [no] birth, old age, [or] death. The causes are lobha (greed), dosa (hate), and moha (delusion).

x When you are doing caṅkamana (walking meditation) first you must arouse a sense of urgency.

x Thinking of this endless round of rebirth. Each step towards death. Then you should exclude all other thoughts and must stick to the present.

x With thoughts of mettā, supporting each other, harmony in mind to achieve this one goal, deliverance from all suffering, Nibbāna.

x When practicing mettā-bhāvanā, (loving kindness meditation), not just wishing long life and happiness, but wishing the highest happiness to end this suffering [for all beings].

x You must stay with the teacher in the jungle for the practice to establish firmly, so that you can face any situation in time to come.

x When you give into food and other things, craving increases and clinging, birth, old age and death.

x Mettā-bhāvanā and the impermanence of feelings, perceptions and consciousness.

x Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates).

x Read the Dhammapada verses, recite them, and learn by heart in Pāli certain verses that strike you.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā)

x Mustn’t have inner conflict. Your father would like you to have a sound education. You can support each other.

x You must stick to a few Suttas and put them into practice.

3 February 1995

Morning

x Majjhima Sutta 125—Dantabhūmi Sutta (MN125; the grade of the tamed), Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates).

x You must purify yourself. In other words, you become selfish—[that is,] retire to solitude to purify oneself in order to overcome the ‘I’ conceit and thereafter serve in a selfless way.

x A teacher generally has an idea of all the Suttas. So, he prescribes to the pupil, according to where the pupil is and where he has to get [to] and it is a step-by-step process.

x If the pupil is a layman and wants to meditate then he should start with Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā), the three ways of conduct.

x If he is just a layman, Sigālaka Sutta (DN31; to Sigālaka), etc.

x You must always start with the three protections11 and with arousing of spiritual urgency, when beginning on caṅkamana (walking meditation) or any kind of bhāvanā (meditation) or spiritual endeavor.

x You must always try to curb and annihilate lobha (greed), dosa (hate) and moha (delusion) by a suitable kammaṭṭhāna (object of meditation).

x One cannot rely on anything that is from outside that is happiness—only the inner peace.

x Caṅkamana (walking meditation) is sticking to the present without giving into thoughts from outside, this should be done with understanding and reflection of the situation of the body, how all activity is centered around supporting the impure body that is connected with all kinds of suffering—old age, sickness, and all kinds of illusive happiness.

x Certain things are practiced for the benefit of future generations e.g. certain Vinaya rules, Venerable Mahākassapa was staying in solitude.

x The three ways of conduct [tīṇi sucaritāni] done unremittingly and immaculately.

x May you get all the protection in saṁsāra.

Afternoon

x Majjhima Sutta 53—Sekha Sutta (MN53; the disciple in higher training).

x It is for the monks, when you become a monk, it is useful, especially regarding the [spiritual] faculties [Indriyas].

x Satipaṭṭhāna leads to jhānas.

x Do this caṅkamana practice when you feel sleepy and control this hindrance.

x All my blessings are there for your practice.

x Always look at Khandhasaṁyutta and Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta. Look at it as it is.

x Always stick to the present, don’t expect anything from the future nor regret or go back to the past. What you do in the present makes the future.

x When you go on caṅkamana (walking meditation), think of this body: made up of defiled actions in the past, so impure and repulsive.12

x Read the Dhammapada verses. Memorise some.

4 February 1995

x When you are in retreat solitude try to control the 5 indriyas [five spiritual faculties] and practice caṅkamana (sati-sampajañña).

x Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates).

x Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases)

x You may speak about the Dhamma when asked.

x Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā). In the evening mettā bhāvanā and anicca.13 When caṅkamana, “sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā” [all conditioned things are impermanent], verses in Dhammapada (Dhp277—279).

x Sacittapariyodapanaṁ, etaṁ buddhāna sāsanaṁ [To purify one’s own mind—this is the teaching of the Buddhas] (Dhp183).

x You may give support to each other, the family, not only this material support, but also this spiritual support.

x See everything as impermanent, and suffering; and not yours.

x Atta Vagga (last verse) in the Dhammapada (Dhp 166) and Bubble & Mirage (Dhp 170).

16 February 1995

x Bahussuta [knowledgeable/learned] is important.14

x After practicing bhāvanā like in Rishikesh [India]. More ability to concentrate. Therefore, successful in worldly activity. But what you build up in the sensual world also
comes down very fast.15 “sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā” [all conditioned things are impermanent].

x As a layman you observe verbal and physical actions. As a monk higher sīla is first restrained by the Pātimokkha [monastic discipline] and indriya saṁvara sīla [sense restraint] and then thoughts, ariya sīla (noble virtue).

x You should always reflect on the impurities of the body, and the impermanence of food. Especially [reflect] if you vomit [it] out, or [if you have] some food still in your stomach undigested, and so on. And also, when you are taking food, [reflect on] the trouble involved in meeting lay people and just to support this body, with people working day and night for a body which is rushing to old age, sickness and death. But when you know that, one is able to remove the cause of suffering or the arising of this body—which is craving, by clear seeing or understanding, so that ultimately the craving will diminish and extinguish. The arising of new suffering will extinguish.

x One should retire with the teacher into the forest for jhāna to take place. Such an environment with less sense impressions to work with is necessary.

x During the day you should work in a sīlavanta [virtuous] way because this affects the sleep and meditation at the end of the day.

x Writing letters and so on by monks—it is not that you should forget your relatives.16

x Even the Brahmā world is suffering. One may re-arise even in the hell realms.

x Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty).

x Sleep—you should try to sleep generally 5 to 6 hours. It is good to reduce it. You should allocate a specific time of the day for this. And at other times you must not allow sleepiness.

x You must share the Dhamma with your relatives, not by going behind them and talking, but by your own actions.

x This solid knowledge should arise. That is insight into Dhamma.

x Today more money is needed and people’s needs are more.

x When you are in retreat, you should have Dhamma thoughts.

x You mustn’t eat very little food or sleep too little; you may fall sick. But you should try to do it with little effort. You have to maintain this body and keep it in good shape.

x Jhāna; then vipassanā.17

x When you have wishes, and when they are fulfilled, the craving increases more, wish after wish will follow.

x Do not wait with certain ideas for a long time, even arahants discuss from time to time.18

2 March 1995

x Supply the causes for the things that you wish to arise.

x Make sure the foundation is firmly established.

x Read Majjhima 125, 117, 107, 118—Dantabhūmi Sutta (MN125; the grade of the tamed), Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty), Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna), Ānāpānasati Sutta (MN118; mindfulness of breathing).

x Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases), Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates), Bojjhaṅgasaṁyutta (SN46; connected discourses on the factors of enlightenment)19 all in Saṁyutta Nikāya.

x Read Word of the Buddha [by Venerable Nyanatiloka Mahāthera] for sammā diṭṭhi.

x When you are here now, just don’t think to abide [in] jhāna, but try to overcome the five hindrances, then the mind will settle easily.

x [Then] you should concentrate to attain jhāna.

x You must do things slowly and thoroughly.

x In time to come when practicing mettā bhāvanā, pleasant feelings may arise. Look at it with equanimity, they are just feelings. All feelings are impermanent, suffering, not mine, and conditionally arisen.

x Equanimity is most important—not emotional feeling.20

x Don’t worry, it will come.

x Regarding bojjhaṅga [factors of enlightenment]—this is towards true enlightenment. Not the way of the [ātman-believing] yogi, [which is] micchā diṭṭhi [wrong view] way.21

x Bojjhaṅga takes place when you are doing bhāvanā.

x Don’t try to have theoretical explanations, but instead experience.

x Don’t stick to an attainment, go on.

x You must change the kammaṭṭhāna (object of meditation) in order to tackle each defilement.

x When going on caṅkamana, control the thoughts, so that it sticks to one place. At that time lobha (greed), dosa (hate) and moha (delusion) are reduced [the] sense of ‘I’ is going away, [leading] towards Nibbāna.

x Sometimes one is enthusiastic, sometimes one’s strength is going down. This will be the situation throughout life. So don’t worry—as long as the conditions are good, you may work as hard as possible, but not overdoing. Always the middle way.

6 March 1995

x Don’t rely on anybody or any person or anything that is external. They can separate [from you] at any moment and are all unreliable. But depend on your own mind that is developed and the All Enlightened One as your guide.

x Observing the physical and verbal actions you do as a layman is known as sīla; [as] a monk, you go further to observe thoughts, and to bring them into control (mental actions), that is Ariya sīla.

x The physical and verbal actions also derive from the mental state (first verse in Dhammapada—Dhp 1).

x First look after your own welfare; it is not selfish. Bringing yourself to selflessness, then you can serve others in the highest way without selfish motives.

x First Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) [theoretical foundation],22 then the five hindrances23 should be checked and overcome; then Satipaṭṭhāna.

x Suttas 117, 125, 106 [of Majjhima Nikāya]—Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty); Dantabhūmi Sutta (MN125; the grade of the tamed); Āneñjasappāya Sutta (MN106; the way to the imperturbable).

x Reading all the Suttas, memorising them, there is no point.Practice and experience. This is important.

x When we see the 32 parts of the body, and [by] not letting any worldly thought arise, one will know that one is
directing towards overcoming suffering or Nibbāna.

8 March 1995

x All my blessings are there for all your endeavors.

9 March 1995

x You must be firmly established in the Dhamma as a bhikkhu when you go out, where you will face all sorts of situations. Must think of the Sutta [relating to] the Simile of the Saw—Kakacūpama Sutta (MN21; the simile of the saw) and reflect that our kamma is behind us.

x First sīla (virtue) and then jhāna (meditative absorption) and then entering the path—sotāpanna (Stream-Enterer), sakadāgāmī (Once-Returner), anāgāmī (Non-Returner), arahant (Fully Enlightened One).

x Until arahantship (full enlightenment) there is this “I” conceit in a very subtle way.

x All lay people are like children, they need a father figure. But when the children grow up, they do not need one anymore. So don’t think of a particular person outside. Rely always on the All Enlightened One and the Dhamma that you have already mastered as your guide. Whenever effort is made and certain bliss is connected to that, take that as a strength to go on and develop.

x Do not teach the Dhamma for any worldly gain and so on.

x Go on with compassion for beings with less understanding.

x First give support to people who are close to you. But don’t try to convert or go behind [them], but [do so] with your own example—and if they are interested, you may help them accordingly. You may send to your friend in Cyprus the Kesamutti Sutta (AN3.65; Kesaputtiya). If [they] are more interested, a list of BPS [Buddhist Publication Society] books may be provided, and maybe point to particular books. Not [to] suggest this is Buddhism.

x Don’t have any ties. Try to reduce ties as much as possible.

x You must understand this situation. We have not always acted in a kusala (wholesome) way in the past. Never [be] displeased.24

x When unwholesome thoughts arise, don’t react with aversion or hate or fear, if reacting that way, it means one is directing the mind in that direction, therefore it increases.

x Don’t stick to an attainment or turn back from [putting] effort.

x Suttas to Anamataggasaṁyutta (SN15; connected discourses on without discoverable beginning). Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty); Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta (MN20; the removal of distracting thoughts) [to] deal with thoughts.

x Doctors are very busy, [with] more diseases, so more activity to cope up with, and they are generally highly considered by the society, for everybody is keen about good health.


But in the Dhamma it is all mentioned: the root cause for sickness—that is immorality.25

x It is difficult to maintain jhāna, so you should do the second best by reading the Suttas etc.

x Live in the present, every pleasant present moment will bring happiness in the future, so never let the mind be displeased.

x Look at everything as a bubble and mirage (Dhp 170). There is no meaning in all this phenomenon.

x There is no ‘I’, there is no ‘me’.

x Daily, make sure all activities are done in such a way [that] there hasn’t been any harm done to anybody. Go to bed early. Before sleeping practice mettā bhāvanā. Do the activity with full concentration, slowly. And on poya days, you may retire in a suitable place and wait. All depends on your practice.

x All external support may come to an end finally. It is our kamma behind us, we have not always acted in a wholesome way.

x All [your] relatives and family have a lot of saddhā due to past lives.

x You may give support to your family, relatives, and all others, not by trying to convert them, but by your example. It is the best way.

x You practice in solitude and go [out] to the world, in order to fully establish in the practice of Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā) in the lay life, so that a sound basis and a foundation is established.

x Based on the way we look from inside; the world is formed from the outside.

x Anyway, we cannot change things outside.

x When going on caṅkamana (walking meditation), see the suffering we have to go through to maintain this meani-
ngless process: coming in contact with disturbing sense impressions and the food we [consume]. [And see the] danger in it—for it [strengthens] the cause for this process that is rushing toward old age, sickness, [and] death—this craving. Yet, one should be happy that we know the root cause of this suffering: it is this craving.

x Building houses and so forth for the body etc.

  1. 7 To Ven. Ñāṇavimala there was no second best to knowing Pāli; that was the best way to understand the Buddha. Regardless of how good they are, translators’ views invariably get projected into their translations, distorting meaning to some degree. He encouraged Shanaka to learn Pāli. However, he directed Shanaka to the Suttas through English translations and Shanaka/Ven. Gnanaloka would rely on them as his primary source of meaning.

  2. 8 Refers to acquiring knowledge of spiritual literature just for knowledge’s sake.

  3. 9 Ven. Gnanaloka elaborates: With sīla and saddhā (faith) and cāga (generosity), you stay with good activities—you’re elevated to a deva state of existence. Then in such a state of mind you practise the Dhamma.

  4. 10 The five spiritual faculties (pañcindriyāni) are inner strengths that sustain progress on the path: saddhā (faith or confidence), viriya (energy or effort), sati (mindfulness), samādhi (concentration, including the four jhānas), and paññā (wisdom or insight into the true nature of phenomena)—Paṭhamavibhaṅga Sutta (SN48.9; analysis, first).

  5. 11 The Three Protections refer to the recollection of refuge in the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha.

  6. 12 Ven. Gnanaloka explains that the body's impure nature arises from past defiled thoughts—due to greed, hate and delusion. It is a result of kamma, that this body has taken the form it has. “Why is it repulsive?” he asks. “Because it’s created by repulsive thoughts. He (Ven. Ñāṇavimala) means that you have to picture the reality of how gross it is.” The reflection is meant for clear seeing: one cannot observe what one is attached to. By reflecting upon the body’s gross nature, a necessary distance for insight is created—“because you cannot observe something that you are attached to.” When thoughts are more refined, the resulting kamma is also more refined, giving rise to subtler bodies of existence. Yet, all remain bound by the same law of arising and passing away.

  7. 13 Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies: the teachings in the Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (about bodily, verbal and mental actions)—this should be done during the whole day. In the evening do loving kindness meditation. Reflect upon impermanence as understood through the Suttas—applying it to the five aggregates of clinging. Reflect on impermanence when doing walking meditation as well.

  8. 14 Bahussuta (Pāli) literally means “much-heard”—i.e., well-read or extensively acquainted with the Suttas. When Ven. Ñāṇavimala says “Bahussuta is important,” he is emphasizing that close and repeated reading of the Buddha’s discourses strengthens right view, supplies the practitioner with suitable objects for reflection, and supports practice.

  9. 15 Ven. Gnanaloka clarified that this was a reference to a trend, known at the time, of business leaders using yogic meditative concentration, developed in places like Rishikesh, for the purpose of worldly manifestation. Ven. Ñāṇavimala, who had direct experience with this concentration power from his time as a corporate director in his lay life, was adding the crucial teaching that all such achievements are ultimately impermanent and can “come down very fast.”

  10. 16 Ven. Gnanaloka recalls an occasion when a visitor from Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s own hometown in Germany arrived at Polgasduwa. Although they were strangers, they shared the same roots—yet his teacher showed no special emotion or reaction. Ven. Gnanaloka notes this unwavering restraint as an example of relinquished bonds—reducing ties with all people and places, even those of profound personal significance.

  11. 17 Ven. Gnanaloka recalls this was the summary in essence of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s talk at the time: Jhāna helps Vipassanā. Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies that it doesn't mean that it is necessary to get jhāna and then insight. There are Suttas where they get insight without mention of jhāna. He continues to explain, in those cases, even if jhāna wasn't mentioned, the mind was still in a “jhāna-like” state—meaning the five hindrances were absent—otherwise the truth could not penetrate. An example is the Susimaparibbājaka Sutta (SN12.70; Susīma), which describes attainment of liberation through wisdom (paññā-vimutta) without possessing formless meditative attainments. The Buddha’s explanation to Susīma confirms that Dhamma-ṭṭhiti-ñāṇa (knowledge of the stability of the Dhamma) is the definitive requirement for Nibbāna. Generally, however, it is good to have sitting meditation and then reading.

  12. 18 Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies, by giving the example that someone may even think they have become enlightened—don’t assume because it may also be wrong. He recommended that it is better to have discussions, which may give different perspectives on what you are experiencing.

  13. 19 Bojjhaṅgasaṁyutta (SN46; connected discourses on the factors of enlightenment): A section of the Connected Discourses where the Buddha teaches the Seven Factors of Awakening—mindfulness, investigation (clear seeing), energy, joy, calm, concentration, and equanimity.

  14. 20 Ven. Gnanaloka explains how Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught that equanimity is the highest conscious experience. He would say based on that you have the formless states.

  15. 21 Here, “the way of the yogi” refers broadly to any contemplative practice—whether inside or outside historical Buddhist circles—that utilizes exalted states of consciousness or absorptions to unite with, or abide in a permanent ‘self’ or soul.While these practices can lead to profoundly peaceful, exalted states of consciousness, they are considered micchā diṭṭhi (wrong view) from the standpoint of ultimate awakening because they fail to see that all phenomena are anatta (void of a permanent identity). The bojjhaṅgas (factors of enlightenment) are unique because they do not build up a meditator's identity; instead, they dismantle the subtle tendency to cling to any state as “myself.”

  16. 22 The original writes ‘(theoretical grasping),’ however, the meaning is to get a theoretical foundation.

  17. 23 The five hindrances are mental obstacles that cloud understanding and obstruct concentration: kāmacchanda (sensual desire), vyāpāda (ill will), thīna–middha (sloth and torpor), uddhacca–kukkucca (restlessness and worry), and vicikicchā (doubt).

  18. 24 Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies, the idea is not to dwell or brood over troublesome life situations; know that they are the consequences of past kamma and those
    consequences will also pass. Rather one should take care not to create new unwholesome     kamma.

  19. 25 Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies: as people’s virtue (sīla) declines, sickness increases in the world. This relates to the principle that immoral conduct (akusala kamma) serves as a root cause for disease. The canonical connection between the decline of morality and the increase in sickness and shortening of lifespan is detailed in the Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta (DN26; the lion’s roar on the turning of the wheel).
