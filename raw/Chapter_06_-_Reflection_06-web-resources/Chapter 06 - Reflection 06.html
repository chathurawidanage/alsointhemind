Reflection 06

Living as an Island Unto Oneself

Living in close association with Ven. Ñāṇavimala often brought to mind the Buddha’s final instructions to the Saṅgha, as recorded in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days). Speaking to Venerable Ānanda shortly before his passing, the Buddha said:

“Tasmātihānanda attadīpā viharatha attasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā, dhammadīpā dhammasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā.”

“Therefore, Ānanda, be islands unto yourselves, be your own refuge, having no other; let the Dhamma be your island, let the Dhamma be your refuge, having no other refuge.”

This single instruction seemed to encapsulate the way Ven. Ñāṇavimala lived. Beholden to no institution, title, reputation or following, his refuge lay not in monasteries or teacherhood, but solely in the Dhamma.

One day, Shanaka saw him slowly making his way back to his kuṭi. At that time, Ven. Ñāṇavimala was already well into his eighties. Decades of walking barefoot across Sri Lanka had worn his body thin. A hip injury sustained in his youth had worsened with age. He leaned heavily on his cane, each movement deliberate.

Out of concern, Shanaka approached and asked, “Bhante, can I help you?”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala turned, his eyes calm and bright, and replied,

“This body is not mine, why should I give it to somebody else?”

That response left Shanaka speechless. In just a few words, Ven. Ñāṇavimala revealed the source of his unshakeable composure: when the body is no longer taken as ‘self,’ its inevitable decay is no longer a personal tragedy.

Time and time again, Shanaka would get a window into just how deeply Ven. Ñāṇavimala had found this to be true.

He once advised Shanaka: “Don't rely on anybody or any person or anything that is external. They can separate at any moment and are all unreliable. But depend on your own mind that is developed and the All Enlightened One as your guide.”

It was the kind of teaching that could only come from someone who had tested it against hunger, against danger, against the collapse of every external support—and it had held.

Years earlier, during his cārikā, Ven. Ñāṇavimala had been confronted by robbers seeking valuables. They demanded he strip to just his under-robe while they rummaged through his belongings. He stepped away and hid. When he returned, he found his possessions scattered across the ground, but nothing had been taken.

Even these dirt-poor thieves found nothing worth stealing. His books were completely written in, margins filled with notes. His robes were weathered. Every item bore the marks of years of use and utter simplicity. There was nothing of value to take, not even to those who had nothing.

On another occasion, at a train station, after rising from his night’s rest, he saw that his bag had been rummaged through, but there had been nothing of value to take. The booklets inside his bag were scattered outside. It included one on maraṇānussati (death recollection)1 which looked like it had been opened. He commented to Shanaka how those poor people who had tried to rob him may have gone through this and even had pity on him.

During the Sinhalese New Year—a time of festivities, family gatherings, and the sharing of rich foods and delicacies—all the lay attendants would return home to be with their families. Shanaka found himself conflicted about whether he should join his grandmother and family for the celebrations, or remain at Polgasduwa and continue his practice.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala noticed his inner conflict. He allowed Shanaka to come to his own decision, but just playfully nudged him: “There are good sweet things here also. You can have that here.”

Later, Ven. Ñāṇavimala would explain to him: “It is not that you should forget your relatives. It is always good to reduce bonds and ties with all people, even in a lay life.” He was unentangled from personal bonds just as he was from material possessions. He taught that the transference of merit through living purely held more of a positive power of influence on family and relatives than just being there physically. For the remainder of his life, Ven. Ñāṇavimala never left the island of Sri Lanka, nor did he express any desire to. He didn’t keep in contact with anyone from his home country.

On another occasion, Shanaka expressed to Ven. Ñāṇavimala that he wanted to leave the island hermitage to donate blood. Shanaka had a strong commitment to social good and was moved by that calling. Ven. Ñāṇavimala supported his wish. Shanaka went to Colombo in the morning and came back to Polgasduwa later in the afternoon. Ven. Ñāṇavimala then explained to him how those meritorious deeds are good, but greater is the merit from cultivation of inner development. To trade that inner work for them, he said, is like sacrificing millions to earn a few dollars. Looking back, he reflects, being with Ven. Ñāṇavimala was like being drawn into an experience that was beyond all things related to the connections of the sensual world.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala never claimed disciples and never sought to build a following. As Ven. Gnanaloka recalls with amusement, his teacher would often say, “I’m not your teacher”—and then proceed to instruct him.

Although he never took on the title of Ācariya (teacher), from the moment Shanaka met him to the time of his passing, Ven. Ñāṇavimala offered steady guidance. Sometimes he pointed Shanaka to the Suttas; sometimes, he gave direct advice, often understood fully only years or even decades later. But more than anything, it was his presence—his silence, his unwavering commitment to the path—that engraved the young aspirant’s heart.

For nearly three years after they first met, Shanaka hesitated. He had a desire to enter monastic life, but he remained on the fence about it with all kinds of doubts playing on his mind. First, could he live as a celibate for the rest of his life?

Ven. Ñāṇavimala gently encouraged him, never forcefully. He would sometimes remark, “How great it would be if you were to ordain.” On another occasion he even went as far as to suggest: “For one year you can ordain, then you can disrobe again.”

One day, hearing his encouragement again, Shanaka responded with frustration: “The temples are deteriorating, the forest monasteries are deteriorating, the monks are deteriorating…”

Without missing a beat, Ven. Ñāṇavimala replied: “Your consciousness is deteriorating.”

Only silence followed.

Shanaka knew he was right. Yet confidence did not come immediately.

Later, at the age of twenty-eight, Shanaka was staying at a cave kuṭi in Athdalagala forest monastery. He was meditating—reading the Suttas and reflecting quietly in solitude. From the rocky outcrop above, Rasvehera—an ancient forest monastery where it was said that hundreds of enlightened beings had once dwelt in caves—was visible.

By this time, the desire to ordain he once had when he was eighteen had largely faded. He saw a beacon of light in his teacher and the subtle pressure from him growing during these years. But he also saw the laxity within the monastic institutions and feared its influence. To him, the risk was too great; he worried that in becoming a monk, he might merely become another symptom of the deterioration he observed. He considered the idea that it was safer to remain an upāsaka (lay person who practices).

One afternoon during the retreat, however, he was reading the Vipassī Sutta.2 After reading, he practised walking meditation. As he did this, a recollection of a small stanza from the Sutta struck him: Jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ—with birth as a condition ageing and death come to be. At once this knowing came to him. He saw his body just disintegrate. This insight was not one of fireworks or overwhelming bliss, but a kind of shock, like finding that the rope that one had been holding in both hands had been a snake all along. What followed was an immense peace of letting go. He realised he had been in such an internal space of silence and mindfulness during this time, that only when later coming out of it did his attention go to the sounds outside of the surroundings in the monastery, and the subsequent sense impressions that followed.

The following day, he stood upon the rocky outcrop looking out in the direction of Rasvehera. Whether it was the knowledge that the Dhātunvahanse—the sacred relics of the ancient saints—were enshrined there, or something beyond explanation, distinct rays began to emanate from its direction. Then, as if carried by the sky itself, an internal voice echoed:

“Klesa Nasana Bhikkhu. Klesa Nasana Bhikkhu.” (Bhikkhu who destroys defilements, Bhikkhu who destroys defilements.)

It gave him an inspired feeling that the energy of the great saints was not a relic of history, but a presence still accessible in the here and now, and that the golden period of the Buddha’s dispensation is still alive. The question finally rose to the surface: Why am I still staying like this? The call to the only work that mattered had arrived—the relentless ceasing of the fires within. The fence he had sat upon for three years was gone. It was time.

Acting swiftly on the forest abbot's advice, he boarded a bus to Colombo the following morning. Beneath his resolve, a part of him feared that Ven. Ñāṇavimala might pass away before he could take his vows.

He returned home and informed his father that he wanted to ordain. This time his father embraced him and said:

“Son, don't look back.”

He then felt a “funny feeling,” not the liberation one might expect. Instead, it felt like a slow death. The weight of such a big renunciation dawned on him. Craving had built a world outside, and now it was being asked to die. This death felt like a lifeless void. Yet somewhere within, he understood, this dying was necessary.

He was going to the ordination ceremony as a layman, in jeans and a T-shirt, with a long beard. Before that he was to meet Ven. Ñāṇavimala, who, bedridden at the time, would be unable to attend.

As he stood before his teacher and announced his decision, Ven. Ñāṇavimala gazed at him for some moments, as if pondering. Then, his words were not about the Dhamma, but something much more unexpected—a remark about appearances: “Don’t know how you would look without your beard.” It was as if the unbending ascetic had receded, leaving only the recognition of a spiritual father for his son.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala had tactfully nudged him on the path, and this tactfulness was observed in many aspects of his life. Ven. Gnanaloka recalls one of these aspects was the way he moved between two worlds. There was the ascetic with a depth of practice beyond this world. And there was the monk who played his part within the community, mingling with other monastics when the situation called for it and engaging in simple conversation. Ven. Gnanaloka recollects seeing how his teacher went to lengths to keep the full depth of his practice inconspicuous. It was his way of blending in without drawing attention to the intensity beneath.

A quarter century has passed, yet the gravity of his teacher's example has never shifted. Though devotees come seeking Ven. Gnanaloka's guidance, looking back, he comments: “I am but a tiny speck of dust compared to my teacher.”

In quiet moments beside his teacher, he had tried to express his gratitude through service or offerings. But he came to understand: true repayment comes only by walking the path Ven. Ñāṇavimala had lived.
