In Reverence to Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera

23 November 1911 – 10 October 2005

Epilogue: The Sage

At the time of the Buddha, many who listened to the Dhamma were moved to renounce worldly life and follow the ancient path of the sages.

In Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera, the spirit of the ancient path came alive again—a life of quiet renunciation, lived beneath the open sky. His footprints joined those of countless seekers, from the forests of India to the hermitages of Sri Lanka and beyond. Wherever such lives are lived, the way of the sage endures—serene, selfless and unbroken through time.

The verses below, spoken by the Buddha, encapsulate the essence of that path—the life of the muni, the silent sage. They remind us that the Dhamma is not a doctrine to be held, but a way to be lived—here and now, in this very life.

May these words awaken in the reader the same resolve that inspired countless seekers—to live by the Dhamma, to see clearly, and to walk the way of the sage.

“Peril stems from intimacy, dust comes from an abode.
Freedom from abode and intimacy: that is the sage’s vision.

Having cut down what’s grown, they wouldn’t replant,
nor would they nurture what’s growing.
That’s who they call a sage wandering alone,
the great seer has seen the state of peace.

Having appraised the fields and measured the seeds,
they wouldn’t nurture them with moisture.
Truly that sage who sees the ending of rebirth
has left speculation behind and is beyond reckoning.

Understanding all the planes of rebirth,
not wanting a single one of them;
truly that sage freed of greed
need not swim, for they have reached the far shore.

The champion, all-knower, so very intelligent,
unsullied in the midst of all things,
has given up all, freed in the ending of craving:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

Strong in wisdom, with precepts and observances intact,
serene, loving absorption, mindful,
released from chains, kind, undefiled:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

The diligent sage wandering alone,
is unshaken by blame and praise—
like a lion not startled by sounds,
like wind not caught in a net,
like water not sticking to a lotus.
Leader of others, not by others led:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

Steady as a post in a bathing-place
when others speak endlessly against them,
freed of greed, with senses stilled:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

Steadfast, straight as a shuttle,
horrified by wicked deeds,
discerning the just and the unjust:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

Restrained, they do no evil,
young or middle-aged, the sage is self-controlled.
Irreproachable, he does not insult anyone:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

When one who lives on charity receives alms,
from the top, the middle, or the leftovers,
they think it unworthy to praise or put down:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

The sage lives refraining from sex,
even when young is not tied down,
refraining from indulgence and negligence, freed:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

Understanding the world,
the seer of the ultimate goal,
the unaffected one,
having crossed the flood and the ocean,
has cut the ties, unattached and undefiled:
that’s who the attentive know as a sage.

The two are not the same,
far apart in lifestyle and conduct—
the householder providing for a wife,
and the selfless one true to their vows.
The unrestrained householder kills other creatures,
while the restrained sage
always protects living creatures.

As the crested blue-necked peacock
flying through the sky
never approaches the speed of the swan,
so the householder cannot compete with the mendicant,
the sage meditating secluded in the woods.”

Muni Sutta (Snp 1.12; the muni)

Appendix

Unlocking the Timeless Insights Preserved in the Suttas

Although the Buddha attained Parinibbāna over 2,500 years ago, something of him is still alive wherever a Sutta is read with sincerity. The Suttas are the closest thing we have to the Buddha’s own voice. Everything else—commentary, modern interpretation, secondhand explanation—is someone else’s reflection of the teaching. The Suttas are the teaching itself, carried forward with extraordinary care by generations of the Saṅgha, who memorized, recited, and faithfully preserved these discourses so that what reaches us today remains remarkably faithful to what was originally spoken.

That is what makes these words unlike any ordinary book. They are not merely teachings about the path; they are the very words that have guided countless practitioners to stream-entry,
once-returning, non-returning, and ultimately arahantship—complete liberation. Every discourse is an opportunity to encounter the Dhamma exactly as it was first taught: not a summary of it, not an interpretation of it, but the living voice itself, still speaking across the centuries to anyone willing to listen with an open heart.

In that sense, reading a Sutta is a small kind of time travel. Sit with one slowly enough, and it can feel as though you are being carried back more than 2,500 years, to a quiet forest grove, a monastery, or a village park, sitting among monks, nuns, and lay followers gathered around the Blessed One as these very words are spoken for the first time. As you read, the centuries seem to fall away, and for a moment the distance between you and the Buddha feels surprisingly small, as though the Buddha himself is speaking directly to you. The possibility of transformation that existed then is still present now.

These discourses were never meant simply to be read, analysed, or stored away as knowledge. They are meant to be contemplated, lived, and realized. They deserve to be approached the way one
would approach something genuinely precious, with reverence, patience, and humility. In the Buddha’s day, people travelled great distances on foot, endured hardship, gave up comforts, and even went forth from the household life into homelessness for the opportunity to hear the Dhamma. In the modern age, those same precious discourses are often no more than a Google search away, carried in our pockets wherever we go. To treat them merely as information would be to forget the immense value they once held for those who sacrificed so much to hear them, preserve them, and pass them on to us. Such accessibility is a blessing that should never be taken for granted.

The Suttas are also like a treasure map. Hidden within each discourse are directions pointing toward the greatest treasure there is: the complete ending of suffering. For a mind fully ripe, a single discourse can act as a complete key, unlocking total liberation in an instant. But for most of us, the map unfolds piece by piece. Each Sutta reveals another landmark, another layer of training, and another profound insight waiting to be discovered.

At the same time, every heart is locked a little differently. Think of each Sutta as a key, and your own mind as a lock you do not yet fully understand. Apart from the Buddha, no one can tell you beforehand which key will fit. The only way to find out is to read sincerely, one Sutta after another, without forcing the search. Then, often unexpectedly, a particular discourse will resonate so deeply that it quietly transforms the way you see yourself, the world, and the Dhamma. When you find that key, the real work begins: returning to that teaching again and again, contemplating it deeply, and allowing it to shape the way you live.

As Venerable Ñāṇavimala said:

“If anyone is sincere, they will guide you to the Suttas.”

Teachers, traditions, and interpretations may vary with time, but the Suttas remain the one stable and trustworthy reference point. Within them lie both the map and the key. Read them slowly. Read them with faith, with curiosity, and with sincerity. For when you open a Sutta with the right heart, you are not merely reading an ancient text; you are encountering the world through the lens of the Buddha himself. As the Blessed One said, “Yo dhammaṁ passati so maṁ passati; yo maṁ passati so dhammaṁ passati”—“One who sees the Dhamma sees me; one who sees me sees the Dhamma.” For within the Suttas, it is the Dhamma that shines forth, and through the Dhamma, the Buddha is truly seen.

For immeasurable ages you have wandered through this beginningless saṁsāra, roaming and wandering on, hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving. Lifetime after lifetime, you have pursued fleeting happiness while remaining bound to birth, aging, and death. Yet now, in this rare human life, you have encountered the words of a Fully Awakened Buddha, carefully preserved in the Suttas for more than 2,500 years. Do not underestimate how precious this is. Do not take this opportunity lightly. After countless lifetimes, you may be far closer to liberation than you have ever been before.

Venerable Ñāṇavimala’s Walking Contemplations
(Caṅkama Bhāvanā)

On numerous occasions, Venerable Gnanaloka (then Shanaka) accompanied his teacher from his hut to the monastery dining hall and back. During these walks, Venerable Ñāṇavimala would often verbalize the sequence of his caṅkama (walking meditation) contemplations.
The following phrases, memorized by Venerable Gnanaloka, offer a
rare glimpse into the structured, moment-to-moment reflection of a master.

The practice unfolds in a progressive arc, moving from contemplating the drawbacks of sensuality (Kāma ādīnava) and the benefits of renunciation (Nekkhamma ānisaṁsa), culminating in the direct contemplation of the path to liberation (Nissaraṇa) through Dependent Origination.

1. Brightening the Mind

The practice begins by cultivating faith and confidence in the Buddha, reflecting on his boundless qualities, reciting a Pāli Gāthā or recalling the great practitioners of the past who walked this very path to liberation.

2. Grounding in the Present Moment

After brightening the mind with faith, one returns to the present by reflecting on the impermanent nature of all phenomena.

“Yadatītaṁ pahīnaṁ taṁ, appattañca anāgataṁ.
Paccuppannañca yo dhammaṁ, tattha tattha vipassati”

“The past is left behind, The future is not yet reached.
But those phenomena that are present, He clearly sees wherever they arise”

[This verse is extracted from Bhaddekaratta Sutta (MN131; a single excellent night)]

3. Contemplating the Body and Cultivating Awareness

Next, one detaches from the body by mindfully recognizing its nature and cultivates awareness in every posture.

“Kāyo anantādīnavo āvāso,
Sabba roga rukkha mūlo”
“This body is a dwelling full of endless faults,
the root of all disease and suffering.”

[Not a direct quote; likely inspired by Girimānanda Sutta (AN10.60;
Girimānanda) and Apadāna (PTS 2.467)]

“Gate ṭhite bhāsite tuṇhībhāve sampajānakārī bhikkhu.”
“A bhikkhu acts in full awareness when walking, standing, talking, and keeping silent.”

“Gacchanto gacchāmi”
“When walking, one knows: ‘I am walking.’”

[Paraphrased from Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta (DN22; the greater discourse on the foundations of mindfulness)]

4. Reflecting on Impermanence

While walking, the contemplation deepens by examining the conditioned and transient nature of the body and all formations.

“Āhārasambhūto kāyo
Āhāraṁ aniccaṁ dukkhaṁ anattaṁ
Kāyaṁ aniccaṁ dukkhaṁ anattaṁ
Sabbasaṅkhāraṁ aniccaṁ dukkhaṁ anattaṁ”

“This body is sustained by food.
Food is impermanent, subject to suffering, and not-self.
The body is impermanent, subject to suffering, and not-self.
All conditioned things are impermanent, suffering, and not-self.”

[Not a direct quote; likely inspired by Puttamaṁsa Sutta (SN12.63; son’s flesh)]

5. Aspiring to Be Like the Sages

Reflects on calming the body, speech and mind, practicing restraint, understanding impermanence, and following the path of the wise ones.

“Santakāyo santavāco
Santavā susamāhito
Vantalokāmiso bhikkhu
Tādisā samaṇā mama
Santindriyā santamānasā
Santaṁ tesaṁ gataṁ ṭhitaṁ
Okkhittacakkhū mitabhāṇī
Tādisā samaṇā mama”

“Peaceful in body, peaceful in speech
Living serenely, inwardly composed
The bhikkhu who has cast away worldly bait
Such are the ascetics dear to me

Peaceful in faculties, peaceful in mind
Peaceful is their going, peaceful their standing
With lowered gaze and measured speech 
Such are the ascetics dear to me”

[Not a direct quote; likely inspired by Sayings of the Buddha, Dhammapada Verse 378 and the commentary to Dhammapada verse 304 (Cūḷa Subhaddā’s Story)]

“Kāyaṁ purāṇaṁ kammaṁ,
Abhisaṅkhataṁ abhisañcetayitaṁ,
Aniccaṁ nirodha dhammaṁ.”

“This body is old karma.
Formed and shaped by prior intentions.

It is impermanent and subject to cessation.”

[Not a direct quote; likely inspired by Natumha Sutta (SN12.37; not yours)]

6. Contemplating Mundane Dependent Origination

The reflection then turns to the causal chain that perpetuates suffering (saṁsāra).

“Avijjā paccayā saṅkhārā,
Saṅkhāra paccayā viññāṇaṁ,
Viññāṇa paccayā jāti,
Jāti paccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparideva dukkha domanassupāyāsā sambhavanti.”

“With ignorance as condition, mental formations arise,
With mental formations as condition, consciousness arises,
With consciousness as condition, birth arises,
With birth as condition, arise aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair.”

[Condensed form of dependent origination; extracted from Paṭiccasamuppāda Sutta (SN12.1; dependent origination)]

7. Contemplating the Impermanence of All Formations

Imagine what it’s like to taste the peace of cessation—the stilling of formations—echoed in the Buddha’s final words.

“Vayadhammā saṅkhārā,
Uppādavayadhammino,
Uppajjitvā nirujjhanti,
Tesaṁ vūpasamo sukho.”

“Conditioned things are subject to decay,
Having the nature to arise and pass away,
They come into being and then cease.
Their stilling is bliss”

[Combination of the Buddha’s final words and the verse uttered by Sakka, Lord of the Gods, following his passing; from the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days).]

“Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā’ti,
Yadā paññāya passati,
Atha nibbindati dukkhe,
Esa maggo visuddhiyā.”

“All conditioned things are impermanent,
When one sees this with wisdom,
One becomes disenchanted with suffering.
This is the path to purification.”

[Sayings of the Buddha, extracted from Dhammapada—Verse 277]

8. Contemplating Transcendental Dependent Origination

As these contemplations are deeply realized in the present moment, the chain of suffering begins to cease, revealing the liberating sequence that culminates in Nibbāna.

“Avijjā nirodhā saṅkhāra nirodho,
Saṅkhāra nirodhā viññāṇa nirodho,
Viññāṇa nirodhā jāti nirodho,
Jāti nirodhā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparideva dukkha domanassupāyāsā nirujjhanti.

Etaṁ santaṁ etaṁ paṇītaṁ, yadidaṁ sabba saṅkhāra samatho, Sabbūpadhipaṭinissaggo,
Taṇhakkhayo, virāgo, nirodho, nibbānan ti.”

“With the cessation of ignorance, mental formations cease,
With the cessation of mental formations, consciousness ceases,
With the cessation of consciousness, birth ceases,

With the cessation of birth, aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair all cease.

This is the peaceful, this is the sublime: the calming of all formations, The relinquishment of all attachments,
The destruction of craving, dispassion, cessation, and Nibbāna.”

[Condensed form of dependent origination; extracted from Paṭiccasamuppāda Sutta (SN12.1; dependent origination) and closing verse describing the peace of Nibbāna is extracted from Girimānanda Sutta (AN10.60; Girimānanda)]

A Note for the Practitioner

While this sequence reflects the specific contemplations verbalized by Venerable Ñāṇavimala, practitioners are encouraged to adapt these principles—selecting verses from the Suttas that resonate with their own experience—to support mindfulness and insight during walking meditation.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s Suggested Readings for Saṅgha
(monastics)

Given by Ven. Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera mainly to young monks—newly ordained or beginning to live independently. He stressed that genuine training starts with a clear grasp of the Buddha’s gradual path (anupubba-paṭipadā): from virtue (sīla) to concentration (samādhi) and finally to wisdom (paññā).

• MN117: Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (the great forty); Complete the Noble Eightfold Path in a specific order to obtain samādhi. After that sammāñāṇa and sammāvimutti.

• The gradual training for a bhikkhu;

• MN107: Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta (to Gaṇaka Moggallāna)

• MN39: Mahā-Assapura Sutta (the greater discourse at Assapura)

• MN53: Sekha Sutta (the disciple in higher training)

• MN59: Bahuvedanīya Sutta (the many kinds of feeling)

• MN27: Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta (the shorter discourse on the simile of the elephant’s footprint)

• MN125: Dantabhūmi Sutta (the grade of the tamed); The gradual training for jhāna.

• AN10.61: Avijjā Sutta (ignorance); If you have difficulties at the present level of practice then take one step back and practice at that level.

• MN61: Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā); Sīla: for laypeople—eight precepts; for monastics—Pātimokkha; for ariyas—ariyakanta sīla. This Sutta is describing a different kind of sīla, which is purity in kāya, vācā and mano (tīṇi sucaritāni). Based on this sīla, pīti will arise.

• MN19: Dvedhāvitakka Sutta (two kinds of thought); Dividing the thoughts.1

• MN7: Vatthūpama Sutta (the simile of the cloth); Reflecting on the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha leads to step by step progress as follows: saddhā, pāmujja, pīti, passaddhakāya,2 sukha, samādhi. This process is how one comes to samādhi with joy and Dhamma-
understanding as a basis.

• MN40: Cūḷa-Assapura Sutta (the shorter discourse at Assapura); More or less the same as MN7: Vatthūpama Sutta (the simile of the cloth) but based on being purified from twelve unwholesome mental qualities (covetousness, ill will, anger, hostility, disdain, contempt, jealousy, stinginess, deviousness, deceit, evil desires, and wrong view).

• MN64: Mahāmālunkya Sutta (the greater discourse to Mālunkyāputta); How to practice for sammāñāṇa and sammāvimutti with jhāna as the basis.
