  1. 6 The following collections from Bala Saṁyutta (SN50; connected discourses on the powers) to Sotāpatti Saṁyutta (SN55; connected discourses on stream-entry) describe successive stages of mental development and provide a gradual training that culminates in the attainment of stream-entry (sotāpatti).

  2. 7 These Suttas are all found in the Vedanā Saṁyutta (SN36; connected discourses on feeling) of the Saṁyutta Nikāya. They deal with the nature of feelings (vedanā) and how to relate to them, particularly in the context of pain, illness, and suffering.

  3. 8 This instruction summarises a progressive path of practice based on canonical
    sources. The     Mahācattārīsaka Sutta (MN117; the great forty) outlines the complete structure of the Noble Eightfold Path leading towards concentration (jhāna). The Khandha Saṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) provides insight into the five aggregates as subjects of clinging, supporting insight into non-self. The following collections—from SN50 through SN55—describe successive stages of mental development: the cultivation of the five spiritual powers (Balasaṁyutta, SN50), the bases of spiritual power (Iddhipādasaṁyutta, SN51), mindfulness meditation and its fruits (Anuruddhasaṁyutta, SN52), the absorptions (Jhānasaṁyutta, SN53), mindfulness of breathing (Ānāpānasaṁyutta, SN54), and finally, the qualities leading to stream-entry (Sotāpattisaṁyutta, SN55). Together, these provide a gradual training that culminates in the attainment of stream-entry (sotāpatti).

  4. 9 The phrase kālaṁ āgameyya literally means “to await one’s time (of death).” In the context of practice, it signifies a state of inner preparedness and equanimity toward death—a serene acceptance that arises when craving and clinging have been weakened. It does not imply passivity or resignation, but rather the culmination of wisdom (paññā) and detachment, where the practitioner lives mindfully, ready to face death without fear or regret.

  5. 10 Ven. Gnanaloka elaborates: Rāhulovāda Sutta aka Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā)—the three ways of conduct—this continuous self-reflection should be done the whole day, using it at night to help reflect on what you have done—from body, words and thoughts. Then determine the next day also not to do it, focusing on how to avoid that. When you are reflecting like this, one can get into a samādhi, and then proceed to attain jhāna (or meditative concentration) through mettā. Doing this before sleep, pushing to the point until one knows when the body needs sleep, then allows the body to naturally fall asleep. This kind of sleep reduces your subconscious inclinations and kamma.

  6. 11 This refers to the Buddha’s counsel to Ven. Ānanda in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days) regarding mātugāma (women): do not see; if seen, do not speak; if speech is required, maintain full mindfulness. It is a renunciant’s discipline of sense restraint—a practical safeguard against sensual desire and a protection for concentration—not a blanket moral judgment.

  7. 12 The five precepts are the basic moral code for daily conduct. The eight precepts (aṭṭhaṅgika uposatha sīla) represent a higher spiritual discipline, requiring celibacy, abstinence from food after noon, and the avoidance of adornment, entertainment, and luxurious beds.

  8. 13 Ven. Ñāṇavimala adhered strictly to the Sutta Piṭaka as the complete guide for human liberation. He once commented on the detailed analytical doctrine, stating, ‘Abhidhamma is for the devas,’ implying its intricate framework was primarily suited for celestial beings, while the practical path for human effort lay in the Suttas.

  9. 14 Mettā cetovimutti—the liberation of mind through loving-kindness—is said to temporarily purify and uplift consciousness, strengthening the five spiritual faculties (indriya): faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. While not a final liberation, this wholesome release (cetovimutti) powerfully supports the development of these faculties toward full emancipation—Aṭṭhakanāgara Sutta (MN52; the man from Aṭṭhakanāgara).

  10. 15 Ven. Ñāṇavimala noted that the Three Wise Men (Magi), said to come from the East, were spiritual sages (rishis) who recognized Christ as a “spiritual king.” This exchange, facilitated by the ancient Silk Route, suggested that established Eastern ethical principles—non-violence and compassion—helped shape early Christian ethics. The main point is that these core ethics had Eastern resonances, even if later religious practices diverged from the strict morality (sīla) of the Buddhist path. Doctrinally, however, Abrahamic metaphysics—a Creator-God and an eternal soul—is fundamentally incompatible with the Dhamma’s principles of non-self (anatta) and dependent origination (paṭiccasamuppāda).

  11. 16 This is a reference to the journey of the Greek hero Odysseus in Homer’s The Odyssey (Book XII). Ven. Ñāṇavimala uses it to convey a powerful Dhamma teaching: Shanaka’s note fuses two separate threats to create a single simile for sensual craving (kāma-sukha): the Sirens (who used their alluring songs to tempt sailors) and the monstrous, flesh-eating Scylla (who caused literal death). Odysseus’s instruction to be tied to the mast and use ear wax represents the fierce determination (adhiṭṭhāna) and disciplined sense-restraint (indriya saṁvara sīla) required to survive the temptation of the pleasures and avoid their resulting dangers (ādīnava).

  12. 17 This likely refers to the tendency of some Buddhists to delay or postpone effort in meditation, believing they will attain liberation under the future Buddha, Metteyya (Maitreya). Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s point highlights the danger of such complacency—those reborn in lower realms will miss that opportunity entirely.

  13. 18 This progression refers to the tenfold path of a “noble one,” which extends beyond the standard Noble Eightfold Path. While the eighth factor is sammā samādhi (Right Concentration), it is followed by sammā ñāṇa (Right Knowledge) and sammā vimutti (Right Liberation), which together represent the final insight and complete freedom of an enlightened being.

  14. 19 Ven. Gnanaloka explains what Ven. Ñāṇavimala means: People will try to determine whether someone has attained arahantship by observing the body—appearance or physical and verbal actions. But arahantship has not occurred in the body; it has occurred with regard to consciousness. To perceive this, one must at minimum be able to perceive another's consciousness, which is not possible for one without such capability. Additionally, one must know what insights have arisen. For one who has not destroyed defilements fully, it is thus “unthinkable.” People interpret according to the level of their own defilements.

  15. 20 Ven. Ñāṇavimala explained that the deliberate effort to bring the mind to the present moment, and even the purposeful avoidance of mental distractions, constitutes new wholesome kamma (kusala kamma). This intentional redirection of energy in the present moment is a powerful action that will bear positive results, strengthening the path.

  16. 21 Āmisa sukha is “worldly happiness,” the fleeting pleasure that comes from satisfying the five senses. Nirāmisa sukha is “spiritual happiness,” the more profound and stable joy that arises from renunciation, meditation, and wisdom.
