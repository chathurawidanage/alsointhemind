Reflection 09

In the Footsteps of a Master

When Ven. Ñāṇavimala lay bedridden in his final years at Parappaduwa Island Hermitage, his attendants would meet him each morning for a blessing before their alms round. As Ven. Gnanaloka recalls, “He would put us in a mettā state… In his bed, you would know that he’s having a kind of vision. You could see that he is seeing something. That’s very, very clear.”

These visions shifted from day to day, and his blessings became a vast field of loving-kindness (mettā). He would encourage his attendants to join him in spreading mettā, beginning with the local villagers and gradually extending it to all beings, offering brief descriptions to aid their visualization. At some point, he began regularly mentioning spreading it to the “youth who have gone
astray in the North.” His usual specific mentions included “the fisherman who would serve,” referring to the daily offering they would get from them in their alms round.

For three consecutive days, however, a curious new phrase entered the blessing. After the usual verses, he said, “Even those who are misguided in the North… they will also serve.”

The repetition of this final phrase was strange. To Ven. Gnanaloka it seemed misplaced, almost puzzling. Perhaps a moment of confusion brought on by age.

The North was then the heartland of the civil war, where Buddhist monks were often singled out by the LTTE for their saffron robes. They were often seen as political instigators, symbols of the Sinhala-Buddhist state. Records tell of monks being dragged from buses and shot,1 temples bombed,2 and rural communities living in fear of sudden massacres.3 Shanaka’s own cousin, an Air Force pilot, had been killed in the conflict there. Such a remark was not just unlikely; it was an impossibility.

But two years later, Ven. Gnanaloka found himself deep in rebel-held territory, standing barefoot as a senior LTTE leader, a member of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, the main separatist group in Sri Lanka’s civil war, removed his cap and placed a handful of vadai (a savoury fried snack) into his alms bowl.

It had all begun at Parappaduwa, where Ven. Gnanaloka was serving his teacher. Inspired by the stories of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s solitary wanderings, he felt moved to take up the ancient practice of cārikā himself. It was a path of radical uncertainty, a practice of meeting each moment without a plan. During those days, when Ven. Ñāṇavimala was asked where he was going, his reply was simple: “I haven’t decided yet. I’ll decide when I get to the corner.”

It was this spirit of profound, present-moment freedom that so inspired the young disciple.

When Ven. Gnanaloka asked his teacher for permission, Ven. Ñāṇavimala gave him a resolute blessing: “Don’t worry. Your guardian devas4 are behind you.” That single line, spoken with such certainty, was more than a promise; it was a reflection of the Dhamma’s protective power.

His teacher had often spoken of a principle woven through the ascetic life: that the pure virtue of a wandering monk naturally attracts the support of unseen deities, or devatās. “Those who are practising,” Ven. Ñāṇavimala would say, “devatās will follow,” drawn to virtue to gain merit. He spoke from decades of experience. There were times when a particular food would come to mind, and then appear before him in ways that seemed beyond coincidence. Once, arriving unannounced in a remote village near Minneriya National Park where food was scarce and no one could have known he was coming, an elderly woman offered him a carefully prepared meal. She told him that a devatā had appeared in her evening prayers, instructing her that a monk would arrive the next morning.5

Yet Ven. Ñāṇavimala would always add caution: wishes fulfilled in such ways could stir further desire. A practising monk must guard his mind with the utmost care.

Ven. Gnanaloka departed with no itinerary. Just a direction—north, a rudimentary map, and the inner compass of sati (mindfulness). After seeking the blessing of his preceptor,6 Ven. Madihe Paññāsīha Mahānāyaka Thera, who told him “Do it without falling ill, this is the time to try such things,” he began walking. He began the journey on 9 April 2003. The path north was no simple undertaking. While Nāgadīpa and the Jaffna peninsula were under government control at the time, he first had to pass through vast areas of the Vanni region, which were still administered by the
LTTE.

He would walk 15 kilometers in the early morning and another 15 in the late afternoon. With Sati and Samādhi as his companions, the body did not complain.

When he reached Anuradhapura, he rested for a week, asking a few lorry drivers about the conditions ahead. He knew that entering rebel-controlled territory would mean stepping into uncertainty, perhaps even death. But as he later shared, “I didn't have an army to protect me—only the teachings of the Buddha, mettā, and the understanding that this body is not mine.”

He carried with him the contemplation from the Khandha-saṁyutta—the teaching on the five aggregates (khandhas) of clinging. Form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness—that is all that takes place. And he saw those he would meet in the same way: not soldiers or rebels, but aggregates arising and passing away, like his own. Just nature meeting nature.

He would recollect: “The khandhas are universal, this craving is universal, whoever decreases this craving through wisdom—with that, dispassion will come, and they can be liberated. I feel it is a potential everybody has. In this way I had a lot of mettā.”

Before crossing the last government military checkpoint, he sought out a monk to purify his sīla and prepare for the possibility of death.7 A forest monk from the Wathuruwila tradition, fluent in Tamil, helped him and even went to the extent of preparing a note for alms rounds and taught him the sacred Tamil phrases: “Subam, śānti, samādhānam” (auspiciousness, peace, harmony).

The soldiers at the final government outpost tried to stop him.8 It was a plea, not an order. “Is there active conflict?” he asked. “No,” they replied. And with that, the Monastic Rules permitted his passage.9

On the other side he was not met with bullets. Instead, a senior leader stepped forward. He spoke English, arranged a travel pass, offered food, and even shielded Ven. Gnanaloka from publicity. Others came—militants, villagers, children—drawn by the sight of a lone monk walking barefoot in silence. “Paavam, paavam [poor thing],” some children would call to their parents from outside as they saw him coming, feeling pity at the sight of him. To others they posed simple questions. “Saappida?”—have you eaten?

He moved through areas without human habitation, across forests and dry plains, where the only shelter was the Dhamma. When militants approached him with suspicion, a kind stranger would often appear at just the right moment, offering reassurance or safe passage.

The journey was a series of such unexpected moments. Some of quiet grace, others of sudden danger.

Once, a motorcycle came hurtling toward him, its rider intent on intimidation. It veered away only at the final instant. Later, with a smile, Ven. Gnanaloka said, “I was ready to die. It seemed he wasn't.”

Through his walk he noticed what had become normalized. Empty sleeves where arms should have been. Missing eyes. Food, a scarcity. Years of unmarked minefields, checkpoints, displacement, and loss. In some, deep-seated anger had taken root. People who had never known another world. He saw, for them, that this had become their only reality.

As he moved deeper into rebel territory, LTTE's Chief of Police Balasingham Nadesan arrived, inspected him, and then asked a question that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than protocol: “Will we ever get peace?”

Ven. Gnanaloka replied, “Peace is inside, not outside.”

“I never saw them as terrorists,” he would later say. “I felt as if I were meeting my own children.”

This feeling was never clearer than in an encounter at Kilinochchi—a place then known as the most heavily secured region in the North, the heart of LTTE control. Some had warned him, “Not to pass through that and go somehow another way. If you do, do not linger there.”

He entered anyway.

Word seemed to have spread of a lone monk, arriving barefoot.

Young soldiers who looked barely sixteen were positioned every twenty meters along the roadside on the way to the main checkpoint. As he approached, they would duck behind trees, then peer out with wide eyes at the strange sight of this monk. Was he mad? How could he not be frightened? The villagers watched too, they seemed to feel it. He didn't show any fear.

At the checkpoint, a young cadre tasked with a body search approached, his eyes filled with a conflict between his duty and his devotion. He looked at Ven. Gnanaloka and said, “Seniors are watching me, Swami, so I need to make physical contact with you.” He then closed his eyes to perform the search. As he did, Ven. Gnanaloka recalls, “I have never seen so much devotion in anyone, Sinhalese or otherwise.”10 The young cadre performed the search, struggling against an inner reluctance to lay hands on him.

After passing through the checkpoint, he was asked if he had a place to stay. He didn't. A policewoman escorted him through the town. When they arrived at a church, a priest greeted him warmly with joined palms, almost with relief, and offered a place to stay for the night.

That evening, LTTE officials questioned him for nearly two hours. A young translator sat between them, carefully choosing his words. Ven. Gnanaloka could sense the translator's protectiveness. His words were shaped so the monk wouldn't appear suspicious or dangerous. Each translation deliberate, as if shielding him. It helped ease any doubts the officials might have had. He stayed one night and moved on.

At another checkpoint along his route, when a militant asked “Are you not afraid of us? We are LTTE,” his answer was: “As long as I have this Shakthiya [inner spiritual strength], I'm not afraid.”

As Ven. Gnanaloka left the LTTE-controlled area, LTTE cadres followed on motorbikes, eager to speak with him one last time. “We have no conflict with your thoughts,” they told him.

He made his way north through government-held areas to Nāgavihara, and from there to the sacred islet of Nāgadīpa. The journey there had taken a month. While staying in a makeshift shelter built by sailors near the Dambakolapatuna Navy Camp, his mind turned to his cousin—the Air Force pilot whose plane had gone down somewhere in these northern waters more than seven years earlier, in 1995. When the camp commander arrived with the daily meal, Ven. Gnanaloka asked if he happened to know anything about the crash.

The commander's reply was uncanny. Just a month earlier, local fishermen had pulled a large piece of wreckage from their net. It was the engine of the lost aircraft. The spot where they found it, deep in the ocean, was just three kilometers ahead of where he sat.

The path had, without any intention, brought him to the place tied to the one Ven. Ñāṇavimala had once sent blessings to, before word of his cousin’s passing had reached him. He stayed an extra day. With the support of the fishermen, he went out on a boat to the exact location and performed a blessing over the water.

His journey now felt complete, and for the first time, his body began asking for rest. He planned to return to Polgasduwa by bus. He was offered a stay at a pilgrims’ resthouse in Nāgavihara. They had learned of his walk from Jaffna and managed to make space for him despite the lack of rooms available.

The resthouse was mixed accommodation for public guests. He was conscious of the Vinaya rules that stated if women were staying under the same roof, the lodging needed a separate entrance, otherwise it wasn't appropriate for a monk to stay overnight.11 The current arrangement he was given was not exactly in line with these rules.

He lay down and began to meditate. As he heard a sound, the thought came that he had walked such a long distance only to compromise on Vinaya rules in the last leg because of convenience. A question occurred to him—would he leave this room, or simply forget about the rules in carelessness? He decided he couldn't sacrifice his discipline. He went across the road to the shrine room of the main Nāgavihara temple. Trying not to draw attention, he approached a lay attendant there and asked if he could arrange a simple mat for him to rest on overnight, as he was observing the Vinaya rule. He asked him to keep it to himself. He would use the resthouse during the day instead.

When he returned to that shrine room that evening, to his surprise, he saw that all the monks had gotten involved in preparing his space with even a mattress placed.

Later that night, as he lay down there, he suddenly felt the feeling of Buddha's presence, of him being alive. This was not through the Sutta understanding this time, but through the Vinaya. He had the recognition that whenever one makes the effort to adhere to the instructions of the Buddha, you get connected to him in that.

Before his final departure, he went out looking for a quiet place for the day’s meditation. There was a cultural event happening in the area that day so it was too noisy. He headed to find a suitable place in Jaffna. He eventually stumbled upon an old Hindu temple called Kailash Natha Swami. The place was unpopular, so it was an ideal quiet place to meditate. The meditation there took on an especially peaceful and elevated quality.

After returning to the resthouse in Nāgavihara, the following morning he began to meditate, this time at the resthouse itself. The meditation felt light. Like cushioned clouds, thoughts drifted. His body began to unwind. Clouds deepened, turning to grey smoke. It rose in twirls as flames from beneath leaped out of a copper-lined pit. A priest in white sat chanting as he stoked the flames with ghee and raw rice. A fire ritual. Believed to be a primal offering, a purging away.

The priest in white then looked at him and pressed his thumb against Ven. Gnanaloka's forehead. He tried to resist it, but the pressure was an immovable force. As the priest pressed, a blissful floating sensation came over him, as if he was leaving his body, accompanied by an immense surge of energy.

He opened his eyes. A dream.

He couldn't recall the last time he had fallen asleep during meditation. Let alone during a morning meditation.

When he woke he was in a state of heightened refreshment and energy unlike anything from ordinary rest. The thought came to him whether it was that chief priest at the Kailash Natha Swami temple.

He decided to visit it again. When the chief priest came and greeted him, he realised it was not the same priest wearing white. Whilst speaking to him, his eyes began scanning around the room. On the wall behind him, he noticed a picture of three priests in white. He recognised that one of them was the one he saw in the dream. Ven. Gnanaloka asked him who he was. The priest conveyed the answer in broken English: “He is the one who made this temple with his own money. He is very pure. I believe he is a deity now.”

The bus was no longer a consideration. The tiredness had now completely vanished, replaced by an energy that demanded he return the way he came, on foot.

In total, the journey there and back covered over 1,200 kilometers on foot, much of it through the rebel-held territory.

But looking back at the whole journey, one scene remains etched in memory. It was when he was passing Kilinochchi on the way to Nāgadīpa. He remembers families had gathered on either side of the road, some with tear-filled eyes. He could feel the weight of their emotion. Their voices rose together: “Why are you suffering for us Sadhu? Why are you suffering for us?”

There were no words he could give. Only his presence, only mettā.

This was not one such moment, but many—the same kind of scene, time and again, the whole way through the rebel-held territory.

Decades earlier, Ven. Ñāṇavimala had taken up the practice of cārikā for more than twenty-five years, traveling to all kinds of villages. Regardless of what faith background they came from, he always received food. When asked why he had walked for so many years, Ven. Gnanaloka would remember how Ven. Ñāṇavimala would talk about it: “You are faced with the real truth of the kāma loka, then you can see the suffering of it, and you get that real feeling of wanting to come out of it.”

Kāma loka: the world as filtered through our five senses. Not just pleasure, but all of it—the clinging, the loss, the endless wanting.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala had traversed the island south to north, east to west three times over. Walking into every province, every terrain, every kind of human struggle.

Every kind of uncertainty.

According to one account, during the Tamil insurgency of the early 1970s, Ven. Ñāṇavimala had been on cārikā when he was surrounded by armed rebels who suspected him of being a spy. They dragged him into the jungle, threatening to kill him. Ven. Ñāṇavimala asked for a few moments to prepare his mind. Sitting on a rock, he closed his eyes and entered deep meditation. When he was ready, he raised one arm, his signal to be shot.

But no shot came. When he opened his eyes, the forest was empty. His would-be executioners had vanished. He rose and continued walking.

“Dhammo have rakkhati dhammacāriṁ”—the Dhamma protects those who live by the Dhamma.

As Ven. Gnanaloka reflects now, it is not his own courage or clarity that carried him through, but the example of a teacher who had walked that path for decades. Not for what he would gain, but for what he could give. A blessing offered wherever he walked.

For Ven. Gnanaloka, the journey fulfilled a deep aspiration. Yet he came to see that physical extremes do not reveal the Dhamma. For that, one needs meditative seclusion with the Buddha's words.

His teacher had been clear: cārikā was not for everyone. It depended on one's kamma. You don't walk to find the Dhamma. You walk because you already have some sense of it.

But even then, one must remain unbound, even from the practice of cārikā itself. The essence was freedom, not the form.

This journey, then, was never his alone. It was part of an ancient path renewed. A living echo of one who said little, lived simply. Yet transformed lives, one silent act at a time.

  1. 1 The Aranthalawa Massacre (1987): LTTE cadres killed 33 Buddhist monks, most of them novices, in the Ampara District.

  2. 2 The Temple of the Tooth attack (1998): The Temple of the Tooth in Kandy, Sri Lanka’s most sacred Buddhist site housing the Buddha’s tooth relic, was attacked by three LTTE suicide bombers driving an explosive-laden truck.

  3. 3 The Anuradhapura Massacre (1985): LTTE attack at the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi Tree, killing about 146 pilgrims at one of Sri Lanka’s most revered Buddhist shrines.

  4. 4 Devā/devatās are celestial beings.

  5. 5 See Chapter 6, 6 December 1995, for this account in the notes.

  6. 6 Preceptor (Pāli: upajjhāya): The senior monk who gives a new monk his higher ordination (upasampadā) and serves as his primary mentor and spiritual guide. The relationship is traditionally akin to that of a spiritual father and son.

  7. 7 According to the Vinaya (monastic code), a monk purifies his sīla (moral conduct) by formally confessing any minor transgressions to another monk; more serious offenses require a formal meeting of the monastic community (Saṅgha) for resolution. This act of clearing one’s conscience is especially important when facing potential death because a mind unburdened by guilt is more likely to be peaceful.

  8. 8 The peace process between the Sri Lankan government and the LTTE, which began with a Ceasefire Agreement on 22 February 2002, and officially ended on 2 January 2008, was marked by frequent violent outbreaks, making the region dangerous throughout this period.

  9. 9 This refers to a rule in the Vinaya, the monastic code of conduct, which prohibits monks from traveling to or through a place where there is active fighting.

  10. 10 The Sri Lankan Civil War was rooted in political and ethnic tensions between the island’s majority Sinhalese (who are predominantly Buddhist) and the minority Tamil community. Ven. Gnanaloka is of Sinhalese ethnicity.

  11. 11 According to the Aṭṭhakathā (commentary), if a room has a separate entrance, you are allowed to stay in that room. Here, “separate entrance” means that you must be able to enter and leave directly through that door, not into a shared lobby, corridor, or another room.
