15 February 1998

Very Important:

x Establish others in the Mahānāma Suttas, (It is a great service you can do to them).

x Practice according to 37, 40 of Majjhima—Cūḷataṇhāsankhaya Sutta (MN37; the shorter discourse on the destruction of craving); Cūḷa-Assapura Sutta (MN40; the shorter discourse at Assapura).

x Sati sampajañña (mindfulness and clear comprehension) then jhāna then Satipaṭṭhāna.

x To practice Satipaṭṭhāna, you should have sammā diṭṭhi and observe ariya sīla (that is not only verbal and physical action but also watching the mind).—Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).

x Watching the mind means overcoming the nīvaraṇas (hindrances) and cultivating the pañca indriya (five spiritual faculties). This is the daily practice of a Bhikkhu. When one attains the path, one knows how to get about to overcome them. One is not overpowered by them. At arahantship only they are completely overcome, never to arise.

x Even at Anāgāmi, there is kāma rāga not at a gross level but to the feelings connected to the jhānas. Only at the fourth jhāna there is equanimity, but it is a feeling. Then you should consider that all feelings are anicca. They are saṅkhāras.

x The Saṅgha is also a saṅkhāra. What you do in the Buddha’s teaching is to root out the cause of suffering. You don’t try to remove it from outside.

x What does not arise, does not pass away, that is the end of suffering. The Dhamma is used as a raft. You see all
formations as anicca, then only you have nibbidā (disenchantment with worldly life) then you become distanced from all saṅkhāras (formations), then it is seen as “not mine,” “not I,” etc.

x When lobha (greed), dosa (hate) and moha (delusion) are rooted out, suffering is rooted out.

x You will come to the final goal.

x 12 Suttas is enough, you mustn’t be loaded with theoretical knowledge.25

  1. 18 Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies this is a teaching on skillful disengagement. The instruction to not “fight” a defilement is because direct resistance, born of aversion, can paradoxically give it more energy. The metaphor to “feed” it, or treat it like a guest, means to calmly acknowledge its presence without alarm or self-blame. Don’t take it too seriously, else it will grow in power. Having acknowledged it without conflict, the practitioner does not indulge it, but skillfully diverts it away and cultivates wholesome qualities, such as the five spiritual faculties (indriyas).

  2. 19 This refers to the Janapadakalyāṇī Sutta (SN47.20; the most beautiful girl of the land), where the Buddha uses a powerful simile for mindfulness. A man is commanded to carry a bowl filled to the brim with oil through a crowd watching a beautiful dancing woman. A guard follows with a raised sword, ready to execute him if he spills a single drop. The Buddha explains that the bowl of oil represents mindfulness of the body (kāyagatāsati), which must be guarded with the same life-or-death urgency and unwavering attention amidst the world’s most powerful distractions.

  3. 20 The advice on Mātugāma (women) was given to Shanaka to emphasize the reflection on the danger of sensuality (kāma ādīnava) on his spiritual path. This challenge manifests through the physical form of the opposite sex.

  4. 21 The “immediately calming” method points to the Bāhiya instruction: “in the seen, just the seen; in the heard, just the heard; in the sensed, just the sensed; in the cognized, just the cognized,” given in Bāhiya Sutta (Ud1.10; with Bāhiya); a close parallel occurs at Mālukyaputta Sutta (SN35.95; Māluṅkyaputta).

  5. 22 The complete progressive framework from understanding the aggregates—Khandhasaṁyutta (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates)—to entering the stream—Sotāpattisaṁyutta (SN55; connected discourses on stream-entry)—the full range of insight and path teachings in the Saṁyutta Nikāya.

  6. 23 This refers to the Makhādeva Sutta (MN83; king Makhādeva), which tells the story of a king who, upon seeing his first grey hair, immediately made a firm determination to renounce the world and take up the holy life. Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies that the teaching here is about the necessity of similarly making a resolute determination in one’s own practice. Without such a firm determination, one’s latent tendencies (anusaya) will continue to arise and take hold. Only a practitioner who has already attained a stage of enlightenment is considered safe from these latent tendencies.

  7. 24 This list of Suttas constitutes a highly specific curriculum designed by Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera to cultivate sammā ñāṇa (Right Knowledge) and lead directly to the final result (Nibbāna). It organizes texts crucial for developing full insight into the Four Noble Truths.

  8. 25 “Twelve Suttas is enough” means depth over breadth. Take a small, well-chosen set, learn them thoroughly—read, memorize key lines, reflect, practice—so study (pariyatti) becomes practice (paṭipatti) and realization (paṭivedha). The point is to avoid theoretical overload, not to forbid wider study.
