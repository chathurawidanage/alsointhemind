# Chapter 08: “What Doesn’t Arise, Doesn’t Pass Away”

## Reflection 08: Breath, Consciousness and the Final Moment

There were rare moments when Ven. Ñāṇavimala sensed that the conditions were right, when Shanaka’s mind was settled and receptive, that he would offer teachings on the deepest truths. It was during one of these quiet intervals, in the cool shade beside the library at Siri Vajirarama Temple, that he posed a question to Shanaka:

In the final moments, at physical death, is the arahant<Footnote id="1">Arahant is a fully enlightened being.</Footnote> able to keep the attention on the breath or does it go elsewhere?

This was no idle question. Behind it lay the profound mechanics of consciousness (*viññāṇa*) and the nature of rebirth.

In the Buddha’s teaching, this question is particularly significant because it touches on the ultimate goal of the path: the complete end of suffering (*dukkha*). This isn’t just everyday unhappiness, but the deep, underlying stress that exists in every life, whether seemingly fortunate or not—the stress of being subject to loss, the pain of unfulfilled desire, sickness, aging and death.

Ending rebirth is the only final solution, because even a life in a peaceful heavenly realm is temporary. As Ven. Ñāṇavimala had once remarked:

> “Even the Brahma world<Footnote id="2">The Brahma world is a celestial realm of immense peace and bliss where beings experience extraordinarily long lifespans, free from physical pain and gross forms of suffering.</Footnote> is suffering. One may re-arise even in the hell realms.”

Therefore, to break this chain for good, one must see it clearly. This means understanding what binds the process of consciousness, turning it into the crucial link that connects one life to the next.

To appreciate the weight of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s question, one must first understand consciousness within the context of the Dhamma. There is the body composed of flesh and bone, but there is adifference between a body with consciousness and one without. Without consciousness, it is merely a physical object. With consciousness, there is a knowing, tied to one of the six sense bases: eye, ear, nose, tongue, body or mind.

Take the ear faculty: there is the ear and there are sound waves. Yet consciousness does not necessarily arise. Only when there is the ear and sound is brought into attention do the conditions align for ear-consciousness to arise. When it arises, this consciousness is a ‘knowing-ness’ associated with that particular sense faculty. It is not an independent entity or ‘soul’ existing on its own. The meetingof the three: ear, sound, and ear-consciousness is ‘contact’ (*phassa*). From contact, feeling (*vedanā*) arises: pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. Alongside feeling, perception (*saññā*) may arise, recognizing and designating what is present—for example, “this is a sound” or “this is a table.” More fundamentally, perception judges the feeling itself: for example, “this is pleasant,” “this is unpleasant.”

From here, based on being judged as “pleasant,” a reaction occurs with desire and greed; as “unpleasant,” a reaction occurs with aversion and hatred; as “neutral,” a reaction occurs with delusion.

*Saṅkhāra*<Footnote id="3">The Pāli word *saṅkhāra*—translated by Ven. Bhikkhu Bodhi as “volitionalformations,” a rendering he himself acknowledges as imperfect—carries subtle shades of meaning depending on context. Its essence lies in its conditioned nature: phenomena that arise dependent on causes and conditions. Within Dependent Origination (*paṭiccasamuppāda*), *saṅkhāra* refers to the intentional or willedactivities (“intentional reactions” as rendered by Ven. Gnanaloka) rooted in ignorance, that condition the arising of consciousness and propel the round of existence. When appearing among the five aggregates (*pañcupādānakkhandhā*), it retains these essential characteristics but takes on a subtle contextual difference. However, for the purpose of this reflection, it is unessential to know this distinction in depth. Ven. Ñāṇavimala himself steered away from elaborate definitions. Ven. Gnanaloka encourages seekers to gain a gradual understanding through broader readings of *Suttas* and reflection, rather than through intellectual analysis of a term.</Footnote> is the Buddha’s term for these intentional reactions, and they are born from ignorance (*avijjā*). Ignorance, here, is the blindness to these conditioned processes: not knowing their impermanent and suffering nature, their cause, their cessation, or the path to their end. A mind veiled by this ignorance rushes toward these burning processes, continually getting hurt, drawn to them, as Ven. Ñāṇavimala warned, “like the moths who fly into the flames.” This reactive process constitutes willed and intentional actions, which manifest through mind, speech, and body as *mano-kamma,* *vacī-kamma* and *kāya-kamma.*<Footnote id="4">*Kamma* (or more widely known as “*karma*”—a Sanskrit form of the word) in this context of Dependent Origination, is virtually synonymous with *saṅkhāra*, as described by Bhikkhu Bodhi, both derived from the verb *karoti*—‘to act, do, or make.’</Footnote> Moment by moment, across the six sense faculties, this continuously drives the ongoing accumulation of new *kamma* and the *karmic* consequences they produce.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught that at the last moment of death, an object appears according to one’s accumulated *kamma*, becoming a condition for consciousness to re-arise. He would illustrate this by giving the example of a virtuous businessman whose mind in this life had been conditioned by business, whose thoughts habitually turned to trade and transactions. Those habitual desires become the *kamma* that fuels the final-moment object—the condition for consciousness to re-arise. Then that person may be reborn in a life with favorable conditions for doing business, such as being born to parents who are supportive of business. Particular conditions follow from how one occupies the present moment, he would say.

Years later, reflecting on these teachings, Ven. Gnanaloka further related his understanding to the one who is one stage below final liberation—the anāgāmi.<Footnote id="5">Anāgāmi: The third of the four stages of enlightenment; a “Non-returner,” meaning one who will not be reborn into the sensual realms (which includes the human and animal worlds) before attaining final liberation.</Footnote> In contrast to the arahant, the anāgāmi has cut off the lower fetters like ill will (*vyāpāda*) and lust for the five senses (*kāmarāga*), but may still harbor subtle desire for the sixth sense (mind door), in addition to having conceit (*māna*), restlessness (*uddhacca*) and ignorance (*avijjā*).<Footnote id="6">For further context, see the Pāli framework of the Ten Fetters (*dasa saṁyojanāni*).</Footnote> Lacking grossdesire for material existence, the anāgāmi retains subtle attachment as well as *kamma* towards the higher formless existences. He understood that this becomes the object that conditions the consciousness to move on, which is why the anāgāmi is reborn in a higher, formless Brahma plane. The subtle attachment has not been completely uprooted, unlike the arahant. Thus, at the moment of physical death, attention is not able to remain exclusively on the breath but gravitates to the object of subtle attachment, which is that of the equanimous *samādhi*. Ven. Ñāṇavimala used to remark that this equanimity is the highest that the consciousness could reach.

Even modern neuroscience, with all its instruments, still struggles to define consciousness. It looks to the brain to trace how neural processes might give rise to consciousness. But the ancient teachings had already revealed that consciousness does not arise randomly, nor from matter alone, but due to specific causes.

Pre-dating Buddhism, the spiritual traditions existing in ancient India already recognized that consciousness arises through specific *karmic* causes. This understanding of *kamma* was related to the world of the five senses. Escape was sought through *samādhi*—a deep meditative experience of stillness of mind*.* They found it was possible to overcome greed and anger with blissfulness and purity, and thus be reborn into the Brahma plane of existence. Yet even a state such as “pure consciousness” is also a feeling, no matter how subtle. As Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught: “They consider that which they strive for as permanent, but it is still within consciousness and therefore a formation.”

Ven. Gnanaloka explains: the Buddha found that where there is feeling, there remains a conditioned state of consciousness. The underlying defilements had still not been destroyed. Hence, beings remained trapped in the cycle of rebirth. The Buddha’s unique contribution was that through wisdom, the causes of consciousness, and thus all conditioned existence, could be transcended.

So what of the arahant—the one who has fully uprooted greed, hate and delusion, without traces of craving and *kamma*?

Ven. Ñāṇavimala explained that the arahant, in that finalmoment, can maintain attention solely on the breath without attachment. The breath is simply known as it is, nothing more. Because there is no *kamma*, there is no object—a thought—to distract attention. Because there is no craving one doesn’t get distracted. Even the breath itself is not attached to. With conditions absent for consciousness to arise, no rebirth occurs. Ven. Gnanaloka recalls how powerfully his teacher’s words resonated: “As Bhante described it, I remember imagining it happening to me and a great peace came over my mind just from the reflection.” After giving this explanation, “It’s deep,” Ven. Ñāṇavimala would warn Shanaka.

Looking back, he reflects: “This is the final liberation of any living being. You’re ending an entire journey, a beginningless journey—*saṁsāra.* When I used to speak to Bhante I felt like he is swimming across from a vast ocean, the way he explains.”

Ven. Gnanaloka would draw the reflection out further. Imagine a situation where someone has been told that they have only a short time to live. They may also have heard advice from somewhere that in the dying moments they should keep their attention on the breath. If one makes an effort by force to keep their full attention on their breath in the dying moment, could they? He says “you will find you can't do that.” The arahant, the one without attachment, doesn't have that struggle. When there are no desires, the breath is seen naturally, without effort.<Footnote id="7">Refer to *Sakkapañha Sutta* (SN35.118; Sakka’s question) where the Buddha explainsthat where there is clinging (*upādāna*), one will not attain Nibbāna. See also: *Āneñjasappāya Sutta* (MN106; the way to the imperturbable).</Footnote> Such reflection on this conditionality, arising from *yoniso manasikāra*, can break the mental defilements, and hence the craving. He adds that “that thinking must happen—how the consciousness is working.”

This isn't about gaining conceptual knowledge of an arahant's final thought process, nor about training oneself to control the breath at that moment. One must also not take this explanation too literally either, thinking of an idealised way one has to die. This is out of one's control; one may die even in a violent way, in which a disturbing experience precedes the final moment.<Footnote id="8">When asked how the arahant would focus on the breath if drowning, Ven. Gnanaloka responds: “The defilements are broken in stages—the arahant is the purest extent of no clinging at all—that's a state of mind. Whether [the arahant] focuses on the breath or not, it's not necessary to know. Maybe another function of the body. But [the arahant] is in that moment. You must not try to imagine how they are doing it, it becomes very intellectual and speculative. The point of the explanation is not about that, but to demonstrate the conditioned nature of consciousness.”</Footnote> It is the *karmic* habits, however, which condition the final moment object, not specifically the way in which one dies.

The teaching itself, as Ven. Ñāṇavimala gave it, had come to Shanaka years before his first self-retreat in 1994. But the depths of this explanation would be understood later during this self-retreat where he first came across the *Khandhasaṁyutta* and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta.* “I was surprised that so many *Suttas* are there by the Buddha and I became quite absorbed with it,” he recalls. He discovered in their pages the confirmation, and the unfolding meaning of his teacher’s words.

These texts contain the Buddha’s core analysis of experience: the *Khandhasaṁyutta* deconstructs our sense of ‘self’ into five aggregates *(pañcupādānakkhandhā),* while the *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* is an extension of the *Khandhasaṁyutta* to explain the experience of the world through our six senses. As Ven. Gnanaloka reflects on these very aggregates, “craving acts like a glue. It binds consciousness to form,<Footnote id="9">The Pāli word for form is *rūpa.* See glossary for further description.</Footnote> feeling, perception and volitional formations. But when that glue is removed by wisdom, the process cannot exist.”

These meanings are found within the *Suttas,* which were introduced to him from their very first meetings.

During that beginning roadside encounter, when Shanaka described the experience of noticing arising and passing away, Ven. Ñāṇavimala directed him toward the *Satipaṭṭhāna* *Sutta*.<Footnote id="10">In addition to the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness), on this occasion Ven. Ñāṇavimala also directed him to the *Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta* (MN28; the greater discourse on the simile of the elephant’s footprint), which contains a reflection on the four elements that comprise existence.</Footnote> The *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, which lays out the four foundations of mindfulness—of body, feeling, mind, and *Dhammas*<Footnote id="11">*Dhammas*: In this context, refers to mental objects or phenomena, including the hindrances, aggregates, sense-bases, factors of awakening, and Four Noble Truths. The fourth foundation involves observing these categories of experience with mindfulness.</Footnote>—holds a central place in Buddhist practice today. As passed down to him by his teacher, Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies that the proper application of *Satipaṭṭhāna* happens when one is free from the five hindrances (*nīvaraṇas*): sensual desire (*kāmacchanda*), ill-will (*vyāpāda*), sloth and torpor (*thīna-middha*), restlessness and worry (*uddhacca-kukkucca*), and doubt (*vicikicchā*). He explains that this is not a matter of forceful suppression, but of cultivating the opposite qualities of the mind—the five spiritual faculties (*indriyas*)*:* faith (*saddhā*), energy (*viriya*), mindfulness (*sati*)*,* stillness (*samādhi*)*,* and wisdom (*paññā*)*.*

Ven. Ñāṇavimala once hinted to Shanaka that when Shanaka was finding reasons for needing to leave the Island Hermitage, it was because these five *indriyas* were not properly cultivated in a balanced way, and if they were he would just stay on. Ven. Ñāṇavimala further remarked that monks who base their practice solely on the Vinaya (the monks’ code of conduct), without the five *indriyas* being properly cultivated in a balanced way, often disrobe for this underlying reason.

Wisdom is a critical component in the *indriyas*. *Yonisomanasikāra* is fundamental to this, belonging to *Dhammā-nupassanā*—the contemplation of *Dhammas*. While the*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* provides the framework, it is primarily through discourses such as those in the *Khandhasaṁyutta* and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* that this contemplation is developed in various ways. Yet, as Ven. Gnanaloka observes, these detailed teachingshave been largely overlooked in modern practice.

On seeing the arising and passing of the five aggregates and recognizing their impermanent nature, Ven. Gnanaloka elaborates: “This insight goes beyond simply noticing that something appears and later disappears, or that a feeling arises and subsides. This is just an immediate noticing of impermanence. On a contemplative level, however, one sees that the very causes and conditions that create these phenomena are themselves impermanent. In this deeper intuitive insight, one can naturally realize impermanence (*anicca*).”

Since *Dhammānupassanā* is present in all the foundations of mindfulness, Ven. Ñāṇavimala encouraged systematic reading of various *Suttas*, allowing deeper understandings to emerge naturally. This broader approach reveals the full richness of the teaching, rather than focusing narrowly on one aspect such as the body.

Ven. Gnanaloka remembers an incident from his layman days when the *Suttas* revealed their practical power in a most personal way.

One day Ven. Ñāṇavimala looked at Shanaka and remarked, “My thoughts went to your cousin.” Since he didn’t usually comment on worldly affairs, the remark struck Shanaka as unusual. His cousin, an Air Force pilot, was only a year apart in age from Shanaka and they had grown up together more like brothers than cousins. As children they shared toys, and as young men his cousin had confided in Shanaka. The bond was deep, stronger even than the one he shared with his own brother. After Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s remark, the following day a telegram arrived from home calling him to come back urgently to Colombo, without explanation. When he arrived, he found the house full of grieving relatives. His cousin had gone missing in action and was presumed dead.

Rather than react immediately, he made his way through thecrowd to his room at the back, his mother following after him, anticipating his sorrow. Sitting down, he turned his mind inward to the reflections presented in *Saḷāyatana* discourses: ‘*The ear is impermanent, sounds are impermanent, ear-consciousness is impermanent; what is impermanent is suffering; what is impermanent, suffering and subject to change is thus non-self*.’ Hearing the distressing news, he saw it was beyond his control—merely an arisen soundwave reaching his ear. He began to witness his ear faculty as if from a distance as a separate thing, and sound as a separate external occurrence, both momentary and conditioned. The sense of “*this belongs to me, this I am, this is myself*” then quieted. In clearly seeing this as an impersonal process, sadness did not take hold. The mind did not shut the experience out but saw its true dependent and impersonal nature. His mother, moved by his unshaken calm, worshipped him.<Footnote id="12">Worship—bowing with joined palms—is traditionally offered by children to parents in Sri Lankan culture.</Footnote> Later, the full, tragic story came to light: his cousin’s plane had been shot down over the northern war zones<Footnote id="13">The aircraft, an Antonov An-32B troop transport carrier, was shot down while approaching Jaffna, killing all sixty-three people on board, including Co-pilot Flying Officer Nihal Ranjith Fernando (Jnr.), who at the time was the youngest pilot in Sri Lanka to obtain a pilot’s license. It was believed to have been struck by a surface-to-air missile fired by the LTTE. The tragedy occurred on 22 November 1995, several months after the outbreak of Eelam War III, the third phase of the Sri Lankan Civil War.</Footnote> by LTTE<Footnote id="14">The Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) was a militant separatist group opposing the Sri Lankan government in the long-standing civil war (1983–2009).</Footnote> rebels.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala would say, “arahants see everything from a distance.” What Shanaka was only beginning to glimpse was a form of this detached awareness—observing experiences through this framework of causes and conditions—an approach that reveals how the *Suttas*, when read with the right spirit, are not just concepts but can give a direct experience of these truths.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice was that “one must not overload with intellectual grasping of the *Suttas.*” Neither rushing the reading as too simple nor over intellectualizing them as academia or abstract philosophy. Both extremes block what the *Suttas* are meant to reveal. Each *Sutta* provides a means for reflection (*kammaṭṭhāna*), by keeping the mind steady on the reflection, without going too far into wanderings of the past or future. Ven. Gnanaloka offers simple guidance: “Read as if the Buddha were speaking directly to you. Cultivate a childlike openness. For this to happen, one can cultivate *samādhi*<Footnote id="15">Ven. Gnanaloka adds that “faith, mindfulness and sense restraint” lead to *samādhi.*</Footnote>—bringing the mind to the present moment—which creates a desirelessness from the world of five senses, opening the space for intuition to happen. Then read the *Suttas*. Don’t aim for some imagined ‘*magga-phala*’ experience. Let the insights unfold by themselves.” To strike a balance between cultivating stillness while maintaining the contemplation of the *Dhammas* presented in the *Suttas* is the central idea. Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught the *Suttas* serve as “signposts”: practical guides pointing the way for contemplation.

Ven. Gnanaloka observed that when meditators got a powerful insight from contemplating the *Suttas*—like seeing that “this is not mine, not I”—they would sometimes cling to it. “See it as also just a Dhamma perception, which is also impermanent,” he would say, “Keep reading, don’t stop there, because that’s the very reason it happened in the first place. Otherwise, one may get stuck in that or just trying to get that equanimous *samādhi* feeling again.” He clarifies: “That equanimous *samādhi* can arise from contemplating the Dhamma itself, which is good, but that’s also where it can get dangerous. One can then incline more to that peaceful feeling rather than contemplating. For that pure contemplation to happen, one shouldn’t be turning to just that peaceful feeling. Else, it can block you from having deeper insight.” He adds that “it happened to me too.”

Repetition runs through the *Suttas*. Passages recur word for word or through abbreviation, intended to deepen insight with each return. The *Nandakovāda Sutta* (MN146; advice from Nandaka) highlights why this matters.<Footnote id="16">Ven. Gnanaloka explains: This *Sutta* describes the internal sense organs and the external sense impressions, based on that you get the experiences of consciousness and feeling. These are impermanent, changing and fragile. So how can you think that the experience you get from that is solid and permanent? The *Sutta* gives the simile of a skinned cow, which is skinned and placed back. The desire represents the tendons and connective tissue that join the skin to the flesh. So how can you put it back the same way if desire has been cut?</Footnote> The Buddha asked Venerable Nandaka to teach five hundred bhikkhunīs and after he delivered the teaching, the Buddha requested he repeat it. Only after hearing it the second time did all five hundred bhikkhunīs attain liberation. The *Suttas* reveal their power not as single, passing encounters, but as living instructions best engaged with repeatedly. One shouldn’t try to squeeze understanding from them either. If something isn’t clear on first reading, simply move on to the next. Ven. Gnanaloka credits his teacher’s tactful approach in guiding him this way to the *Suttas* again and again.

Such reflections, done with a receptive and investigative mind, could lead to subtle forms of support. He remembered one occasion when, whilst Ven. Ñāṇavimala was speaking about impermanence, Shanaka found himself absorbed in that, silently catching that insight himself. At that moment, Ven. Ñāṇavimala would say, “just wait like that,” watching and waiting as if a form of mind-to-mind communication or internal exercise was taking place.

That subtle influence, he came to understand, was not confined by distance or space.

At another time, while staying in the Himalayas, reading and reflecting in his kuṭi, Ven. Gnanaloka felt an influence—a blessing perhaps—from Ven. Ñāṇavimala, reaching him across the great distance supporting that insightful contemplation. Reflecting on these experiences, Ven. Gnanaloka felt they were not a coincidence, but a kind of connection on a ‘Dhamma-plane’. He reminds fellow practitioners “don’t think you’re alone,” affirming Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice to him—if the qualities are aligned, support comes, even from those beings afar but on the same Dhamma-plane. Ven. Ñāṇavimala passed away not long after this experience, while Ven. Gnanaloka was still practicing in the Himalayas.

Ven. Upasama, another close attendant disciple of Ven. Ñāṇavimala, witnessed Ven. Ñāṇavimala during these last days take his final breath. His account records:

> “Half an hour before he passed away, Ven. Upasama was at his bedside continuously. Ven. Ñāṇavimala got himself prepared for the last minute by feeling his arms and fingers then cleared his ears, pushed up his chin and pulled up his robe to cover his chest, then kept both the arms resting on the chest as if he was ready. Ven. Upasama happened to look at Ven. Ñāṇavimala's face and saw him expel his last breath peacefully. How fortunate would it be to face the last moment in this fully controlled manner! May he have attained the highest bliss, Nibbāna!”<Footnote id="17">For a detailed account refer to *Pure Inspiration: Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera; Recollections of His Life, Practice and Teachings (2016).*</Footnote>

Ultimately, it all points to one truth. The final breath, the last moment of attention, the condition that determines whether consciousness re-arises or ceases—it all depends on how one has perceived the Dhamma and how the defilements have been destroyed. Whether craving still binds, or whether wisdom has finally cut the thread.

### 14 May 1996

- **_Majjhima_ 5 to 10 and in detail MN107, 117, 125 and then to _Saṁyutta_ SN22 to SN50, SN55.**

- *Majjhima* 5 to 10—*Anangaṇa Sutta* (MN5; without blemishes); *Ākankheyya Sutta* (MN6; if a bhikkhu should wish); *Vatthūpama Sutta* (MN7; the simile of the cloth); *Sallekha Sutta* (MN8; effacement); *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta*(MN9; right view); *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness).

- MN107, 117, 125—*Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna); *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty); *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed).

- *Saṁyutta* SN22 to SN50, SN55—*Khandha Saṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) to *Balasaṁyutta* (SN50; connected discourses on the powers), *Sotāpatti Saṁyutta* (SN55; connected discourses on stream-entry).

- **We can’t do without thinking. They are still formations. But one must consider and see whether they are formations that lead towards _asaṅkhata_ (the unconditioned) and make sure that one is coming closer to one’s goal by using the chosen formation.**

- **What one wants to realize—that should be made daily [practice] so that it becomes a habit.**

- **You must never fight with the defilements. You must say “Oh! You are there. Well, I will feed you more” and you should cultivate the five _indriyas_. Never develop or react surprisingly or adversely, [otherwise] the _patigha anusaya_ (latent tendency to aversion) is nourished and the defilements get a strong hold.<Footnote id="18">Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies this is a teaching on skillful disengagement. The instruction to not “fight” a defilement is because direct resistance, born of aversion, can paradoxically give it more energy. The metaphor to “feed” it, or treat it like a guest, means to calmly acknowledge its presence without alarm or self-blame. Don’t take it too seriously, else it will grow in power. Having acknowledged it without conflict, the practitioner does not indulge it, but skillfully diverts it away and cultivates wholesome qualities, such as the five spiritual faculties (*indriyas*).</Footnote> It is the same with wars and so on, repression doesn’t work.**

- **The Dhamma way is very much different to the Christian way of repentance and remorse etc.**

- **One may wish and have long life, but more the body gets older, more the decay.**

- **Think of the simile of the oil taken in the midst of a crowd and the man behind with the sword.<Footnote id="19">This refers to the *Janapadakalyāṇī Sutta* (SN47.20; the most beautiful girl of the land), where the Buddha uses a powerful simile for mindfulness. A man is commanded to carry a bowl filled to the brim with oil through a crowd watching a beautiful dancing woman. A guard follows with a raised sword, ready to execute him if he spills a single drop. The Buddha explains that the bowl of oil represents mindfulness of the body (*kāyagatāsati*), which must be guarded with the same life-or-death urgency and unwavering attention amidst the world’s most powerful distractions.</Footnote> Must remember all these _Suttas_. For now stick just to the essential.**

### 15 February 1998

**_Very Important:_**

- **Establish others in the _Mahānāma Suttas,_ (It is a great service you can do to them).**

- Practice according to 37, 40 of *Majjhima*—*Cūḷataṇhāsankhaya Sutta* (MN37; the shorter discourse on the destruction of craving); *Cūḷa-Assapura Sutta* (MN40; the shorter discourse at Assapura).

- **_Sati sampajañña_ (mindfulness and clear comprehension) then _jhāna_ then _Satipaṭṭhāna._**

- To practice *Satipaṭṭhāna*, you should have *sammā diṭṭhi* and observe *ariya sīla* (that is not only verbal and physical action but also watching the mind).—*Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).

- Watching the mind means overcoming the *nīvaraṇas* (hindrances) and cultivating the *pañca indriya* (five spiritual faculties). This is the daily practice of a Bhikkhu. When one attains the path, one knows how to get about to overcome them. One is not overpowered by them. At arahantship only they are completely overcome, never to arise.

- Even at *Anāgāmi*, there is *kāma rāga* not at a gross level but to the feelings connected to the *jhānas*. Only at the fourth *jhāna* there is equanimity, but it is a feeling. Then you should consider that all feelings are *anicca*. They are *saṅkhāras.*

- The Saṅgha is also a *saṅkhāra*. What you do in the Buddha’s teaching is to root out the cause of suffering. You don’t try to remove it from outside.

- What does not arise, does not pass away, that is the end of suffering. The Dhamma is used as a raft. You see allformations as *anicca*, then only you have *nibbidā* (disenchantment with worldly life) then you become distanced from all *saṅkhāras (formations),* then it is seen as “not mine,” “not I,” etc.

- When *lobha* (greed)*, dosa* (hate) *and moha* (delusion) are rooted out, suffering is rooted out.

- You will come to the final goal.

- 12 *Suttas* is enough, you mustn’t be loaded with theoretical knowledge.<Footnote id="25">“Twelve *Suttas* is enough” means depth over breadth. Take a small, well-chosen set, learn them thoroughly—read, memorize key lines, reflect, practice—so study (*pariyatti*) becomes practice (*paṭipatti*) and realization (*paṭivedha*). The point is to avoid theoretical overload, not to forbid wider study.</Footnote>
