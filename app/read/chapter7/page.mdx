# Chapter 07: “Stick to the Exact Words of the Buddha”

## Reflection 07: Final Lessons on Detachment

Even as illness and age pressed upon him, Ven. Ñāṇavimala remained unmoved, like a mountain, standing firm against wind and storm. Whether in the solitude of forests or the sterility of hospital corridors, his practice did not concern itself with altering the outer world but with a steady, inward response to it through reflection and equanimity.

From early on in his monk’s life at Polgasduwa Island Hermitage, this became evident. At the time, as the hermitage’s disciplined character began to change, attracting a different kind of monastic and lay visitor, many of the senior monks felt it was no longer conducive to their practice and departed one by one. Ven. Ñāṇavimala remained.

“For *karmic* reasons,” he once said, “we receive a particular place and teacher. To remain there also offers support for the rest.” He held that leaving should not be done hastily, but in consultation with the Saṅgha. He stayed for a decade.

When he finally began his wandering life (*cārikā*), it was not from dissatisfaction, but as a natural unfolding of the path.

After those long years of wandering, his health began to decline. The toll of *cārikā* and advancing age showed themselves most markedly after he returned first to Siri Vajirarama Temple and then to Polgasduwa Hermitage. There, severe prolonged diarrhea rapidly diminished his weight and vitality.

Characteristically, he made nothing of his condition, maintaining his daily practice of receiving alms at the *dāna sālā*<Footnote id="1">Dāna sālā is the hall where monks gather to eat the single meal of the day, which has been provided by lay supporters.</Footnote> and, when special requests were made, giving Dhamma advice or blessings. To outside eyes, this decline of physical condition was a time of hardship; but Shanaka sensed something altogether different—a palpable dispassion, and a detachment from the body that preserved his radiance even as his strength faded.

Ven. Gnanaloka described one such occasion, when he was still a layman at Polgasduwa, when Ven. Ñāṇavimala first lost consciousness:

“On that particular day I was washing items in the *dāna sālā* when alarmed attendants and monks rushed to tell me of Bhante’s condition. I found him unresponsive, his head drooping and both hands composedly clasped in his lap.

Yet remarkably his face retained its moonlike radiance—that glow never dimmed, creating an unforgettable image. All present were hesitant to intervene, as he had never before allowed anyone to assist him in such an intimate manner.”

Shanaka then gently laid Ven. Ñāṇavimala's body down flat.

“I reached for Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s belt, quickly pulled out the keys, and ran to his kuṭi. I found the medicinal honey he kept and ran back with it. Placing it on his tongue, I remember being struck by the radiance that still seemed to emanate from his unconscious body. I could not tell whether it was the honey, the passage of time, or something else, but suddenly he regained consciousness and spoke the following in Pāli:

‘*Vayadhammā saṅkhārā… uppādavayadhammino; uppajjitvā nirujjhanti, tesaṁ vūpasamo sukho*. (All conditioned things are impermanent … they are of the nature to arise and pass away; having arisen, they cease. Their stilling is bliss.)’<Footnote id="2">This utterance stems from *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days)—the Buddha's final words—combined with Sakka's verse from the *Parinibbāna Sutta* (SN6.15; final Nibbāna)</Footnote>

‘*Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā ti yadā paññāya passati, atha nibbindati dukkhe esa maggo visuddhiyā*. (When one perceives with wisdom that all conditioned things are impermanent, then one turns away from suffering. This is the path of purification.)’<Footnote id="3">*Dhammapada* (Dhp 277)</Footnote>

Then he said, ‘This foul body, just lift this up.’

At that moment, I realized that his consciousness had faded while reflecting on these verses, and it was those same reflections that brought him back. This was my first time physically assisting him. It was a moment of mixed emotions—on one hand bearing witness to the helpless nature of the human body of someone so remarkable, but on the other a most joyous experience at the rare privilege of being by his side during this time. It was both the saddest and happiest day of my life.”

Doctors would periodically come and check up on Ven. Ñāṇavimala at the hermitage, and would often leave with a sense of admiration. Once, a new doctor came to perform a medical check-up. Unfamiliar with Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s character and perhaps overzealous to treat his declining health, the visiting doctor was met with a single remark:

> “There is no medicine for old age.”

While his mind met old age with such clarity, his body’s decline soon became undeniable. Worn from years of austere wandering, his body now demanded a level of care he had long resisted. Yet even here his practice revealed itself, not as resistance to aging and sickness, but as a direct meeting of them.

In line with his austerity, he chose to be admitted to a government hospital and whilst there he remained the same: inwardly composed, outwardly simple. His preference for public over private care was not incidental; it reflected his lifelong consistency. For one who had rejected comforts, a hospital ward was no different from a kuṭi or a forest glade; it was merely another setting for practice.

Shanaka, who now became one of his close attendants, visited regularly, and Ven. Ñāṇavimala would sense his presence before even seeing him. Recognising the sound of his footsteps in the corridor, he would softly call out, “Shanaka, Shanaka,” to Venerable Mettavihārī, who was the other close attendant there.

Sometimes Shanaka slept on the floor beside the hospital bed, keeping a watchful eye over his teacher.

Ven. Gnanaloka recalls those hospital days vividly:

“I possessed great faith but little experience in caring for anyone. Yet I remained present, offering a kind of protection that I could.”

Even during these times, Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s independence was apparent. He had once warned Shanaka: “Don't be in the hands of the devotees.”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala was concerned about what he saw happening: how temples and monasteries were often beholden to devotees, doing things primarily to please them. Especially if they held a high standing in society, he saw monastics would go out of their way to maintain close relationships, at the expense of their practice.

He thus cautioned Shanaka against becoming *Āmisa-garu*—weighed down by material gains. It is when a practitioner begins to honor the worldly offerings—comfortable lodgings, rich offerings and the praise of followers—above the internal work.

This speaks to the balance of monastic life. The Buddha intentionally established the monastic order so that monks were not self-sufficient. By forbidding them from growing food, picking fruit from trees, or handling money, he made them reliant not just on the lay community, but on faith itself. This physical dependence for material needs—food, robes, shelter, and medicine—served to continually remind monks of their non-ownership. Survival depends entirely on the kindness of others.

Yet, alongside this dependence, Ven. Ñāṇavimala showed by example an inward autonomy. While the body relied on the world for its very life, the mind had to remain rooted in the Dhamma rather than the favor or opinions of others. This was *Dhamma-garu*—placing the weight of respect only upon the truth.

He warned that the Buddha described how the *sāsana* (Buddha’s dispensation) decays from within when the mind loses its edge, when worldly benefits become the priority,<Footnote id="4">Refer to *Dhammadāyāda Sutta* (MN3; heirs in Dhamma), *Sīha Sutta* (AN5.99; the lion), *Tatiyaanāgatabhaya Sutta* (AN5.79; future perils, third), *Catutthaanāgatabhaya Sutta* (AN5.80; future perils, fourth)</Footnote> and the path to liberation is traded for a comfortable cage. Through watching him live, Shanaka took to heart the weight of this warning.

After being hospitalized, Ven. Ñāṇavimala accepted assistance, but never gave favor to any attendant. More often, seeing there was another attendant available, he would instruct Shanaka to return to his practice and give the other attendant an opportunity to earn good merits. The lesson was subtle but unmistakable: service too must not become a form of clinging.

Ven. Gnanaloka later reflected: “There was no difference between the first day I met him and the last day I met him.” Outwardly he could be close and kind, but inwardly he maintained a steady dispassion, balanced with a universal compassion that never leaned into intimacy.

The steady discipline carried into his daily routine. Even within hospital walls, he seemed to erase the distinction between day and night. Ven. Gnanaloka recalls how at 2 a.m., he would engage in walking meditation in the hospital corridors, quietly pacing every two hours. Even when lying down, there seemed to be a watchful awareness that carried with him.

One day Shanaka was doing walking meditation on the balcony of the hospital ward, as Ven. Ñāṇavimala watched. When Shanaka returned, Ven. Ñāṇavimala asked him:

“Did you meet your friend… your thoughts?”

Then, after a pause, he added with a soft smile,

“Don’t overdo it. Take some rest.”

It was how he spoke of wandering thoughts, those passing visitors of the mind. He had once advised Shanaka not to forcibly chase them away, but to meet them gently as if they were a guest, and let them pass on their own. Then the underlying tendency to aversion finds no ground to grow.

What stayed with Shanaka were moments like these—the peculiar, but illuminating way of teaching hidden in simple exchanges.

As his teacher’s health declined, those small exchanges took on a deeper tone. Ven. Ñāṇavimala later needed to stay at Siri Vajirarama Temple as it was easier to access medicines and appropriate care. During this period, one incident left a lasting imprint on Ven. Gnanaloka.

He recalls entering Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s room and offering to help adjust his body in bed. He replied with an uncharacteristically abrupt “No!”

Then, after a pause, he asked, “Did anything touch you?”

Shanaka was confused until he realized that stool had involuntarily passed from Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s frail body. The moment this was understood, Ven. Ñāṇavimala's response was piercing:

> “Whether it’s inside or outside, it doesn’t make a difference.”

The body, the Buddha taught, is just elements (*dhātu*)—solidity (*pathavī*), fluidity (*āpo*), heat (*tejo*), and motion (*vāyo*). Inside, outside. All the same. Nothing to claim as ‘mine.’ Just nature unfolding.

Ven. Gnanaloka recalls “he was looking at his body with that reality, really meaning it. You could feel it.” At first, Ven. Ñāṇavimala declined Shanaka’s offer of help, stating he would wait for the other attendants. But when Shanaka remained quietly beside him, he consented.

What stood out through the years was not hardship, but the steadiness of a life wholly given to the Dhamma. In old age, pain often makes people irritable, understandably so. Yet even facing severe hardship, he never complained to his attendants.

Only once did Shanaka see that his body was overwhelmed. For a couple of hours, soft sounds escaped—the low groaning “er” of physical pain, the body expressing what the mind seemed to not do. Alarmed, Shanaka rushed to his side, fearing he may be close to dying.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala's response was: “Don't worry. Why are you worried? Everything has to go. We will all have to pass away.”

Since the first day he got a chance to physically assist Ven. Ñāṇavimala at Polgasduwa, a thought remained with Ven. Gnanaloka: a single day of his teacher’s existence on earth offered more worth than the entirety of our lifetimes, for his presence was a rare light that shone upon both the Buddha’s Dispensation (*sāsana*) and the world. How fortunate to encounter one who walked the spiritual path with uncompromising focus and complete dedication. While many pursue this spiritual journey with admirable knowledge and sincerity, Ven. Ñāṇavimala stood apart—a life that was not an interpretation of the Dhamma, but its living expression.

### 29 February 1996

- *Majjhima Nikāya*: *Cūḷagosinga Sutta* (MN31; the shorter discourse in Gosinga); *Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta* (MN28; the greater discourse on the simile of the elephant’s footprint); *Puṇṇovāda Sutta* (MN145; advice to Puṇṇa)—These three are connected.
- In *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness), soon after *Ānāpānasati* comes the repulsiveness of the body. To look at it as it actually is, when walking, standing etc., in all positions. So that one tears away from the *kāmaloka* (the sensual realm) altogether. When you consider that, turning away happens naturally.
- Slowly begin to practice *Majjhima* 117—*Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty) that leads to *jhāna* and then *Saṁyutta* SN22; SN50–SN55 to attain *sotāpanna*, if you continue to practice like this, at latest, at the moment of death, one will attain the path.<Footnote id="8">This instruction summarises a progressive path of practice based on canonicalsources. The *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty) outlines the complete structure of the Noble Eightfold Path leading towards concentration (*jhāna*). The *Khandha Saṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) provides insight into the five aggregates as subjects of clinging, supporting insight into non-self. The following collections—from SN50 through SN55—describe successive stages of mental development: the cultivation of the five spiritual powers (*Balasaṁyutta*, SN50), the bases of spiritual power (*Iddhipādasaṁyutta*, SN51), mindfulness meditation and its fruits (*Anuruddhasaṁyutta*, SN52), the absorptions (*Jhānasaṁyutta*, SN53), mindfulness of breathing (*Ānāpānasaṁyutta*, SN54), and finally, the qualities leading to stream-entry (*Sotāpattisaṁyutta*, SN55). Together, these provide a gradual training that culminates in the attainment of stream-entry (*sotāpatti*).</Footnote>
- Ultimately, all this practice should lead to *kālaṁ āgameyya*.<Footnote id="9">The phrase *kālaṁ āgameyya* literally means “to await one’s time (of death).” In the context of practice, it signifies a state of inner preparedness and equanimity toward death—a serene acceptance that arises when craving and clinging have been weakened. It does not imply passivity or resignation, but rather the culmination of wisdom (*paññā*) and detachment, where the practitioner lives mindfully, ready to face death without fear or regret.</Footnote>
- *Rāhulovāda Sutta* (and reflect before sleeping to attain *jhāna* through *mettā bhāvanā*).<Footnote id="10">Ven. Gnanaloka elaborates: *Rāhulovāda Sutta* aka *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā)—the three ways of conduct—this continuous self-reflection should be done the whole day, using it at night to help reflect on what you have done—from body, words and thoughts. Then determine the next day also not to do it, focusing on how to avoid that. When you are reflecting like this, one can get into a *samādhi*, and then proceed to attain *jhāna* (or meditative concentration) through *mettā*. Doing this before sleep, pushing to the point until one knows when the body needs sleep, then allows the body to naturally fall asleep. This kind of sleep reduces your subconscious inclinations and *kamma*.</Footnote>
- Regarding *Mātugāma*, in *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days).<Footnote id="11">This refers to the Buddha’s counsel to Ven. Ānanda in the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days) regarding *mātugāma* (women): do not see; if seen, do not speak; if speech is required, maintain full mindfulness. It is a renunciant’s discipline of sense restraint—a practical safeguard against sensual desire and a protection for concentration—not a blanket moral judgment.</Footnote>
- The notes you take from Venerable Mettavihārī won’t be applicable at present but will be useful and available to use at a later period when required.
- It is good to make note of them and repeat them so that one doesn’t forget them.
- **You won’t be able to take the ten precepts when you are a layman, but you can take eight precepts (*aṭṭhaṅgika uposatha sīla*) and the *ājīvaṭṭhamaka sīla* (eight precepts focused on right speech and right livelihood).**<Footnote id="12">The five precepts are the basic moral code for daily conduct. The eight precepts (*aṭṭhaṅgika uposatha sīla*) represent a higher spiritual discipline, requiring celibacy, abstinence from food after noon, and the avoidance of adornment, entertainment, and luxurious beds.</Footnote>

### 28 March 1996

- Greek mythology originates from Hinduism. They had contact with the East. e.g. China and India, etc. So most of the myths are [influenced] from Hindu[ism].
- Even Christ had beginnings from the East. Though he could not fully grasp the Dhamma as he had a Jewish background [rooted in Abrahamic beliefs in a Creator-God]. He was highly saintly, though he allowed drinking wine and killing animals.<Footnote id="15">Ven. Ñāṇavimala noted that the Three Wise Men (Magi), said to come from the East, were spiritual sages (rishis) who recognized Christ as a “spiritual king.” This exchange, facilitated by the ancient Silk Route, suggested that established Eastern ethical principles—non-violence and compassion—helped shape early Christian ethics. The main point is that these core ethics had Eastern resonances, even if later religious practices diverged from the strict morality (*sīla*) of the Buddhist path. Doctrinally, however, Abrahamic metaphysics—a Creator-God and an eternal soul—is fundamentally incompatible with the Dhamma’s principles of non-self (*anatta*) and dependent origination (*paṭiccasamuppāda*).</Footnote>
- When old inclinations appear, one must deal with them tactfully. It is impossible to fight them.
- The one who captured Troy, he had to cross a stretch of ocean. People warned him telling him that on the one side there were abysses and only death could come if you turn to that side. And on the other side there are nymphs alluring, but they are flesh eating. So he told his fellow sailors to cover their ears with wax and to tie him to the mast and if he tries to loosen himself, then to tighten further. This was to be done until that stretch was passed. In the case of the bhikkhu, it is the practice of Satipaṭṭhāna.<Footnote id="16">This is a reference to the journey of the Greek hero Odysseus in Homer’s The Odyssey (Book XII). Ven. Ñāṇavimala uses it to convey a powerful Dhamma teaching: Shanaka’s note fuses two separate threats to create a single simile for sensual craving (*kāma-sukha*): the Sirens (who used their alluring songs to tempt sailors) and the monstrous, flesh-eating Scylla (who caused literal death). Odysseus’s instruction to be tied to the mast and use ear wax represents the fierce determination (*adhiṭṭhāna*) and disciplined sense-restraint (*indriya saṁvara sīla*) required to survive the temptation of the pleasures and avoid their resulting dangers (*ādīnava*).</Footnote>
- **The other is how the cowherd beat the cows when they ate the harvest. When they decided to eat again, they recalled the painful experience. So, they never went there—[*Vīṇopama Sutta* (SN35.246; the simile of the lute)]. Similarly, you should think of *kāma ādīnava* (the drawbacks of sensuality) and *nekkhamma ānisaṁsa* (the advantages of renunciation).**
- *Satipaṭṭhāna*—the *asubha* (of the *kāmaloka*) and then *Ānāpānasati*.
- MN107, 117—How to attain *jhāna*—*Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna); *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty) + *Saṁyutta* 22 to 55—*Khandha Saṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) to *Sotāpattisaṁyutta* (SN55; connected discourses on stream-entry).
- Marvels of the ocean—*Pahārāda Sutta* (AN8.19; Pahārāda). How to be a true representative of the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha.
- You should [skillfully] accommodate yourself to the place and situation to create harmony.
- Saṅgha is also a formation. It lasted 2500 years, the Buddha foresaw the decline of the *sāsana*. That is why Vinaya was brought as an outside formation to support the longevity of the *sāsana*. In the beginning the bhikkhus were allowed to live as they wish, as most of them used to attain arahantship as soon as they entered [the order].

1. *Itivuttaka* (along the stream what one meets, the dangers)—[*Nadīsota Sutta* (Iti109; a river)]
2. *Dhammapada* (Dhp; verses of the Dhamma)

- In the night, try to sleep with a fresh, peaceful mind so that one will avoid unwholesome impressions throughout the night. And it will be highly *puñña* (meritorious) supporting one’s future.

### 30 March 1996

- As I see it I think you should attend the classes and pass the examination at this point.
- I hope you will attain, in the evening, *mettā cetovimutti* [liberation of mind through loving-kindness]. It will help you. It is a merit for future happiness.
- If people arise in a lower world, [they] will miss Metteyya Buddha also.<Footnote id="17">This likely refers to the tendency of some Buddhists to delay or postpone effort in meditation, believing they will attain liberation under the future Buddha, Metteyya (Maitreya). Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s point highlights the danger of such complacency—those reborn in lower realms will miss that opportunity entirely.</Footnote>
- Marvels of the ocean—*Uposatha Sutta* (Ud5.5; observance day) e.g. [even] at the cost of life, do not transgress a training rule laid down by the Buddha—*Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā) and to attain path *Saṁyutta* 22 to 55.

### 3 April 1996

- Majjhima 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10—*Anangaṇa Sutta* (MN5; without blemishes); *Ākankheyya Sutta* (MN6; if a bhikkhu should wish); *Vatthūpama Sutta* (MN7; the simile of the cloth); *Sallekha Sutta* (MN8; effacement); *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta*(MN9; right view); *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness).
- *Mahānāma Suttas* (as a layman this is the base, and the practice should be the *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases)). As a bhikkhu MN107, 117, 125—*Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna); *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty); *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed) and then *Khandha* and *Saḷāyatana*, then you are *gotrabhū* [who has entered the lineage of the Noble Ones]—then one is fit to take the *dāna* [offering given by people].

### 4 April 1996

- Majjhima 28—*Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta* (MN28; the greater discourse on the simile of the elephant’s footprint).
- Majjhima 122—*Mahāsuññata Sutta* (MN122; the greater discourse on voidness).
- Don’t do [any] work that is indirectly not connected to the main practice, in a way that you would say, ‘after that work is finished, then I will start reflecting on the *Dhamma*.’ Know [that the practice] should happen then also. Because if we die at that time we will arise according to that nature of things. So the practice should be always directed to *sampajañña* [clear comprehension], and then to *sampajañña* [clear comprehension], and attaining the path.
- You may not live [with] any kind of expectation etc., that is an illusion, when you are practicing the true Dhamma there is no disappointment.
- *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā), *Saṁyutta* 22—*Khandha* and *Saḷāyatana* and *kālaṁ āgameyya*, you may stick to a couple of *Suttas* and practice them. It is more than enough.
- The determination to help all beings which you have decided upon, you may go with that—State of Mind.

### 9 April 1996

- *Suttas* MN5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10—*Anangaṇa Sutta* (MN5; without blemishes); *Ākankheyya Sutta* (MN6; if a bhikkhu should wish); *Vatthūpama Sutta* (MN7; the simile of the cloth); *Sallekha Sutta* (MN8; effacement); *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta*(MN9; right view); *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness). Go through them slowly and pick the ones that are conspicuous at the moment. Deal with them individually. When they are overcome you would notice the others, then you can set about them as before.
- Now the noble eightfold path has to be slowly cultivated. Then there is *sammā samādhi*, *sammā ñāṇa* and finally *sammā vimutti*, then you will know how to get about (Majjhima 64)—*Mahāmālunkya Sutta* (MN64; the greater discourse to Mālunkyāputta).<Footnote id="18">This progression refers to the tenfold path of a “noble one,” which extends beyond the standard Noble Eightfold Path. While the eighth factor is *sammā samādhi* (Right Concentration), it is followed by *sammā ñāṇa* (Right Knowledge) and *sammā vimutti* (Right Liberation), which together represent the final insight and complete freedom of an enlightened being.</Footnote>
- Know only a little experience will come initially in *mettā bhāvanā* (loving-kindness meditation), but as you go on, the experience will be strengthened and it will be a more complete experience.
- You may give your foreign friends and other newcomers, in the beginning, *Kesamutti Sutta* (AN3.65; Kesaputtiya), and maybe *Dhammapada*, seeing their response; and you may find suitable ways and means to deal with them. For ones who are a little advanced, the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days).
- As time goes on you will get the ability to help others with a more pure state of mind, and then you will know what is appropriate for them and how to use your thoughts.
- You don’t have to write a big letter, you can just write “best wishes” only. [regarding writing to parents and friends]
- Ultimately, one must prepare oneself for *kālaṁ āgameyya* [waiting for one’s time/death]. For that, you slowly reduce the feelings, making them finer. Because after all, it is nothing but feelings, perception, consciousness, *pañcupādānakkhandha* (five aggregates subject to clinging).

### 12 April 1996

- You would find nothing from outside that is more helpful than what you have already inside, in the form of understanding etc. (This is in relation to following the Dhamma’s way of life).
- It is sad to say that the essence of the practice is almost lost everywhere. People are getting more used to living a comfortable life.
- *Majjhima* 135—*Cūḷakammavibhanga Sutta* (MN135; the shorter exposition of action); To Gotamī [*Dakkhiṇāvibhanga Sutta* (MN142; the exposition of offerings)].

### 14 April 1996

- *Suttas* MN5, 6, 7 etc.—*Anangaṇa Sutta* (MN5; without blemishes); *Ākankheyya Sutta* (MN6; if a bhikkhu should wish); *Vatthūpama Sutta* (MN7; the simile of the cloth) etc. in Majjhima Nikāya, the *kilesas* are mentioned in detail.
- [In] any situation, keep the inside calm, never be disturbed, this is the very practice of the Dhamma.
- The body is not the Arahant, it is unthinkable.<Footnote id="19">Ven. Gnanaloka explains what Ven. Ñāṇavimala means: People will try to determine whether someone has attained arahantship by observing the body—appearance or physical and verbal actions. But arahantship has not occurred in the body; it has occurred with regard to consciousness. To perceive this, one must at minimum be able to perceive another's consciousness, which is not possible for one without such capability. Additionally, one must know what insights have arisen. For one who has not destroyed defilements fully, it is thus “unthinkable.” People interpret according to the level of their own defilements.</Footnote>
- We must go in stages. First raise the mind to higher states or stages from the grosser level with all its gross defilements—that is the realm of *devas*. In that state there is not this kind of [physical] body. But one must know that all formations [even heavenly ones] are *anicca, dukkha, anatta*. So while in this human state one can do the highest reflection and already transcend the whole situation of forming byattaining the path, etc.
- Never let the mind ponder and go back to past events etc. Try to bring it to the present, and that is *kamma*.<Footnote id="20">Ven. Ñāṇavimala explained that the deliberate effort to bring the mind to the present moment, and even the purposeful avoidance of mental distractions, constitutes new wholesome *kamma* (*kusala kamma*). This intentional redirection of energy in the present moment is a powerful action that will bear positive results, strengthening the path.</Footnote>
- Always from *āmisa* to *nirāmisa sukha*.<Footnote id="21">*Āmisa sukha* is “worldly happiness,” the fleeting pleasure that comes from satisfying the five senses. *Nirāmisa sukha* is “spiritual happiness,” the more profound and stable joy that arises from renunciation, meditation, and wisdom.</Footnote> Never the other way.
- Must never neglect to increase and grow the Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, *sati* [mindful recollection of the Triple Gem] or *saddhā* [faith and confidence in them].
- What to speak etc. in Sutta 122 in Majjhima—*Mahāsuññata Sutta* (MN122; the greater discourse on voidness).
- Always overcoming the *nīvaraṇas* [five mental hindrances] and developing the *indriyas* [five spiritual faculties]. *Saṁyutta* 22 to 55 etc. This is done in stages first fulfilling the noble eightfold path.
