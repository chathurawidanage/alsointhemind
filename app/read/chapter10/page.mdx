# Chapter 10: “Find a Way to Bring Many to This Path”

## Reflection 10: A Seed Foreseen, a Path Unfolding

In the quiet seclusion of Polgasduwa Island Hermitage, Ven. Ñāṇavimala spent over a decade immersed in disciplined training under the guidance of eminent monastic scholars—Ven. Nyanatiloka, Ven. Ñāṇaponika, and Ven. Ñāṇamoli. These were monks who didn’t just study the Pāli Canon, they carried it in their blood. And among the original teachings of the Buddha, Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s roots of renunciation took hold.

But the Island Hermitage was only a beginning. After eleven years, Ven. Ñāṇavimala stepped beyond the boundaries of monastery walls and began a life of ascetic wandering, lived entirely by the Dhamma’s rhythm. Those who encountered him felt a life lived so simply that its very presence made the Dhamma visible.

Among the many who watched and listened was the young Shanaka, who had been drawn to meditation from a young age. Reserved by nature, he lived as a recluse even before his ordination. He rarely engaged in small talk, even among fellow monastics, and had no aspiration for recognition or teaching others.

Yet his teacher saw otherwise.

As a novice, Ven. Gnanaloka was once told, unexpectedly, “Your devotees are waiting for you.” At the time, it sounded more poetic than prophetic. Still, over the years, Ven. Ñāṇavimala would prepare him, not with formal instruction, but with passing remarks that hinted at something more to come.

“When you happen to discourse to others,” he once said, “you must discourse to them from their framework or language. Your idea or Dhamma ideas—it will create an impression.”

Then, in 2001, what had once been subtle suggestions crystallized into a clear mission from his teacher: “You must find a way to bring many to this path. Create separate *cārikā piṇḍapātika bhikkhus*.”

It was a rare and weighty instruction—especially for one who had spent his life resisting social contact, avoiding even casual interactions with other monks. But the words were delivered without pressure, as if Ven. Ñāṇavimala knew the moment would come by itself.

That moment came years later, not in a monastery, but high in the Himalayas.

In 2015, while attempting a high-altitude ascent near Mount Kailash,<Footnote id="1">Mount Kailash, located in Tibet, is considered one of the most sacred mountains in the world, revered by Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and adherents of the Bön religion.</Footnote> Ven. Gnanaloka faced sudden, life-threatening altitude sickness. With no access to oxygen or proper medical help, he began gasping for breath. Each breath brought less air. The body began to fail. Natural instinct was to panic, but his training had prepared him for this. He turned inward, not to fight death, but to meet it. In that surrender, a stillness came.

And in that space between life and death, a troubling thought arose:

*I couldn’t share enough with the people.*

*Can’t help it, next life I’ll have to.*

This tension was magnified by his private aspiration: he had vowed to share the Dhamma he had received only after no trace of greed, hate or delusion was left in his heart.

He realized then that sharing need not wait for perfection. One doesn’t need to master the entire ocean to offer a cup of water. That realization drew him out of his long seclusion. The vow made in silence at his teacher’s stūpa had found its hour.

It wasn’t a desire to teach, but a sense of duty that emerged. Slowly, almost reluctantly, he began to guide others—offering retreats, sharing insights, and encouraging those ready to walk the path. In time, he even began to support others in renunciation, nurturing a new generation of monastics inspired by the same wandering spirit.

Though his own instincts had always leaned toward quietude, it was clear the turning point had been foreseen. Not by him, but by the one who had trained him.

Looking back now, the threads are unmistakable. From cryptic remarks to direct instructions, Ven. Ñāṇavimala had not only planted the seed but somehow glimpsed its flowering.

“Actually my focus is on the Buddha,” Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies.

He was not trying to fulfill anyone’s expectations or carry out a plan. He was simply practicing day by day, moment to moment, living the Dhamma.

“Bhante made it alive for me,” he reflects. “I would never have known from the *Suttas* what the Buddha is, if it wasn’t for his guidance.” Not a memory, but a living practice through which his teacher’s presence remains. “He’s not dead for me, if I do these things,” he adds.

He sees the transformation in his own life as no accident. It had taken shape long before anyone else noticed.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala did not speak of destiny. His way was to place the right word in the heart of one who was ready and then wait, patiently, as life brought it to fruition.

### 14 July 2001

- [*Personal Note:*] All blessings for creating the opportunity for many to follow the Noble path. (Regarding a separate *sīmā* and the safeguarding of purity of the Saṅgha).<Footnote id="2">Ven. Gnanaloka Thera expressed this statement of gratitude to his teacher, Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera, on the day the new *sīmā* was opened at Parappaduwa Island Hermitage. A *sīmā* is the legally defined boundary within which the Bhikkhu Saṅgha (Monastic Community) conducts formal communal acts—such as higher ordination (*upasampadā*), the closing of the rains-retreat, disciplinary procedures, and the annual robe-offering ceremony (*kaṭhina*) to safeguard procedural purity.</Footnote>

- To do everything with the advice, blessings and approval of [Ven. Madihe Paññāsīha] Mahānāyaka Thera.

- Create separate *cārikā* bhikkhus.<Footnote id="3">Ven. Ñāṇavimala encouraged the establishment of such monks to preserve the ancient forest tradition of renunciation and simplicity. Interestingly, this advice was given to a young monk—Ven. Gnanaloka—who had been in robes for only two years at the time.</Footnote>

- Many will get an impression in the future from what you do in the morning (*piṇḍapāta*) and some will want to join.

- *Kasibhāradvāja Sutta* (SN7.11; Kasi Bhāradvāja) applies especially to you: when you happen to discourse to others, you must discourse to them from [within] their [own] framework or language, your idea or Dhamma ideas it will create an impression.

- You must find a way to bring many to this path.

- Create separate *cārikā* *piṇḍapāta* bhikkhus.

- (Bhikkhus in mind) recruit young ones from the age 20 who are especially coming from good families, who can make a determination on the essential goal.
