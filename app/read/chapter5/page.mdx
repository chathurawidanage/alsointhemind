# Chapter 05: “Always Be with Thoughts of Mettā”

## Reflection 05: Soft like Butter, Hard like Diamond

Ven. Ñāṇavimala embodied an infinite gentleness of spirit held by an unbreakable will. To some, he appeared as a saintly monk, radiating serene benevolence; to others, he was a fierce ascetic whose unyielding determination allowed no compromise on the path to liberation. As Ven. Gnanaloka would later reflect, his teacher was “intense in the Dhamma,” yet that intensity was always expressed through a soft, warm smile. It was a harmony of extremes captured perfectly by the way he would describe him: “soft like butter, hard like diamond.”

He did not shape his teachings to please, but to awaken. Sometimes his words were tender, at other times sharp. Sometimes his silence carried more weight than speech. Yet always they were directed precisely to the person before him. Whoever came to him was urged to walk with urgency, as though time were slipping away. For many, this intensity was too much to bear. Drawn by the aura of a saint, they often found themselves unsettled by the fierceness of his path. With Ven. Ñāṇavimala, there was no idle talk, no wasted time. It was practice, practice, and more practice.

A senior monk who had completed over ten Rains Retreats once came to pay respects. Despite his seniority, Ven. Ñāṇavimala treated him almost as a novice, advising that he should remain under the guidance of a teacher until maturity was reached. By then he knew not to question his teacher’s judgement, but he still felt a little awkward.

Yet as they left the kuṭi, the monk turned to Ven. Gnanaloka and remarked, “He knows everything about me.”

A precise instruction, spoken exactly as it was needed.

At other times, the lesson came without words. On one occasion, a doctor was brought by lay supporters who thought he might be of help. Ven. Ñāṇavimala was on his bed, in Shanaka’s line of sight, with a Buddha image on the wall to the side. As if thinking Ven. Ñāṇavimala was just a frail old man with little awareness, the doctor addressed his words to Ven. Mettavihārī, one of the attendants, rather than Ven. Ñāṇavimala himself.

At one point he made a direct remark to Ven. Ñāṇavimala: “Everyone thinks you’re an arahant, but I don’t consider like that.”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s response was not in words. He remained in silence, his neutral gaze resting with a softness, a trace of a smile upon his face.

Nothing in him shifted. No trace of irritation, no hint of pride.

For Shanaka, however, it was very different. Hearing his teacher spoken of so carelessly stirred a surge of anger.

“I wanted to throw him out of the kuṭi,” he later admitted.

To be near Ven. Ñāṇavimala was often to feel as though one had stepped into a kind of force field—a space of stillness where the mind naturally became settled. But in that instant of anger, it was as if he had been thrown out of the force field. Before his eyes, Ven. Ñāṇavimala seemed to drift away, growing distant. The Buddha image on the wall beside him also appeared to recede, as though pulled back into the distance. Then clarity came like a vision, exposing what had arisen in his mind: a thought of anger, ill will; a mind inflamed. Where his own mind was inflamed, Ven. Ñāṇavimala sat unmoved by either praise or disrespect.

Even in matters of requisites, Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s attitude remained the same—gentle yet unbending. During his student years in Cyprus, Shanaka had worked for the first time in his life, laboring for seven days. After returning to Sri Lanka, he went to a shop with his earnings and selected the best robe, alms bowl, and other requisites he could find, preparing a complete *Aṭapirikara*—the traditional offering of a monk’s eight basic requisites. He brought it to his teacher with deep devotion, expecting him to accept it.

Smiling at the memory, Ven. Gnanaloka later recalled, “I can still picture myself standing before him in his room. When I offered the *Aṭapirikara*, he didn’t accept it!” To Shanaka’s determined insistence, his teacher’s response was: “Offer it to the Saṅgha.”

It was an answer Shanaka would hear many times. Though he was among Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s closest attendants, there was never special treatment. This was a level of asceticism that, even after decades of his own practice, Ven. Gnanaloka felt he could never match. “Even for a glass of water, he didn’t ask,” he reflects. His teacher seemed to exist in a state of perfect mindfulness. An unwavering attention infused his every action. “If he was not talking, he would be absorbed in even lifting a finger,” he recalls. The smallest gesture. The simplest word. Nothing, it seemed, was ever done heedlessly.

Yet this firmness was never coldness. On rare occasions, Ven. Ñāṇavimala would accept offerings, but always out of compassion, and seemingly from a deep karmic sensitivity. Once, a young mentally disturbed boy had come to meet him and offered him a simple packet of Nestomalt [powdered malted food drink]. Another time, some of Shanaka’s friends from India placed their offering before him: a dozen bananas, unceremoniously wrapped in newspaper. To the quiet amusement of the onlooking Shanaka, Ven. Ñāṇavimala accepted both without hesitation, seeming to know that for these individuals, such an opportunity might never arise again, that this act of merit carried a weight they could not otherwise gain. For them, his acceptance was an act of kindness. For others, his gentle refusals became lessons in detachment.

His responses were never predictable. He would usually dismiss requests for photographs. Shanaka’s grandmother learned this first-hand when Ven. Ñāṇavimala was on *piṇḍapāta* stopping by her house. When she asked to take a picture, his response was: “Why do you want to see this foul body? See the *Dhamma*.”

Yet on one occasion, when a foreign visitor who was due to leave Polgasduwa that day requested some photographs, to Shanaka’s surprise, he agreed. Then Ven. Ñāṇavimala turned toward Shanaka with a hint of amusement and said, “It’s not a movie.” The act of allowing, so different from his usual refusals, was a key insight for Shanaka. It was a glimpse into a compassion that was not bound by rigid consistency, but one that responded with wisdom to what was needed in the moment. Whether a sharp lesson in detachment or a simple act of kindness. As Ven. Gnanaloka later reflected: “Even for the smallest things, I would ask him what to do, because I knew the answer would always be different.”

As he reflected on these seemingly contradictory responses, Ven. Gnanaloka came to see that they were not inconsistencies, but the actions of a mind that had gone beyond the reaches of the ordinary mind. The source of this freedom was his way of seeing the world, a perspective Ven. Gnanaloka was given a glimpse of on another occasion. Once, Ven. Ñāṇavimala remarked that “life is just a dream,” and with those words, he laughed. For Ven. Gnanaloka, so accustomed to his quiet composure, the very quality of this rare laughter stood out. It was the kind of laughter of one who had seen beneath the thin surface.

For over sixteen years, Ven. Gnanaloka saw this interplay again and again. Ven. Ñāṇavimala never clung, never favoured, never softened the path to make it easier. Yet behind his firmness there was always compassion. To many, this intensity was too much, and they drifted away. But for those who stayed, the lessons were unforgettable.

### 14 April 1995

- *Sutta* No. 107—*Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna)—where, when doing other work one must also practice. Not just to always stay in seated meditation.
- When not well, keep the mind calm, practise *Buddhānussati* and make the mind happy. Calm the mind with a meditation object.
- Daily reflect on the three ways of conduct and, before sleeping, practice *mettā* *bhāvanā*. That is *kamma* which will give *vipāka* (change the situation).
- **Daily reflect on the 5 contemplations** (Maharagama Vath Pirith).<Footnote id="1">The five contemplations, as found in the *Abhiṇhapaccavekkhitabbaṭhāna Sutta* (AN5.57; themes), are five facts the Buddha taught for daily reflection: that one is subject to aging, illness, and death and has not gone beyond them; that one will be separated from all that is dear; and that one is the owner and heir of one’s own actions (*kamma*). This is also commonly found in Sinhala chanting texts, such as the Maharagama Pirith Book (the English equivalent being ‘The Mirror of the Dhamma’ compiled by Venerable Nārada Mahāthera and Venerable Kassapa Thera).</Footnote>
- First *Sīla* (three ways of conduct), Right View (Word of the Buddha), *Satipaṭṭhāna* (Overcoming the hindrances), *Jhāna*, *vipassanā*.
- When going on *caṅkamana* (walking meditation), if a worldly thought arises, consider it as “it is not mine.”
- This; three ways of conduct [*tīṇi sucaritāni*], is it difficult?<Footnote id="2">Ven. Ñāṇavimala stresses this reflection of bodily, verbal and mental actions, reflecting before, during and after, as a foundational practice that should be done always. Ven. Gnanaloka comments “I’ve noticed that those who have developed *samādhi*, they have that developed in their mind (also).” Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice about doing things “slowly, mindfully, carefully” goes with this.</Footnote>
### 15 April 1995

- Know the way you should try to attain *Jhāna—*(the way is described in *Suttas*; *Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna) and *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed) of *Majjhima Nikāya*).
- When meditating, certain pleasant, unpleasant or sensual thoughts connected to the past may arise. What is past is not important. The present is important.
- You can notice how moment to moment objects are always changing.
- The present moment or situation due to the past way of conduct is something we cannot brush aside, but we may act in such a manner that no greed, hate or delusion may arise and that is *kamma*.
- In the past for many births, we have depended on feelings for our happiness.
- Death is nothing but breaking up of this present body which never belonged to us and the mind getting a new base to get on.
- The art of living in the present; it takes time to get established in that; not by days or weeks, but years.
### 17 April 1995

- Control of talking and thinking, *Suttas—**Kāyagatāsati Sutta* (MN119; mindfulness of the body), *Mahāsuññata Sutta* (MN122; the greater discourse on voidness) and *Cūḷasuññata Sutta* (MN121; the shorter discourse on voidness) of [*Majjhima Nikāya*].
- Talk only on Dhamma, otherwise be quiet.
- Thinking and reasoning should stop a bit.
- No point knowing, preaching and so forth many *Suttas*. If one is not meditating, then one is not *Dhammavihāri—* [*Paṭhamadhammavihārī Sutta* (AN5.73; one who dwells in the Dhamma, first)].
- It is good to know many *Suttas* thoroughly and meanwhile meditate.
- There is no point looking for external support. The whole practice is dwindling away but make sure what is inside—the state of mind is kept undisturbed and pure.
- When you are old you have to depend on others, more bondages. Therefore, more suffering.
- In the library, you may find the *Suttas* in Pāli and an English translation. You may read both.
### 20 April 1995

- Whenever there are inclinations (in the sensual world) don’t try to fight them. It is impossible. But divert the mind to thoughts of Dhamma or recite certain verses.
- Watch the mind and keep it purified.
- Anyone who delights in feelings, delights in suffering.
- In the beginning read certain *Suttas*, but as you practice and go on you may read more accordingly. Don't just go [straight] to reading more *Suttas*, practice is important.
- Generally, a teacher should be one who has entered the path.
### 13 May 1995

- Start with Right View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*) [by] read[ing] the Word of the Buddha [by Venerable Nyanatiloka Mahāthera] etc.
- Tradition is so dangerous. That’s why you should stick to the *Suttas*.*<Footnote id="3">Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s emphasis here is on rejecting practices that deviate from the teachings found in the *Suttas*, such as rituals. He, however, cautioned against being openly critical, as noted on 21 April 1994 about the danger in doing that—“even what is left will disappear.” The best approach is not to encourage such practices.</Footnote>*
- When going on [*cārikā*—wandering on foot] as a Bhikkhu, you have to go on *piṇḍapāta* (alms-round). All the difficulty. When no illusion.
### 14 May 1995

- Read *Suttas*; *Sallekha Sutta* (MN8; effacement)*, Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta* (MN9; right view)*, Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty).
- When you see Paṭiccasamuppāda (Dependent Origination) you see *Dhamma*.
- *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).
- Read *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).
- *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases) is an expansion of *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) through the six sense doors. The *Suttas* and so on are similar.
- **You may talk on the Dhamma to whoever shows interest, but you must do this with your example. You may do with means that are suitable to them tactfully.**
- **Whether someone has entered the path and how far he has progressed one cannot say. Only an Arahant (Fully Enlightened Being) knows for certain when he comes to that (that is his arahantship). This sort of questioning is meaningless.**
- **You must look after your welfare first. This is done by *Satipaṭṭhāna* (foundation of mindfulness).**
- **Our teacher is the All Enlightened One**
- Aṅguttara Nikāya 6.85—*Sītibhāva Sutta* (AN6.85; coolness)
- You must deal with the different *nīvaraṇā* [hindrances] with suitable objects of meditation.
- Reading of new *Suttas* should be done with practice.
- Working toward a certain worldly position etc. they are all to do with feelings. But all that ultimately comes to an end by old age and death.
- Always health is taken over by sickness, youth by old age and life by death.
- Sickness may come suddenly; we do not know what *kamma* we have done. The sickness of the body cannot be overcome but the sickness of the mind can be. It is very important to purify the mind from *lobha* (greed), *dosa* (hate), *moha* (delusion). The best conditions for recovery from physical sickness is *mettā* *bhāvanā*, high *puñña kamma* and *upekkhā* etc.
- Even if difficult situations come, you mustn’t let the mind get disturbed. [The] mind should abide... should abide blissfully every time.
### 15 May 1995

- All your efforts are not in vain. They will follow you like your shadow.
### 17 May 1995

- You must learn to accommodate with the place or environment to create harmony but not at the expense of *akusala kamma* (unwholesome actions). That you should know.
- There is happiness in married life and in renounced life. There is a difference. What is this married life? It is an illusion, an intoxication. It is due to not knowing things as they are. Take the example of Venerable Mahākassapa. How both stayed together and departed in separate ways. That is possible.<Footnote id="4">Venerable Mahākassapa and Venerable Bhaddā Kapilānī, both Arahants, were once husband and wife who lived in a celibate marriage and later renounced household life by mutual consent.</Footnote>
- Even if someone is kind and restrained, but it is an obstacle to stop this *saṁsāra* (the cycle of suffering and rebirth).
- Do your studies also properly. You may retire from time to time as you do now.<Footnote id="5">At that time Shanaka was pursuing his higher studies, and Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s comment “do your studies properly” is regarding that, seeing that Shanaka was perhaps neglecting this aspect.</Footnote>
- Simile of the sailor, very important, crossing the river [Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s own simile, detailed in the 27 April 1994 entry].
- You may feel this is right and that is right.
- You mustn’t enforce on others. But show with your example.
- Always [be] with thoughts of *mettā* etc.
### 18 May 1995

- Arahants look at all things with *upekkhā* (equanimity).
- Some arahants with *abhiññā* (supernormal powers) and the Buddha had the ability to see the attainments of others.
- Until you come to arahantship (full enlightenment) one doesn’t know. So it is good to have or continue to develop *saddhā*.
- You would not always have [quiet] suitable surroundings. I had it only twice in life.<Footnote id="6">Ven. Ñāṇavimala would also add that being in solitude is itself a result of *kamma*. In other words, do not attach to solitude or continuously long for it. Such periods arise and cease depending on conditions.</Footnote>
- You must make use of all situations to come to your goal. Obstacles should be made use of. You have to get involved in certain activities, that is unavoidable. Therefore, when you are engaged in such activity, direct your full concentration to that.
- Regarding the *Suttas*, certain things omitted by the Buddha means the person the Buddha talks to has already cultivated what is omitted. Sometimes things are omitted because certain preliminary things have to be cultivated [first].
- Your studies; they will also come to an end. It might come to an end suddenly.
- Though we have lived in a good way this life, we don’t know what we have done in the past births. So certain painful *kamma vipāka* (ripening of past actions), we should learn to bear it up with equanimity.
- *Mettā* *bhāvanā* and *Buddhānussati*.
- You must do *dāna*, *sīla* and *bhāvanā*. Never neglect *bhāvanā*. [Do it] daily.
- Due to past *kamma* certain urges may come. That you must take care [by not acting on those urges].
- Not letting the mind go to the future and the past, sticking to the presently arisen Dhammas with perfect knowledge.
### 19 May 1995

- *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases)—Very important.
- *Attadatthaṁ paratthena* (look after your welfare etc.)— Dhp 166.
- **His last words: Dhamma and the Vinaya is our teacher.**<Footnote id="7">Referring to the Buddha’s last words before his passing—*Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days).</Footnote>
- Always this path is against the stream. So, you should cut across like the sailor. You must sometimes accommodate, but direct it slowly to the goal.<Footnote id="8">“You must sometimes accommodate”—means one must learn to be flexible, sometimes it may seem like one is even going with the current, but slowly gradually directing it according to the conditions, without trying to cut across directly. In a practical real world sense, this may appear as they have to take a decision such as taking a job or duty in marriage, in order to fulfill a certain worldly responsibility. However even in the face of those decisions, one must gradually steer it tactfully towards the goal.</Footnote>
- Don’t get upset by any situation. Our *kamma vipāka* has to take place.
- Don’t have a fixed idea about the future or how you get about. So just get on peacefully moment to moment.
- All your efforts are not in vain.
- The highest way you can respect the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha is through the practice.
### 28 May 1995

- You may concentrate on the foundation—not thinking of *jhāna* etc. nor to be a scholar, but as the practice goes on you may refer further to *Suttas* and could categorize them under ‘*sīla*’ (virtue), ‘*samādhi*’ (concentration) and ‘*paññā*’ (wisdom).
- *Iṣṭa Devatā* [guardian celestial being] is there so you have nothing to worry.
- Studies: You may do your duty with maximum effort on your part, so that you would have no inner remorse or conflict concerning your duty, but the result should be expected with equanimity.
- You may learn Pāli [language] when you see the necessity for it.
- Certain verses you may recite and memorise in Pāli, [those] which appeal to you.
- Mirror of the Dhamma,<Footnote id="9">Recommended by Ven. Ñāṇavimala was ‘The Mirror of the Dhamma: A Manual of Buddhist Chanting and Devotional Texts’, compiled by Nārada Thera and Bhikkhu Kassapa. This English-language manual served a similar function to the traditional Sinhala Vath Pirith Potha, published by the Siri Vajiragnana Dharmayathanaya Temple at Maharagama.</Footnote> daily reflection on death etc.
- May you put brick by brick etc.
- Mustn’t be negligent and also mustn’t be too eager, but the middle way.
- Hope your effort and understanding will continue.
- Not letting the mind go to the future and the past, sticking to the presently arisen Dhammas with perfect insight as to its true nature of impermanence, suffering and therefore not mine. And at that time no *lobha*, *dosa* and *moha* (no craving, clinging —*paṭiccasamuppāda*), then the mind is directed to Nibbāna.
- **“*Nibbānaṁ paramaṁ sukhaṁ*” [“Nibbāna is the ultimate happiness/bliss”].**
### 26 June 1995

- *Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna), *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed), *Sekha Sutta* (MN53; the disciple in higher training), *Ariyapariyesanā Sutta* (MN26; the noble search).
- *Saṁyutta Nikāya, Khandhasaṁyutta* and *Saḷāyatanasaṁ- yutta—*some you may memorise.
- *Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (DN22; the greater discourse on the foundations of mindfulness).
- We are always going to either the past or the future. But if we stick to the present, you would then experience *anicca*. That which is *anicca* is *dukkha*. That which is *anicca* and *dukkha* is not mine.
- A bhikkhu has no attendant. When you go on *cārikā* you are quite alone. If you are not well, the hospital is there free of charge.
- *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).
- Don’t think of this personal services, but think of [the]highest form of merit.<Footnote id="10">Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice was directly related to Shanaka’s service to him during his final years. He taught that while physical assistance creates merit, the highest form of merit is gained through the disciple’s own sustained purification of mind in meditation.</Footnote>
- *Buddhānussati* (Recollection of the Buddha).
- *Maraṇasati* (Mindfulness of Death).
- *Asubha bhāvanā* (Contemplation of Impurity/Foulness) (already in *Satipaṭṭhāna*, when you are practicing there is no ignorance, no craving, no *lobha, dosa*, and *moha—greed, hatred, delusion*.).
- *Saṁyutta Nikāya* (Read *Khandha* and *Saḷāyatana Vagga*).
### 29 June 1995

- *Saṁyutta* 22 and 35 onwards—*Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).
- No *avijjā* no *saṅkhāra*, no *saṅkhāra* no *bhava*, no *jāti* no old age, no sickness, no death. What doesn’t arise doesn’t pass away.<Footnote id="11">This statement reflects the reverse sequence of *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination), showing the cessation aspect (*paṭiloma-paṭiccasamuppāda*). When *avijjā* (ignorance) ceases, *saṅkhārā* (volitional formations) cease; with the cessation of formations, there is no *bhava* (becoming), and consequently no *jāti* (birth), *jarā* (aging), *vyādhi* (sickness), or *maraṇa* (death). The phrase “What doesn’t arise doesn’t pass away” expresses the realization of nibbāna—the unconditioned state, free from arising and ceasing.</Footnote>
- **Read *Satipaṭṭhāna*. Practice it. The *nimittas* [mental signs/objects] are there for *samādhi* in *Satipaṭṭhāna*.**
- **Must attain *jhāna*. It is the suitable condition for entering the paths.**
- You may read *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN16; the great passing, the Buddha’s last days). Where it is mentioned, how you respect the Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha in the highest way, is only through the practice. The other thing is, though you hear that a certain teacher is famous or popular, compare it with the Dhamma and the Vinaya. So the Dhamma and the Vinaya is our teacher.
- *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā) is very important. After the *vandanā* [worship].
- Nobody can make another practice or attain. They can only show like the Buddha. They are only *Dhamma kāya*.<Footnote id="12">In the Theravāda tradition, *Dhamma kāya* means “embodiment of Dhamma.” Like the Buddha, even realized teachers cannot make another person practice or attain—they can only point out the way through the Dhamma they have realized.</Footnote>
- Read certain passages, verses and ones that appeal to you, memorise them or recall them.
### 8 July 1995

- Solitude is most necessary for the understanding to arise. As when there are distractions through the sense doors, the required concentration used to investigate the Dhamma is disturbed.
### 11 July 1995

- Formations will never be complete and perfect. So you must learn not to be dissatisfied, but to get on with inner peace and *upekkhā* [equanimity]. And that *upekkhā* too you should reflect its true nature. It is consciousness. It arises and passes too. Seeing it in this way you continue up until arahantship. This inner peace (*upekkhā*) you should see as it is, without delusion.
- **[Whether] I am there or not make sure you retire for the *poya*.**<Footnote id="13">Poya (full moon day) is a public holiday in Sri Lanka, and this instruction emphasizes dedicating the full day to intensified practice.</Footnote>
- **And before you sleep, make sure you reflect and do *mettā* *bhāvanā*, or in other words go to sleep with a raised or elevated mind.**
- **When you have attained the path, try your best to establish others who are first connected to you by birth, and more beings as possible.**
- **Make sure while you’re doing your studies that you retire from time to time.**
### 12 July 1995

- Face facts with confidence (in general in all situations).
