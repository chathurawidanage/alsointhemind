# Before It Was Too Late

## A Hundred-Fold Promise

> To tell you honestly, some interesting things happened to me,

Venerable Gnanaloka said, looking up from the pages.

> I was getting very strong emotions on occasions, you know. I was seeing... like... seeing myself on very key occasions, where I am... meeting Bhante.

He paused, searching for words.

> Which I don't recall like that.

By now we knew what he meant. For over two decades, Ven. Gnanaloka has practiced not holding onto memories. His teacher, Ven. Ñāṇavimala—whom he addressed as “Venerable Sir,” later “Bhante”—trained him in the art of living in the present. Memories, left unattended, don't arise, even memories of his own life.

But when he read the third draft of the Reflections in this book—stories of his teacher woven from multiple sources: his own notes from 25 years earlier, other accounts, his public talks, and our conversations with him—something broke through. Not just images, but *presence*. “Very key occasions,” returning with such clarity that he was seeing them again.

We asked him what he meant. What was it about these stories?

> I don't recall him in a sentimental way,

he said carefully.

> But if I put a focus on that…

The precise word remained unuttered, but the implication was clear,

> it touches a very core part of my heart.

In that moment, we understood: This book needed to exist.

Ven. Gnanaloka’s chance encounter in 1990, when Ven. Ñāṇavimala was seventy-eight, began a sixteen-year discipleship. During seven of those years (1994–2001), he kept notebooks filled with his words. This book is more than a preservation of those notes; it is an invitation to step into those years and encounter the Dhamma as it was lived.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala had come a long way to be standing on that roadside in 1990. Born Friedrich Möller in Germany in 1911, his path to Sri Lanka had been unlikely—through war, imprisonment and discovering a Buddhist text in an internment camp. After ordaining in 1955, he spent a decade at Polgasduwa Island Hermitage—the world's first Theravāda monastery established by a Westerner for Western monastics.

Then he became a *cārikā bhikkhu*, a wandering monk, walking barefoot across Sri Lanka for twenty-five years. It was said that there was no village in Sri Lanka his feet hadn't touched.

He practiced a rare echo of the Buddha's original instruction, a tradition nearly lost to time. *Caratha, bhikkhave, cārikaṁ bahujanahitāya bahujanasukhāya*— “Wander forth for the welfare of the multitude,” the Buddha had told his first awakened disciples.<Footnote id="1">Vin 1.21 (Mārakathā); Dutiyamārapāsa Sutta (SN4.5; Māra’s snare, second)</Footnote> This became known as *cārikā*—the practice of wandering on foot without settling, forming no attachments to place or possession. He taught that the wise cling to nothing, like a droplet on a lotus leaf, touching but never sticking. The monk moves through the world the same way—being in the world while clinging to nothing, yet bringing welfare wherever he wanders.

The sight of such a monk—his presence, his simplicity—brought a rare blessing. For those fortunate enough to offer him alms or hear a few words of Dhamma, the encounter was rarer still.

Eventually, the body sustaining this journey grew frail, and walking reached its natural conclusion in 1991. He told the young disciple that the *cārikā* had been his service to the people.

Those who encountered him often found their lives altered. Venerable Katukurunde Ñāṇananda, whose influential talks and writings on early Buddhism would be studied worldwide, described him as the “Mahākassapa of this age.” In the Buddha's time, Ven. Mahākassapa was foremost in *dhutaṅga*—the thirteen austere practices of intensely ‘shaking off’ mental defilements—and the only disciple with whom the Buddha exchanged robes. Ven. Katukurunde Ñāṇananda told Ven. Bhikkhu Bodhi:

> If you want to get a sermon from Mahākassapa, go see Venerable Ñāṇavimala.

Ven. Bhikkhu Bodhi, translator of major Buddhist texts, would note that “he would adjust his Dhamma talk to meet the other person in precisely the way that best fit the other person’s needs,” crediting him for shaping his early understanding of meditation. He also noted that while Ven. Ñāṇavimala exemplified the ascetic ideal, it was not the only model the Buddha praised.

Ajahn Brahmavaṁso, now one of the most recognized Buddhist teachers in the West, describes what he witnessed as “the best Dhamma talk that I have ever had the good *karma* (accumulated merit) to listen to!,” recalling how Ven. Ñāṇavimala “wove quotes from the *Suttas* together with explanations from his own experience into such symphony of Dhamma.”

Despite his influence on a generation of Buddhist teachers, Ven. Ñāṇavimala himself remained almost invisible. He gave no public discourses, wrote no books, built no institutions. When he passed away in 2005, most of the Buddhist world, even in Sri Lanka, where he'd walked for decades, barely knew his name.

None of that mattered to him anyway.

While many could only receive brief encounters, the specific depth and continuity of his private instructions were uniquely captured by Ven. Gnanaloka. An intensely private transmission he now shares publicly, believing the Dhamma exists to end suffering, not to be guarded.

He describes Ven. Ñāṇavimala as embodying a rare combination of having the deep *Sutta* understanding of a Pāli scholar united with the relentless striving in meditative practice of a wandering forest ascetic. His knowledge came from studying under Venerable Nyanatiloka and Venerable Ñāṇamoli, two of the first Western monks to master Pāli and translate key Buddhist texts. This lineage of going directly to the *Suttas* as primary authority shaped everything about how he taught and practiced. He upheld the ancient practice so faithfully, even in a world of increasing comfort. It's easy to miss how rare such commitment is, even within monastic life.

When Ven. Gnanaloka, as a young layman, first encountered him, he assumed all monks were like this. Only gradually did he discover he'd found someone exceptional. He had encountered or read about many great masters of the recent era, yet there was something in Ven. Ñāṇavimala that stood out.

During his years at Polgasduwa, Ven. Gnanaloka observed how visitors, whether seeking teachings or simply curious about him, found him formidable. His door was locked and windows closed most of the time. Visitors would come from afar, but many kept their distance, watching him sweep slowly, carefully, and mindfully around his kuṭi—as though observing something sacred they dared not interrupt. Ven. Gnanaloka noticed a pattern: in those who did approach, arriving with frantic eagerness and carrying offerings, something would shift. They would typically leave in what he described as a “bewildered calm.”

It wasn't just what Ven. Ñāṇavimala said. It was that he was completely present. Each small action such as receiving an item, pouring water or adjusting robe, done with total attention.

Somehow, that simple presence became contagious. Yet it also demanded something.

There was an intensity to his focus, a sharpness that could unsettle those who weren't grounded, putting many into a state of urgency to attain Nibbāna without delay.

Staying rooted in practice, he didn’t veer into idle conversation. While fluent in Pāli, the language of scriptures, his knowledge of the local Sinhala remained perhaps deliberately limited and carried a heavy German accent. This was so much so that even local people occasionally couldn't understand him. Yet once, addressing visiting monks from Wathuruwila, he spoke with unexpected clarity:

> Lankawe Sinhala handuruwanta saddawa thiyanawa, habai, shakthiya madiy

—Sri Lankan monks have faith, but not enough energy.

When Ven. Gnanaloka asked him how he accessed Sinhala words he had never heard him speak, he explained: mindfulness opens access to knowledge stored across lifetimes.

His simple utterance to those monks was a call to match faith with effort, and a glimpse into what mindfulness could unlock.

It wasn’t an isolated observation. In 1996, Shanaka recorded a warning from his teacher in his notebooks:

> It is sad to say that the essence of the practice is almost lost everywhere. People are getting more used to living a comfortable life.

1996—five years before his final teachings would be recorded. Before Google. Before anyone carried the internet in their pocket.

Ven. Gnanaloka remembers overhearing fellow monks speak with enthusiasm about the new technology of the internet. Ven. Ñāṇavimala just listened. He then replied:

> This is also in the sensual world.

In an age when a generation has increasingly begun to report that life feels meaningless, the numbers flat for decades and then climbing steeply as smartphones and social media became near-universal,<Footnote id="2">Monitoring the Future, University of Michigan, annual survey of American adolescents; see also J. M. Twenge, *iGen* (2017), and J. Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024). The associations are correlational.</Footnote> his words feel less like an observation and more like a warning. It raises a question:

What if the comforts we choose aren’t actually bringing us comfort?

When Ven. Ñāṇavimala turned away from the world, he turned towards something the world had forgotten: that real comfort—the kind that actually brings freedom—doesn't come from following distractions that pull us outward, but from going inward: staying with what's difficult. It requires effort guided by wisdom: the willingness to be uncomfortable and see clearly, and in that seeing, a release. As he would say, it is “a happiness free from the world.”

Even in his time, with competing interpretations of how liberation was to be understood and attained, his approach to finding clarity was unwavering: go to the source.

The source was the Buddha's original discourses—the *Suttas*.

Ven. Bhikkhu Sujato—founder of SuttaCentral and a scholar of early Buddhist texts—in his course on Early Buddhism, shares an account of how Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī (the Buddha's maternal aunt, foster mother, and the first ordained nun) came to the Buddha seeking advice:

> While Mahāpajāpatī (Gotamī) wants to go on retreat, do intensive meditation, Buddha is not exactly giving her specific meditation instructions.<Footnote id="3">*Saṁkhitta Sutta* (AN8.53; brief) is the specific Sutta mentioned by Bhikkhu Sujato. The Buddha instructs Ven. Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī to recognize true Dhamma by its effects: whether teachings lead toward dispassion (not passion), being unfettered (not fettered), shedding (not accumulating), fewness of wishes (not many wishes), contentment (not discontent), seclusion (not fondness for company), arousing energy (not laziness), and being easy to look after (not hard to look after). This is just one of many such instances found throughout the Suttas that demonstrates Bhikkhu Sujato’s point about the nature of how the Buddha instructed.</Footnote> He is not saying do Ānāpānasati…do this meditation, or that meditation.

He identified something telling about the way Buddhism is taught today.

> In modern Buddhism, when people talk about meditation, they often focus on details of meditation techniques. In the Suttas we don't find nearly so much detail about techniques, but a lot more about how you are living, what is the attitude in your mind, the focus and direction your mind is taking. These are the things that really matter.

When one observes the notes taken by Ven. Gnanaloka in his teacher’s presence, it becomes clear that this is the exact spirit in which Ven. Ñāṇavimala had guided him.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala was direct and sparing with detail. He would often direct them to the *Suttas* or specific passages, but rarely elaborate at length. Other times he would share a story to inspire or to convey a certain Dhamma lesson. In meditation, he offered practical suggestions, yet refrained from outlining fixed techniques.

Being with him was like stepping through a doorway into the Buddha's way of practice.

During his first self-retreat with Ven. Ñāṇavimala, then twenty-three years old and still a layman, Ven. Gnanaloka experienced the power of this approach. Rather than lengthy explanations, his teacher would offer a brief, captivating explanation such as on consciousness, suffering or liberation, and then direct him to the *Suttas*. Ven. Gnanaloka would read them not as subjects for intellectual study, but as mirrors to observe his own experience, watching these teachings unfold in his own mind. As he reflects:

> It's not like intellectually pondering on this

—it was a habit of direct observation, applying the teachings to lived experience.

On the last day of that retreat, the fourteenth day, a full moon, he perceived the Dhamma in a way he never had before.

> I will never forget that day,

Ven. Gnanaloka says.

> That was the time I really felt I had faith in Bhante. I also felt: these Suttas, 2,600 years old—these are the real teachings. From our level of defilements, our interpretations have come.

The importance of the *Suttas* revealed itself:

> That was the first occasion where from the Suttas I had a perception of Dhamma.

He added:

> That was enough to inspire me to know that the Buddha is alive in these teachings. As well as to know that this could be the end of our journey [in *saṁsāra*]. There is nothing else to do.

For Ven. Gnanaloka, there was no clearer path than this.

He came to see that true understanding of the *Suttas* transcends intellectual study. It requires direct experience. These insights arise naturally in moments of quiet contemplation, when the mind is relaxed and free from expectation, whether reading a few words of the Buddha or contemplating a single teaching.

Ven. Gnanaloka recalls those days fondly. With his teacher, he felt kindred—

> like comrades in battle,

he says. A spiritual battle.

In 2005, Ven. Gnanaloka was in India—the land where the Buddha walked and where remnants of his time still linger. The region had called to Ven. Gnanaloka since his youth. Even as a young layman, given money by his father for a one-month trip, he'd stretched it to three by sleeping at bus stands and washing at the communal facilities of local ashrams, unwilling to leave the land so charged with spiritual history. This time in 2005, with Ven. Ñāṇavimala's blessing and trust in Venerable Upasama (a close attendant monk caring for Ven. Ñāṇavimala), he was staying in a cave in the hills of Badrinath—a sacred valley sealed off by towering Himalayan peaks. Each day, he would walk eight kilometers down the mountain for alms, then eight kilometers back up. He was having difficulties—a foreigner near the China border (Tibet region), with troubles brewing over obtaining the necessary permits to continue staying on.

With the Rains Retreat<Footnote id="4">The Rains Retreat: Known in Pāli as *Vassa*, this is a three-month annual retreat observed by Buddhist monastics during the monsoon season.</Footnote> approaching and needing to find a place to settle, an attendant devotee invited him to join a pilgrimage north. He left his bowl and shoulder bag behind and set out. Only along the way did he discover the route led north, deeper into the Himalayas, to Leh, Ladakh. He remembered he could contact a senior monk there—Bhikkhu Sanghasena, who welcomed him to stay for the Rains Retreat.

During his time in Leh, a feeling arose. Undeniable. He shared it with Bhikkhu Sanghasena:

> I feel I may not meet Venerable Ñāṇavimala again.

A few days later, descending from the monastery with an attendant devotee to make a phone call from a booth in town, he received the news. His teacher had passed away. The funeral was already over.

There was no grief—his practice had released such attachments. Only profound gratitude. His teacher had given him everything that mattered: direct access to the Buddha's teachings, and the living example of how to practice them.

Several months later, he was invited to the stūpa consecration ceremony of Ven. Ñāṇavimala. On 10 October 2006, one year after his teacher's passing, the ceremony took place at Parappaduwa Island Hermitage, adjacent to Polgasduwa. A crowd of more than fifty gathered. They expected him to speak, to share memories and to eulogize his teacher. Even his parents, present in the audience, encouraged him to say something.

But he refused.

By then he was shaped by years of solitary practice in the forest.

> I was very serious,

he recalls.

> I didn't want to be in the public eye. It was very personal for me. Bhante, my guru. Even the notes were very personal for me. I was waiting for that time, when it could be passed on.

Instead of speaking, he quietly went to where the ashes were kept and worshipped. In that silence,

> I made a vow,

he says.

> Though I'm not going to say anything now, in a hundred-fold I will help them, give them the teachings.

Twenty years later, that vow bears fruit with the release of these teachings.

## Becoming Students Once More

The notes exist because of an instinct Ven. Gnanaloka couldn't fully explain.

Something had been pulling him forward long before. When asked decades later why he—by nature someone reluctant to take notes even in school—had felt compelled to record everything, he paused. A long pause.

> Somehow, I knew it was precious,

he said finally.

> Even then, I didn't quite know why—only that it had to be preserved.

He had simply acted—reached for the notebook again and again without asking why. Some instinct beyond language, beyond decision. It felt as if it had a destiny beyond his own understanding.

When we first proposed creating this book, Ven. Gnanaloka's response was:

> Let it be anonymous. Release the notes.

He insisted the focus should be on his teacher.

For an ascetic monk who had spent nearly three decades cultivating self-effacement, the idea of a book with stories related to his life contradicted everything he'd trained for.

> I am training to dismantle this idea of self,

he would say—not to perpetuate it. But we had a problem: the notes alone—concise, sometimes cryptic, written in the shorthand of direct experience—would not reach beyond those already deep in practice.

To serve wider audiences, they needed context: the spirit in which they were spoken, the questions that prompted them, the life that gave them weight. They needed his voice and his memories.

Which meant he couldn't remain anonymous.

Eventually, he agreed—for the sake of sharing the teachings, preserving Ven. Ñāṇavimala's legacy and to fulfill the vow he made.

The work took nearly two years. Meeting an ascetic who carried no phone, moved between undisclosed locations and Sri Sambuddha Ramaneeya Ashramaya Forest Hermitage, and prioritized practice over appointments meant learning to wait. Messages relayed through attendants could take weeks. Evening meetings with devotees and young monks would often run late.

Ven. Gnanaloka would sometimes tell us as he left Sri Sambuddha Ramaneeya Ashramaya Forest Hermitage:

> Don't think we have much time left.

This cryptic line often puzzled us, until he explained. Now in his mid-fifties, he feels a great need to serve and to fulfil his duty. He would compare his life to the timeframe of a day:

> When you look at it from the perspective of a day, I am approaching the evening.

A reminder, too, not to take for granted the opportunity we have to find a true refuge in the Dhamma.

When we finally did meet, more than twenty times over those two years, he was generous with his time. Sessions would begin near midnight and continue until three or four in the morning. The world was silent. His recollections, perhaps even sharper at night, flowed freely. We captured teachings with audio recorders. Shanaka captured them with pen and paper. Different tools, but the same living current: we were there as students to receive a direct transmission, not merely to compile a historical record.

We would read him his own notes, written decades earlier. Stories spilled out—details that hadn't made it into the original entries, moments he had forgotten, teachings that needed the context of place and time to make sense. These became the reflections that accompany the notes in this book—the stories behind the teachings, the moments that gave them life.

The reflections move from the authority of the *Suttas* to tackling sensuality, from compassion in action to the deepest insights on consciousness and liberation. Woven through these teachings is the arc of a transformation—how a young man named Shanaka, drawn to meditation yet caught in worldly desires, gradually became Ven. Gnanaloka under his teacher's patient, uncompromising guidance. Each carries both the teaching and the testing of it, showing not just the path, but what it asks of those who walk it. Even *cārikā* through dangerous territory.

Months into the recording process, during one such late-night session, he said something that stopped us.

> Sometimes when I read,

he began,

> I was in tears actually.

He explained:

> I don’t go back to memories of those experiences. I am not trained to go back to those memories that much. It’s a big strain for me. If I want to, maybe I have to specially make an effort.

But the reflections in the book had done what his own will wouldn’t—they brought him back.

> It brought me to those feelings of saddhā—faith,

he said.

> I’ll never forget. And it’s very clear. Bhante’s talking of the same points, especially now when I’m reading, the same way.

He paused, then added something we hadn’t anticipated.

> I was also doubtful, did I tell the exact details?

We understood then what it meant to be entrusted with something precious.

We weren't monks or scholars. We were practitioners—and sitting before Ven. Gnanaloka, we became students once more, receiving teachings to preserve and transmit.

The original notes are Ven. Gnanaloka’s. The Reflections—the narratives surrounding those notes—are our attempt to transmit faithfully what we researched and heard in those midnight sessions.
