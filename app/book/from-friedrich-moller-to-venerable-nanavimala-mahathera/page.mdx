# From Friedrich Möller to Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera

Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera (1911–2005) was a deeply revered yet elusive ascetic monk who remained almost entirely out of the public limelight. The unshakeable spirit and wisdom he embodied made him a quiet legend amongst dedicated practitioners, both lay and monastic.

Born in Germany as Friedrich Möller, one of four children, he displayed a spiritual inclination from an early age. He grew up in the small village of Rinteln, near Hannover, in a devout Christian household, during a time when the Dhamma was not widely known in the West.

In the years following World War I, though only seven years old, he witnessed what suffering looked like in war’s wake. He watched the wounded and destitute coming to his village looking for food, saw a wealthy man his family knew reduced to begging on the roads. His school teacher, who had been shot in the eye while bringing water to a wounded enemy soldier, urged his parents to encourage him in studies and never to let the boy join the army.

Though his household was devout, he didn't see his family as deeply spiritual. The exception was his grandmother, who shared something of his temperament. She had, with remarkable accuracy, foretold when she would die.

He enjoyed sports like athletics and gymnastics, particularly the horizontal and vertical bars, but felt out of step with the world around him. Even as a boy, he would slip away to the forest alone, seeking solitude, neglecting his farm duties. His mother worried about what would become of such a child.

One time, when asked to slaughter a chicken during farm duties, he found the experience devastating. That moment left a lasting mark on him, and he vowed never to do such a thing again. Even then, he sensed this wasn't his path.

A chance meeting with an Indian medical student later introduced him to the *Bhagavad Gītā*, an Indian spiritual classic on duty and liberation. Its message of compassion and spiritual discipline resonated with the same tenderness he had shown toward animals. This awakened in him an aspiration to travel to India.

Leaving Germany was no easy matter, however. From 1935, Adolf Hitler’s consolidation of power began demanding the enlistment of young men in military service. Then, in his mid-twenties, Möller got an opportunity to march at the 1936 Olympic Games, where he witnessed Hitler address a packed stadium of over a hundred thousand people. He saw the authority in his voice and how a generation of young men were being drawn to him. He despised the regime's militarism and ideology of violence. Though the full wartime draft was not yet active, he needed a way to secure passage out of Europe.

Möller had not qualified for university (the Abitur), having failed a critical requirement—Latin. Instead, he went to Hamburg to pursue vocational training in business and commercial studies. About three to four years before the war began, this training secured him a position as a stenographer at a Hamburg trading house leading to his narrow passage out of Europe during an increasingly tense time. Ven. Ñāṇavimala would later remark that his failure to enter university had, in fact, been his good fortune. Had he qualified, he would almost certainly have been conscripted into the military.

The post was initially located in Madras (renamed to Chennai in 1996), India. He performed his work with utmost capability, enabled by a concentrative power developed through his personal practice of yoga. He quickly rose through the ranks to a top position. About a year before the outbreak of World War II he was transferred to Colombo, Ceylon (pre-independence name of Sri Lanka), as its branch director. This job offered him a pleasant and luxurious life, as well as a high standing in colonial society.

But when World War II erupted in 1939, this comfortable life ended abruptly. Though he had done no wrong, being a German national under the British colonial rule of Ceylon during that time meant that he was classified as an “enemy-alien.” Along with other German nationals in Ceylon, he was interned at Diyatalawa.

As a man in his early thirties, now in confinement, he had to come to terms with life's bitter reality—that the comfortable life he had built was not as secure as he had imagined, and that it could suddenly collapse through circumstances beyond his control. He realised the deep instability of worldly security. While in Diyatalawa, he turned to books on yoga and philosophy to understand his predicament, but found that knowing about these ideas was very different from having the power to do anything about it.

When Singapore fell to advancing Japanese forces in February 1942, Ceylon lost its key protection. What Churchill called “that indispensable island”—Ceylon, Britain's strategic base in the Indian Ocean<Footnote id="1">So described by Churchill after the fall of Malaya and Singapore. Ceylon became the critical base guarding the Allied sea lanes to the Middle East, India, Australia, and the Persian Gulf’s oil; the island’s security was now paramount—hence the internees' transfer inland to India.</Footnote>—was now exposed and under direct threat. The authorities began transferring the enemy-alien internees to larger camps in India. Möller was among those sent to the largest of them, Dehra Dun Internment Camp, in the remote Himalayan foothills.

The camp was divided into seven to eight separate wings. They were each surrounded by double-barbed wire fences and housed different groups—Germans, Italians and even Catholic missionaries. Möller was assigned to Wing One, where the so-called “upper class”<Footnote id="2">Although the wings at Dehra Dun were officially divided by nationality, political category and perceived racial identity, the one that concentrated many socially and professionally privileged Germans came to be viewed inside the camp as socially elevated; in internee slang it was therefore nicknamed the ‘upper-class’ wing.</Footnote> were housed: company representatives, independent traders, doctors, teachers, scientists and even mountaineers. According to internee accounts, conditions were more humane than those in Germany or run by the Dutch, Japanese, or Russians—the British treated internees “properly.” The camp functioned like a self-contained little town with a cinema, sports fields, library, hospital, canteen, gardens, and some internees even had pets. They were even given once or twice weekly walks into the surrounding foothills under armed guard.<Footnote id="3">Heinrich Harrer and Peter Aufschnaiter—mountaineers from that same Wing One—joined five other Germans for a well-planned escape in 1944 by cutting through the barbed-wire fence, not during a walk but using those escorted outings for reconnaissance. It was the two alone, however, who managed to trek to Lhasa, where Harrer became tutor to the young Dalai Lama. See Harrer’s memoir *Seven Years in Tibet* (later adapted into a Hollywood film by the same name).</Footnote>

Yet, for all this outward order, an inner psychological storm swept through year after grinding year. Möller was among the many who became inwardly broken—cities bombed, lives lost, and his own disillusionment as the life he’d built crumbled. As fellow internee Walther Eidlitz, a Jewish Austrian writer, would later recall of the Dehra Dun camp:

> There was no one in the camp who was not at some time overcome by despair and close to committing suicide.<Footnote id="4">Detailed in the book written by Bhikkhu Nyanatusita and Hellmuth Hecker, *The Life of Nyanatiloka Thera: The Biography of a Western Buddhist Pioneer* (Kandy: Buddhist Publication Society, 2018).</Footnote>

Yet in this same wing, Möller encountered what would prove to be a hidden blessing. As it happened, Wing One also housed Ven. Nyanatiloka Mahāthera—the esteemed German monk who had brought the light of the Dhamma to Europe and Asia—along with his German disciple, Ven. Vappo. Here the cool breeze of the Buddha's original words first touched his heart.

Through Ven. Nyanatiloka, Möller discovered his published work, *The Word of the Buddha*—a compilation of the Buddha's discourses arranged according to the framework of the Four Noble Truths.<Footnote id="5">*The Word of the Buddha* was later translated into multiple languages and recognised as a foundational text for both Eastern and Western students of Theravāda Buddhism.</Footnote> Its rigorous, systematic presentation of suffering and its cessation offered something beyond theoretical speculation—it presented a verifiable path. For Möller, reading the Buddha's words directly was a revelation. It pierced through his despair with profound clarity. That book lit a flame within him, never to stop.

Following the war, Möller was repatriated to Germany in 1946, leaving behind nearly seven years of internment as an “enemy-alien”—a dark period that however forged his spiritual conviction and direction.

Post-war Germany was a land of ruin and loss as many young men had perished. Although he had a strong aspiration to go back to Ceylon to ordain as a monk, he was deemed ineligible because he had not been a monk in Ceylon before the war. Ven. Nyanatiloka and other Buddhist monks however were spared the same fate, and were able to return immediately with the help of increasingly politically powerful Sinhalese Buddhists and their several organisations.<Footnote id="6">Sinhalese Buddhist lay and civic organizations gained considerable influence in the late colonial period, alongside the island’s largely non-violent independence movement led by an educated middle class. Through their efforts they managed to request D. S. Senanayake (who would become Ceylon's first Prime Minister the following year) to put pressure on the British government to allow them to return to Ceylon. This was successfully granted in Sept. 1946. This movement culminated in the transfer of power to local representatives and the establishment of the Dominion of Ceylon on 4 February 1948—setting the stage for a renewal of Buddhist institutional life after over three centuries of colonial rule.</Footnote>

Faced with the harsh prospect of rebuilding his life in a devastated homeland, Möller took work as a farm hand in his village in the countryside near Hamburg. However, the only remuneration he received here was free food and lodging. Working twelve hours a day, he had little time for his practice. Realising this would not help him fulfill his aspiration, he later found work in the finance department of the municipal corporation in Hamburg, living a simple and secluded life in a small rented room, saving what little he could. Although he lived thousands of miles from where he had encountered the teachings, the Dhamma never left his heart.

The nation was now facing a spiritual hunger to heal, as many churches also started re-establishing. Möller was able to participate in a different kind of support, by getting involved in Sutta discussion classes organised by a local Buddhist group.

Hamburg had an emerging Buddhist community, part of a post-war spiritual revival. It also provided the support Möller needed to fulfill his spiritual resolve. Through his involvement, he met Asoka Weeraratna, the visionary founder of the German Dharmaduta Society (GDS), established in Colombo in 1952. Weeraratna was visiting Germany to explore establishing a permanent Buddhist mission and was searching for German candidates suitable for monastic ordination. Möller, then 42 years old, emerged as an ideal candidate. The Society sponsored his passage to Ceylon under an agreement: he would train for three years in Ceylon before returning to Germany as one of GDS's first Buddhist missionaries.

It had been nearly thirteen years since he was first taken from Ceylon’s shores. In June 1953, Möller arrived back in Colombo, ready to begin his monastic training as an *anāgārika*.<Footnote id="7">A lay renunciant who wears white robes observing eight to ten precepts.</Footnote>

As an *anāgārika*, he kept to himself and maintained a disciplined inward practice. A shopkeeper at a bookstore next to Siri Vajirarama Temple who witnessed him come to the store would later remark that his intensity even then was striking, his gaze never wandering.

After spending a year at the Dharmaduta Society in Colombo and some time at the Forest Hermitage in Kandy, on 19 September 1955, he was ordained as a novice monk (*sāmaṇera*) at the Island Hermitage on Polgasduwa. His full ordination (*upasampadā*) followed shortly thereafter, with Ven. Madihe Paññāsīha Mahānāyaka Thera—one of the most respected Sri Lankan scholar-monks and monastic leaders of the 20th century, as his preceptor. After three years in Ceylon, his training was complete and he was due to return to Germany as a missionary in time for the Buddha Jayanthi celebrations (the 2,500th anniversary of the Buddha’s passing) in May 1956. Yet he felt he had barely scratched the surface of the Dhamma, and stated he was not ready to teach. He requested permission to stay on at the Island Hermitage to deepen his practice.

Polgasduwa Island Hermitage provided an ideal setting for intensive practice. Founded in 1911 by Ven. Nyanatiloka, this secluded sanctuary nestled in the Rathgama lagoon offered solitude and a strict adherence to early Buddhist monastic discipline. It had become a renowned center for serious Dhamma practitioners, including Ven. Nyanatiloka's disciple, Ven. Ñāṇaponika Mahāthera—whose later writings would help introduce Theravāda Buddhism to the West<Footnote id="8">They were the only Western-born monks invited to participate in the Sixth Buddhist Council in Yangon, Burma (1954–1956), in recognition of their deep knowledge and contributions to Buddhism. Ven. Ñāṇaponika was also a fellow internee in Dehra Dun, but staying in a different Wing.</Footnote>—and notable Sri Lankan monks like Ven. Katukurunde Ñāṇananda. The monastic training and emphasis on the direct study of the Suttas shaped Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s path, leading him to uphold the purest form of Buddhist monastic life.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala was the last living disciple of Ven. Nyanatiloka, placing him within a distinguished lineage of Western-born monks dedicated to reviving classical Theravāda Buddhism. Although Ven. Nyanatiloka was the key influence in guiding him onto the path, it was another resident monk, Ven. Ñāṇamoli Thera, whom he regarded foremost as a mentor and source of theoretical guidance. Ven. Ñāṇamoli was an English monk renowned for his deep study and translation of the Pāli Canon, having himself learned Pāli from Ven. Nyanatiloka.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala resolved that before teaching others the Dhamma, he must first directly experience its truth himself.

After 11 years of dedicated practice at the Island Hermitage, he took up the ancient practice of *cārikā*—walking from place to place as a wandering monk. This was a profound training in freedom, designed to systematically break all attachments to places, people, and possessions. He travelled the island on foot for over twenty-five years—from south to north, east to west—meeting people of all walks of life. He carried only what the Vinaya permitted: his alms bowl, the three robes, and the few basic allowables needed for survival, including a piece of soap wrapped in jackfruit leaves. His journey was one of silent blessing; each step was a teaching. To those who met him on the road—villagers, travelers, housewives—he offered the chance to give alms and thus earn merit. Occasionally, with just a few words, he would deliver profound Dhamma teachings that left lasting impressions on those who heard them.

He would usually stay no more than three days in one place except during the Rains Retreat. The austere practice he developed was reminiscent of Ven. Mahākassapa Thera, one of the foremost disciples of the Buddha. Ven. Ñāṇavimala embodied a similar asceticism, wisdom and unshakeable detachment from worldly concerns.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala by nature kept to himself. He didn’t encourage disciples, didn’t build monasteries, and didn’t seek followers. He lived detached—from people, places, even from his own body.

The physically demanding practice of *cārikā* over the years took its toll. After 1987, a hip affliction prevented him from walking long distances, and he finally ceased his wandering life in 1991. He then spent four years in Colombo at the Siri Vajirarama temple before returning to the Island Hermitage in 1995. He later moved to the more secluded adjacent island of Parappaduwa where, in the last years of his life, he was bedridden and stayed in almost complete seclusion, cared for by dedicated attendant monks.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala became an inspiration for many of the most eminent monks of his era, who recognized his extraordinary practice and dedication to the monastic path. A testament to the reverence he inspired is powerfully conveyed in the book, *Pure Inspiration: Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera; Recollections of His Life, Practice and Teachings* (2016), which offers first-hand accounts from monks who, at the time, were young monastics—many of whom went on to become highly respected senior monks or teachers. These include, among others, the recollections of the late Ven. Gampaha Pemasiri, the late Ven. Katukurunde Ñāṇananda, the late Ven. Ñāṇadīpa, Ven. Bhikkhu Bodhi, Ven. Ajahn Brahmavaṁso and Ven. Guttasīla, who detailed their memorable encounters with him.

On 10 October 2005, at the age of 93, Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera passed away on Parappaduwa Island. At the time of his passing, he was the oldest and most senior Western Buddhist monk in the world. His passing marked not an end, but the consummation of a life lived with radical simplicity—a life that had long since transcended the need for worldly markers. Today, as many of the forests he once trod have been claimed by cultivated fields and concrete, his legacy lives brightest where his teachings are put into practice: in the mindful step of a *cārikā bhikkhu*, in the stillness between thoughts, and in the unwavering return to the Buddha’s original words. For those who encounter his story, he remains what he always was—a mirror reflecting the Dhamma’s fullest possibility.
