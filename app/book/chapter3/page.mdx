# Chapter 03: “The Way You Deal With Your Parents”

## Reflection 03: Seeing Through the Snare of Sensuality

The Buddha taught the spiritual seeker to look beyond appearances, to see the true nature of experience as it is. Beings, he said, are trapped in an illusion they don’t recognize and don’t know how to break free from. What the world takes as beautiful has an inherent foul nature; what it calls lasting is impermanent; what it delights in as pleasure is bound up with suffering—*dukkha*. As Ven. Ñāṇavimala would say: “Everything in the world is covered by a thin surface.”

Those words were not just philosophy. The Buddha revealed that even a world of wealth and comfort was not a solid reality, but a fleeting collection of conditions. The words Möller read in the internment camp felt like a breath of fresh air, a light piercing the fog of illusion. They spoke to the hollow core of everything he had once held as certain. He began seeing the danger the Buddha warned of: mistaking the unstable for stable invites suffering again and again.

To be free of that danger requires dismantling the web of false conditioning woven by craving and ignorance. To penetrate this illusory appearance was to see through that thin surface he spoke of. As he went on to say: “If it is penetrated, there would not arise attachment.” This was the path to the end of *dukkha*. He undertook this with endurance and clarity. An unrelenting commitment to simplicity, renunciation and truth. A striving that would come to define his life as a monk.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala wandered barefoot across Sri Lanka, carrying nothing but his bowl, robes, shoulder bag and other necessary requisites.<Footnote id="1">The eight requisites of a monk are the three robes, almsbowl, razor, needle/thread, belt & water strainer.</Footnote> He accepted only absolute necessities for survival—and even then, only the bare minimum. He once told Shanaka, “You rest only in hospitals,” a reflection of his own uncompromising resolve. It wasn’t an exaggeration.

Years later, when Ven. Gnanaloka himself began undertaking *cārikā*, he met doctors who had treated Ven. Ñāṇavimala during bouts of severe illness. They remembered him staying only until he could stand again; then, without announcement, he would vanish—on foot, alone, with no destination beyond the next forest or village. For him, rest was not a right but a concession to necessity, and even then, only briefly granted.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala would often say that “life, even the life of a bhikkhu is full of *dukkha*: sickness, old age, and the continuous effort just to find sustenance.” The body, he reflected, is merely a fragile sack of thirty-two foul parts. “If one vomited what was consumed, one would recoil in disgust. Even before it enters the stomach, it is offensive. Let it sit a day or two, and it rots. Yet the world clings to this form—this perishable thing—mistaking it for pleasure.”

The flower that delights the eye is already wilting; the skin that seems radiant today will soon wrinkle and fade. Even delight itself grows weary with time. What the world praises as beauty is only impermanence briefly disguised.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala understood sensuality not as something to chase, but as delusion wearing the mask of sweetness. Reflecting on the snare of romantic attachment, perhaps the most potent expression of sensual desire, he said:

> “Whatever momentary happiness is there, it is a temporary intoxication. But far more suffering is connected with it.”

To grasp at this momentary sweetness is to accept its shadow: the change, the loss, the effort of holding to what slips away and the ceaseless longing.

Desire, aversion, and delusion—these are the fires that keep us bound to discontent, always seeking, never still. He came to see that freedom lay not in belief, but in understanding the chain of arising and ceasing—how every flame of clinging depends on its fuel, and when the fuel is gone, the fire ends.

This clarity had not come overnight. Long before he donned the robe, even as a young layman in Germany, he had glimpses of it.

Once, there was a beautiful young woman from his hometown who was devoted to him. One day, he saw her speaking warmly with another man and in that instant, jealousy struck him like a sword. The pain was sharp, but the insight that followed was sharper still. The trap was clear—how attachment binds the heart, how longing turns quickly to anguish.

He ended the relationship quietly, moved by a painful recognition that such attachment would only lead to suffering. It was a glimpse of the turning away that would one day deepen into renunciation.

For Shanaka, this inner steadiness didn’t come easily. He would later describe his school years as a “terrible time”, a period of raging hormones and intense temptation.

Growing up in Lesotho among expatriate families, he was surrounded by Western ideals. Some classmates were sexually unreserved, others experimented with drugs. While drugs held little interest for him, the allure of Friday night discos—the dancing, the laughter, the intoxicating company of girls—was a constant pull.

Though he longed to join them, he was too shy to ask his father for money. That hesitation, mingled with a sense of shame, kept him away from habits that might have veered him off course.

Even at that young age, traces of a deeper potential flickered beneath his outward rough character. At sixteen, he had a quiet intuition that life was about to change, that by eighteen, something decisive would turn. And it did. But the first signs came not in temples or scriptures, but in the ordinary tests of youth.

There was a British girl he had a crush on. The interest was mutual; she even sent a friend to pass a message of affection. Yet when his own friend urged him to respond, his reply was unusually philosophical for a teenager: “I don’t want to play with the world until I know about it.”

That restraint came from an unspoken sense that the world operated according to a certain order, and he didn’t want to mess with it until he understood its rules. This moral instinct, a wish to act rightly without knowing why, was a kind of *sīla*, he believes, that earned him respect even from those girls.

That same instinct followed him into early adulthood.

Having just turned eighteen, before his first real spiritual experiences or any encounter with Ven. Ñāṇavimala, he found himself facing a test of another kind. While playing club cricket, he associated with older teammates who often went to the pub after matches for beers. One day, they started chanting:

> “Shanaka is going to drink today!”… “Shanaka is going to drink!”

He had seen what alcohol did to his elders, how it made them unrecognisably obnoxious. He had no want for it. But the pressure was overwhelming. The disco was called ‘Why Not’. Inside, at the bar, the drink was placed in front of him, and for a moment, his mind wavered. Perhaps just a sip, a thought whispered. It’s only once… why not?

He was just about to reach for the glass.

And then, through the noise, came a clear voice. One of the players’ wives turned toward him and said:

> “Shanaka, stick to your principles.”

Her words cut through the confusion. In that instant, his thoughts cleared, and conviction returned. His hand stilled beside the glass.

He said, “No.” It was final. The act of refusing stayed with him. It showed him how easily one could slip, and how firmly one could stand.

Even then, Shanaka’s sense of *sīla* was more a matter of con-science than insight—a kind of moral pride, a wish to show he had the willpower to resist what others yielded to, mixed with a sense of shyness.

However, something within him was preparing for change, though he could never have imagined it unfolding how it did.

What began shortly after, as a spontaneous spiritual insight, soon turned into a sincere discipline under the steady influence of Ven. Ñāṇavimala.

As the years passed, that guidance grew firmer. As Shanaka neared twenty-five, Ven. Ñāṇavimala perhaps sensed that he stood at a threshold—the age when the world begins to pull one outward, offering paths that promise comfort, but bind the heart more tightly. He would remind him to be firm in his direction, knowing that the world would soon expect other commitments.

Around this time, Shanaka took a job at an auditing firm, a decision that was met with his teacher’s quiet disapproval. He remarked to Shanaka, almost in passing:

> “I tell you to go this way,” he gestured one direction, “but then you go that way,” turning his hand the other way.

It was said in such passing, the words seeming to drift away as he quickly turned to another topic. Yet, for Shanaka, they did not drift at all. The words landed with an immense weight. For Shanaka this passing remark carried the force of a reprimand. At least, that's the way he perceived it. It was the only time Ven. Gnanaloka recalls his teacher expressing visible disapproval toward him.

During this time, some assumed Shanaka had let go of the idea of ordaining. They speculated that he would settle down and start a family. But Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s influence lingered in him like a constant echo. His teacher had once advised that if he could practice to maintain the discipline even here—amidst the noise, the demands, and the sights of the office—only then would his consciousness truly be replaced.

Shanaka took this to heart, resolving to use the environment to his advantage. Following his teacher’s guidance, he practiced sense restraint. He averted his gaze from the powerful allure of feminine beauty, not out of disrespect, but as an act of inner discipline, and kept his interactions with women brief and professional. It was an immense effort to steady a mind conditioned by countless lives of desire. So immersed was he in practice that he meditated during lunch breaks, even in the office restroom.

He could maintain outward composure, but it often concealed a fiercer reality inside. The fire of lust still raged. This became painfully clear at twenty-six, during a pilgrimage to the sacred Ruwanwelisaya *stūpa* in Sri Lanka. Midway through the journey, his mind was suddenly overwhelmed by a storm of lustful thoughts, as if a force from outside himself had descended upon him. The assault was so intense that he was forced to turn back. He never reached the holy site that day.

What he didn't yet know: the intensity itself was a sign. When breakthrough nears, the storm can often become fiercest.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s uncompromising counsel remained in him:

> “You must see a tremendous need to turn away from the *kāma-loka*—the world of sensual pleasure.”

Yet the mind does not submit to willpower alone. It must be met wisely, its habits seen for what they are, not fought against.

For this, Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s advice was: “When unwholesome thoughts arise, counter them with *asubha saññā*”:

This practice is about seeing the truth of the body beyond the superficial appearance that the world values so highly. It means investigating its inherent foul nature by seeing it as the Buddha taught—a fragile assembly of internal organs, tissues, and fluids, thinly veiled by a layer of skin;<Footnote id="2">The 32 parts of the body: Head hairs, body hairs, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinews, bones, bone marrow, kidneys, heart, liver, pleura, spleen, lungs, large intestine, small intestine, contents of the stomach, feces, brain, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, skin-oil, saliva, mucus, fluid in the joints, and urine.</Footnote> an assembly subject to decay and unworthy of attachment. Does the sight or smell of any of these internal parts evoke attraction? Yet, we have built a conceit, calling this assembly ‘mine.’ This reflection reveals how the mind falsely takes the body as its own—an attachment born of deception, which perpetuates seeking delight in food and the human form. Seeing this, the grip of identification with the body weakens. When the body is no longer identified with, pleasure-seeking through its sense faculties loses its power. The fever of lust gradually cools. A wise, liberating dispassion arises, finding its worth in wisdom, not in fleeting appearances.

Then Ven. Ñāṇavimala added:

> “If that fails, read the Dhammapada.<Footnote id="3">The Dhammapada is a revered collection of the Buddha’s verses on ethical and mental cultivation, drawn from the Pāli Canon.</Footnote> Trace the root. See where the attachment lies. Know that it stems from ignorance—from clinging to what is not truly yours.”

He urged the careful watching of the mind—to learn to recognize the root before it overpowers. Ven. Ñāṇavimala would say:

> “Whenever you delight in pleasant feeling, know that delusion is close by.”

He would tell Shanaka again and again, throughout the day, to bring one’s mindfulness to reflect on one’s conduct. He directed him to the *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā)—the Buddha’s counsel to his own son. This practice is to hold a mirror to body, speech, and mind—look before you act, watch while acting, and review afterward. If unwholesome actions have occurred, honestly recognize them and resolve to let them go.

Ven. Gnanaloka knew of a rishi in India who carried the essence of this principle: each day, he would write down every unwholesome thought that had arisen in him on the wall of his cave. Over time, the writings grew fewer. Then eventually, they became zero.

The mind’s activities, when recognised honestly without judgement, begin purifying themselves.

“Purified mind brings purified action,” Ven. Ñāṇavimala used to say.

He spoke of three protective factors against sensual lust. The first was physical exertion.

Staying in comfort without exertion, he said, allows urges to increase more easily. Where possible, he would encourage practice with some physical endurance—walking such as for alms, physical movement that leads to sweating. This physical exertion was never for fitness or the vain idea of sculpting a body, but a deliberate support to quell restless energy. “Not everyone can sit in one place all day and meditate,” he would say. “That is not for all.”

He would point out that the practice Shanaka did at Parappaduwa was the right way—the morning rowing across the lagoon, the long, taxing miles walked for alms.

The other two protective factors were reading the *Suttas* and solitude.

For his first solitary retreat, Shanaka chose the secluded Kalugala Forest Monastery, a sanctuary nestled in the hills of Sri Lanka’s Kalutara District near the dense Sinharāja rainforest. It was the ideal setting to put his teacher’s guidance into practice. Before departing, Ven. Ñāṇavimala gave him one essential instruction for his protection:

> “Be well-grounded in the Four Protective Meditations before heading to the jungle.”

They were:

- *Buddhānussati* [Recollection of the Buddha’s qualities]—to uplift and steady the mind,

- *Mettā* [Loving-kindness]—to guard against ill will,

- *Asubhasaññā* [Perception of foulness]—to weaken sensual craving,

- *Maraṇānussati* [Recollection of death]—to cultivate urgency and clarity.

These were not rituals to be recited. They were armor. A toolkit for the mind in solitude. And he was right. Immediately upon entering the forest, lustful thoughts arose.

Shanaka came to see sense restraint not as submission, but as a freedom—a peace of mind untouched by the pull of the senses.

Yet even as this understanding deepened, Shanaka found himself torn between lay life and the monastic path. He looked to his teacher for an answer. Once, his teacher shared a simile that encapsulated the training as a gradual approach:

> “Like a sailor when crossing a river—slowly, not losing the other shore—direct the boat slowly, gradually cutting the stream. Similarly, you should direct your life.”

With practice and his teacher’s guidance, Shanaka gained confidence in navigating that boat, adjusting according to the conditions and choosing the right tools at the right moment. Sometimes appearing to drift with the current, but never losing sight of the far shore, always skilfully angling back.

“Obstacles come when gems are close,” he later reflects, looking back on the unsuccessful attempt at going to Ruwanwelisaya. A couple of years after that incident took place, practice did indeed bring a clarity that cemented his conviction. The grip of the grosser defilements began to loosen. The confidence especially deepened when he donned the robes in 1999. The monastic discipline provi-ded foundational support for what had been developing within.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala would remind him: “One must not worry about what changes from the outside... but be happy that one is constantly following the way that learns to delight in the Dhamma, which is ultimately developed beyond feelings.” He taught this art not just with words, but with his life. In that silent, steadfast example, the answer shone brighter than any spoken word. And in that light, his student found the courage to follow.

### 26 October 1994

- It is good to start the practice early in life (earlier the better).
- You must see in your own self [the body] the impurities and how it is rushing towards old age.
- When one sees this with full concentration, there is no craving (*paṭiccasamuppāda*). You must also see the other bodies with the same destiny and impure nature.
- When you are old, it is difficult.
- Watch over the three ways of conduct<Footnote id="4">Refers to the practice highlighted in the *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).</Footnote> and practice *mettā bhāvanā*, and wait.
- If you have to do a job [get one] that will give you enough time and is in accordance with Dhamma.<Footnote id="5">Ven. Ñāṇavimala advised Shanaka to seek employment in fields like the Red Cross, civil service, or as a carer/attendant. The emphasis was on choosing a livelihood (*ājīva*) that aligned with the Dhamma (Right Livelihood) and left ample time for practice, rather than just pursuing large monetary gain. Such work generates wholesome *kamma*.</Footnote>

### 28 October 1994

- *Suttas* to Mahānāma. Though they were laymen they attained the path. Practice the way it is taught there. *Buddhānussati*, *Dhammānussati* and *Saṅghānussati* then *mettā, anicca saññā*.<Footnote id="6">Mahānāma was a prominent Sakyan layman and a relative of the Buddha, known for asking practical questions about how a layperson can cultivate faith and virtue. The practices often discussed in these discourses include the recollections (*anussati*) of the Buddha (*Buddhānussati*), the Dhamma (*Dhammānussati*), and the Saṅgha (*Saṅghānussati*), as well as the cultivation of loving-kindness (*mettā*) and the perception of impermanence (*anicca saññā*). These teachings can be found in several discourses addressed to him: AN6.10; AN8.25; AN11.11; AN11.12; SN55.21; SN55.22; SN55.37.</Footnote>
- As a layman or a bhikkhu, in any case, *Majjhima* 61— *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).
- *Majjhima* 107, 125 gradually to *jhāna* and the *anicca* of that—*Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna), *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed).
- *Majjhima* 64—*Mahāmālunkya Sutta* (MN64; the greater discourse to Mālunkyāputta).
- Word of the Buddha [by Ven. Nyanatiloka Mahāthera] (not what is mentioned in the commentaries). But follow advice strictly from the early Buddhist texts.<Footnote id="7">Ven. Ñāṇavimala encouraged Shanaka to focus just on the presentations of the early texts themselves.</Footnote>

- **If you are a laymen try to join an organization like Red Cross or civil service where you can perform action doing merit at the same time supporting the body.**
- If you take up a job in the management field you may earn a lot of money and now you are 24 but 25, 26, 27, these ages you will be approached by parents and relatives to have a partner. Here you have to make a decision. [*Personal Note*:] I must be frank about this [subject of marriage].
- Jotipāla and Ghaṭīkāra—*Ghaṭīkāra Sutta* (MN81; Ghaṭīkāra the potter). Ghaṭīkāra was a layman—an anāgāmī, who reached the Suddhāvāsa [Brahma world]. Jotipāla joined the Saṅgha, attained nothing at that birth and became an arahant as Sammāsambuddha [Gotama] before Ghaṭīkāra.

- **All my blessings to your endeavors.**

### 29 October 1994

- How much work you have in the layman’s life, still you should allocate certain time for the practice and in any case during *poya* [holiday] you should take *sīla*. If possible before and after *poya* you may retire [for] 2 or 3 days at a stretch.
- You need merit, don’t fear merit.
- The Buddha was once asked why he was so powerful? etc. He replied how he did *mettā bhāvanā* and how he rose in the Brahma world and how he became the *Cakkavatti king* many times as a result of his *puñña kamma*.
- **Learn to experience everything from a distance, as everything is *anicca, dukkha* and *anatta*.**

### 30 November 1994

- Were you free from disturbance from inside and outside. From inside it is not possible to be free from stray thoughts. Unless you attain *jhāna* (meditative absorption) where you have one thought.
- To attain *jhāna*, you should be highly *sīlavanta* (virtuous). The *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā); in this *Sutta* not only verbal and physical, but mental. So, you must purify the mind. - If you want to be a bhikkhu, you must really look into your heart.

### 26 December 1994

- **Consider the way you deal with your parents when dealing with other Godly beings. You must work and do your duty in such a way to get their blessing. But do not go [to them] for ultimate refuge, as they are still in saṁsāra (the cycle of suffering and rebirth). [You] must go for refuge in Dhamma.**
- We have created this situation.
- **Try to practice this awareness of the body (that is, as if looking at it from outside) or as if observing another body, cultivate it or practice it as much as possible because at that time you are living in the present.**
- **Read, grasp and practice a couple of *Suttas*. Concentrate on them.**
- Don’t try to memorise a lot of *Suttas*, like in the case of a scholar. Practice is important.
- **It is very important, this practice (to reflect on all activities during the day and to think over these activities one by one before your sleep, then to make [a] determination [*adhiṭṭhāna*] to avoid any activities you did the previous day that are regrettable).**
- It is good to end the day with *mettā bhāvanā*, after doing *Buddhānussati* [recollecting the Buddha’s virtues] and when doing *mettā bhāvanā* in the end reflect on the impermanence of that also.
- **[Your] Father too will definitely come to understand. He will definitely give his deep blessings.**
- **When certain impressions come, when reading or reciting the *gāthās* [verses] or *Suttas*, don’t sort of feel, I should immediately go, get there and things like that.<Footnote id="15">Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies that when a meditator gains a moment of clarity while reading or reciting the teachings, they should not get lost in wishing for it again. Instead, they should focus on diligently practicing the very things that led to that insight.</Footnote> No frustration, wishes and so on. You must remember that situations change.** So, you should work for it in the desired direction continuously and constantly. Like when you desire to reach a certain place (e.g., Kandy city), simply by wishing you would not get there. But if you start moving in the right direction and working towards it, stage by stage, you would finally come there. It is the same way.

### 27 December 1994

- There should be a good foundation of *sīla* (virtue) and then *jhāna* (meditative absorption) and *Satipaṭṭhāna* (foundation of mindfulness).
- First 10 *Suttas* in *Majjhima Nikāya* leading to final liberation.<Footnote id="16">While all of the *Suttas* in the *Majjhima Nikāya* (MN) are valuable, the first ten (from *Mūlapariyāya Sutta* (MN1; the root of all things) to *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN10; the foundations of mindfulness)) are especially recommended for meditators as they lay a foundational understanding of the core principles of the Dhamma that lead directly to the path of liberation.</Footnote>
- Purified mind is followed by purified action. It is important to first concentrate on the state of mind then after that other actions will be all wholesome (first *Dhammapada* verses).<Footnote id="17">“*Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-made. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts, suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.*” (Dhp1)</Footnote>
- Read a couple of *Dhammapada* verses daily.
- While attending [to] normal activities, it is not possible to attain the *jhānas* (meditative absorption). But when you practice *sati* or awareness, then when the time for sitting meditation, it is more supportive to attain the *jhānas*.
- When you practice this awareness of the body (that is looking at it as if another body) and the things that constitute the impurities, then there is a turning away of the mind from the body (dispassion). Then due to this a certain feeling of peace may arise. Now one must understand the impermanence of feeling and should not accommodate any craving for it. When one does that, [then] clinging (*upādāna*), existence (*bhava*), birth (*jāti*), old age, sickness—all this is not there (*paṭicca samuppāda*).
- But to do all this, one must build a sound foundation of *sīla* (virtue).
- Don’t think about what to do in the future or how to act because all situations change. When the time comes, consider and decide.

### 28 December 1994

- Must make sure there is a good basis for *sīla* (virtue), for the Dhamma building to be constructed.
- One cannot always be in meditation or *jhāna* (meditative absorption). But when one is attending to other work like moving about, one should establish oneself in mindfulness as much as possible and it becomes the most wholesome condition to attain the *jhānas* when one sits down to it.
- One should establish three ways of conduct and should reflect every evening before sleeping [in regards to] any activity that is regrettable—that which should be avoided the next day and so on. It should continue day after day. This practice should be put into effect without delay.
- Then one practices *Satipaṭṭhāna* to overcome the hindrances, and then attains the *jhānas*. Afterwards one turns away from that as well, that is when one enters the path.
- Know you have a theoretical grasp, but experience is important. You must experience it.
- Whenever there is a decision to be made and you do not know what to do, then you should practice *mettā bhāvanā* before you sleep and then think about the ultimate goal you want to achieve, and the decision you have to make, and then you can fall asleep. In the morning you would get the answer.
- Normally, reasoning out is not correct. This way it is more reliable.
- **In a nice appealing fashion, someone may appear (maybe the wife in past lives), you must know that this is Māra.<Footnote id="18">Māra is also known as the “Great Deceiver” or the “Evil One,” that embodies temptation and illusion, seeking to distract the spiritual practitioner from the path to liberation.</Footnote>**
- But when you practice *Satipaṭṭhāna* (foundation of mindfulness) it is quite safe. Don’t worry about anything. Every moment should be [a] pleasant abiding.
- You must be one with the Mahāsaṅgha, not considering an individual, then though we are separate in body, we will be one in mind.

### At a Previous Discussion

- **You must go and stay in a jungle, if you are forced from inside, not otherwise. You must see a tremendous need to turn away from the *kāma-loka* [world of sensual pleasure].**
- **A lot of arahants were near the Himalayan region. This area is extremely difficult. This was the reason that many became yogis, to escape from the *kāma-loka*, because there is nothing pleasant in this outside world of five senses. So, they turn away inwardly.**
