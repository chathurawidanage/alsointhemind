# By the Roadside, 1990

Sometimes a single encounter can alter the course of an entire lifetime. For Shanaka Dharmakeerthi, later ordained as Venerable Bambalapitiye Gnanaloka, that moment came on a quiet street in Colombo, Sri Lanka, in 1990.

He was eighteen, visiting his grandmother in Bambalapitiya, near Siri Vajirarama Temple, when she invited him to offer alms to a foreign monk who had come to their house on *piṇḍapāta* (alms round). While she stepped into the kitchen, Shanaka remained standing beside the monk. A stillness emanated from him. A quiet light.

The young man could not step away.

This was Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera—wandering ascetic, a noble saint, and a rare embodiment of the Buddha’s path in its purest form.

Shanaka could not have known then that this meeting would mark the beginning of a lifelong spiritual apprenticeship.

At the time, he was far from any traditional Buddhist influence. At fourteen, he had moved with his family to Lesotho, Southern Africa, where his mind became absorbed in the vastness of physics. Like many young people passionate about science, he was convinced that its laws alone held the key to revealing the ultimate nature of reality. Shortly after turning eighteen, while walking along the Caledon River, he experienced a deep, powerful recognition of impermanence—a moment so vivid it tilted his life toward solitude and contemplation. It left him with the bold, naïve conviction that he was his own teacher.

He still carried that conviction when he stood beside the monk in Bambalapitiya.

Days after their first meeting, fate intervened again. In Panadura Town, Shanaka saw a monk from behind, walking barefoot under the midday sun—robes weathered from the road, carrying a walking stick. His slight frame somehow held the presence of a giant. To Shanaka's eyes of wonder, there was something of a warrior about him. His steps, though slow, carried a surprising vitality. It was the same foreign monk, Venerable Ñāṇavimala. Stopping his motorbike, he offered to bring a bottle of ‘SMAK’ fruit drink. The monk looked at him with eyes that seemed to see far beyond the surface and said:

> This is not what we expect from you…that you also follow this path.

As he said this, a gentle smile crossed his face.

The words rang through Shanaka like a struck bell. He had never spoken of his spiritual leanings to the Venerable, yet here they seemed to be laid bare.

They sat together on the roadside. Shanaka had brought a SMAK, but the glass bottle refused to open in his hands. Seeing his struggle, Ven. Ñāṇavimala called him over and drew a Swiss Army knife from his shoulder bag. With a flick, the cap was open. He handed the bottle back to Shanaka so that the offering could still be Shanaka’s to make. He then took his alms meal in silence, while Shanaka waited by the pavement.

Shortly after the meal, Shanaka opened up about his experience of perceiving impermanence, weaving it with his knowledge of physics and philosophy. Ven. Ñāṇavimala listened in silence. Then he said:

> This is also in the mind.

The sentence cut through every layer of self-assurance as if he'd been reduced to the size of an ant. What he had taken to be profound realisations were seen for what they were.

At that moment, the ground shifted. A knowing emerged: he had found his teacher.

Two days later, he returned to Siri Vajirarama Temple and shared more of his experiences. Ven. Ñāṇavimala listened and said:

> Read the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, and everything will be okay.

From then on, Shanaka began reading the *Suttas*, seeing them not just as texts for study but as subjects for contemplation, shaping his practice around them even while pursuing his studies. Yet, while working towards a degree in Business Studies in Cyprus, his practice weakened and even keeping his vows of moral conduct became difficult. But deep down, a yearning intensified. He was torn between two worlds. By the time he was twenty-three, the spiritual calling had grown too strong to ignore, and he returned to Sri Lanka.

Hearing that his teacher was at Polgasduwa Island Hermitage, he went to seek blessings before leaving for a self-retreat at Athdalagala forest monastery. Instead, Ven. Ñāṇavimala offered:

> There are empty kuṭis here.

It was a rare invitation to practise under his direct guidance. The abbot, however, seeing only a young man in jeans and a T-shirt, refused to allow a layperson to stay at the monastery. “This is not a holiday place,” he was told. But Shanaka was unwilling to let the opportunity slip away. Inwardly, he resolved to live outside the island under a tree, to walk for alms in the village with a plastic bowl, and to visit the hermitage for his teacher’s guidance.

As Shanaka walked back behind Ven. Ñāṇavimala, heavy with disappointment, Ven. Ñāṇavimala paused, turned back to him, and said:

> There may be some karma behind it.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala advised him to obtain a letter of approval from the Mahānāyaka Thera<Footnote id="1">Mahānāyaka Theros are high-ranking Buddhist monks who oversee and regulate the Buddhist clergy in Theravāda Buddhist countries.</Footnote> at Maharagama Siri Vajiragnana Dharmayathanaya. The letter was granted by Venerable Madihe Paññāsīha Mahānāyaka Thera, who, five years later, would become his preceptor. With the letter in hand, Shanaka returned to Polgasduwa and was finally allowed to stay.

In 1994, during his first solo retreat there, he experienced a deeper intuitive connection. Not with contemporary forms of Buddhism, but with the Buddha who had walked the earth more than two and a half millennia before. Through the *Suttas*, he felt close to that timeless presence, and in Ven. Ñāṇavimala, he perceived not merely a person, but a living embodiment of the Dhamma itself. Every two days, he sat before Ven. Ñāṇavimala, receiving words as rare as they were illuminating, each pointing him back to the *Suttas*. Days were spent in the library reading the texts his teacher recommended, contemplating and meditating on them.

At night, under the dim glow of a kerosene lamp, he carefully recorded each instruction, knowing he was preserving something precious. Those notes, guarded for decades, trace the full arc of a training in the Buddha's path. Presented here, they are not simply records; they are the living current of a master's guidance encouraging a seeker towards the original *Suttas*, capable—if one listens closely—of altering the course of a life.
