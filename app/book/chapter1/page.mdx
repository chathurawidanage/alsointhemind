# Chapter 01: “Avoid Every Bondage With The World”

## Reflection 01: The Makings of a Disciple

Shanaka was born in 1971, during the height of the youth insurrection in Sri Lanka<Footnote id="1">In 1971, the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), a Marxist and anti-imperialist youth movement, launched an armed uprising with coordinated attacks on police stations across Sri Lanka. The revolt was crushed within weeks but left thousands dead and many imprisoned.</Footnote> At the time, his parents were living in Anuradhapura, where his father worked as an agricultural engineer.However, his maternal grandfather, a doctor, insisted that Shanaka’s mother travel to Colombo and stay with them until the baby was born. According to his mother, this decision turned out to be a blessing in disguise.

Shanaka came into the world several weeks premature, not in a hospital, but in the backseat of his grandfather’s car, near a Buddha statue, as they were rushing to the hospital. His grandmother, overjoyed, is said to have exclaimed:

> *“Mage budu putha ipaduna”* (“My son, blessed as a Buddha, is born.”)<Footnote id="2">The phrase ‘Budu putha’ (blessed as a Buddha) is a fairly common Sinhalese term of endearment used to express that the child is precious or beloved in a moment of great joy.</Footnote>

Though they may have hoped for a saintly child, what they got was a whirlwind of energy. From his earliest years, Shanaka was rebellious and intense. When his father’s work later took the family to Kandy, he was enrolled at Trinity College, a prestigious private school known for its discipline and refinement. But Shanaka resisted conformity at every turn. He fought, broke rules, and challenged authority. Bruce Lee became his hero. Canings at school were frequent, and at home, his mother struggled to contain his fierce spirit.

Yet one thing stood out, even amidst the chaos: Shanaka’s instinctive reverence for monks. As a foreshadowing of his later path, at around nine years old, in an act of childhood play, he and a friend pretended to be monks, circumambulating a school tree they imagined to be a sacred Bodhi tree. They both thought it would be a good idea to run away from home to become monks, but knew their parents would be against it. The earnest play lasted a couple of days. Then it was back to fighting, back to trouble, the usual boyhood chaos.

His life changed course when his father accepted a foreign assignment in Lesotho, Southern Africa. There, Shanaka’s unchanneled energy found a new outlet in sports, particularly athletics and cricket, where he proved to be a good fast bowler. In athletics he outdid many of his schoolmates to such an extent that they would ask him, “What do you eat?” At the same time, a curiosity about the nature of life began to emerge. He would often walk alone along the Caledon River, contemplating questions of time, space, and existence. Albert Einstein became his new inspiration, and he began to dream of studying astrophysics at Princeton University. The searching did not go unseen. When he eventually left the school, one teacher, a man he remembers as “like a soul connection,” wrote in his leaving recommendation, “Shanaka is searching for a stimulus and a fulfillment beyond what we English call school.”

Entering his teenage years, he was particularly drawn to the theory of relativity—how time slows as one approaches the speed of light. The theory suggested that time was not absolute, but relative.To his mind, this lent credibility to the idea of different planes of existence—something he'd read about in one of his mother's Buddhist books<Footnote id="3">See Piyadassi Thera, The *Spectrum of Buddhism* (1988). The collection includes essays on Buddhist cosmology and the relative nature of time across various planes of existence (*Loka*), drawing parallels to modern physical theories.</Footnote>—where, in the higher planes, days stretch vastly longer and lifespans are measured in eons.

He had learned that mathematically, if something could travel faster than the speed of light, it would go backward in time. He began to relate this to the function of memory, how recalling the past is like going back in time. Meditation and intuition, he theorised, made you go faster and faster, time slowing progressively, like the eon-stretched time of higher planes. And there would be a speed where time would stop.

At eighteen, during one such walk, in a solitary, barren terrain near his house, a question arose:

> *What are the causes of any phenomenon?*

And then, just as instantly, came an insight:

> *Anything that begins in time definitely ends. There is no beginning for this understanding, therefore there's no end.*

The realisation struck him like a flash. All phenomena arise and pass away. This life was fleeting. But this very knowing transcends time. Whatever he was conscious of that had looked solid before now felt like jelly. Even the buildings of Princeton University felt like jelly. The dream began to fade.

In that moment, a picture appeared in his mind—a monk experiencing this very truth. A quiet resolve followed:

> *I must become like that and make this happen again and again. I must stay in silent places like this.*

When he returned home he informed his father that he wanted to ordain. That single sentence sent shockwaves through the family. From that day on, his once restless and fiery manner softened naturally. He walked more intently, ate mindfully and spoke less. His mother, who had a spiritual inclination, supported him wholeheartedly and became his first student.

His father, however, saw it differently. To him, it seemed as though his son was throwing away his future—his education, his cricketing talents, and his worldly potential. Their relationship grew strained. Shanaka knew he had caused pain, and with time, he came to understand just how difficult it must have been for his father to accept. But shortly after this turning point, he met Ven. Ñāṇavimala. The contemplations and experience during his walk by his house would form the ideas that he later shared with Ven. Ñāṇavimala on that day by the roadside.

Had he not met his teacher, Shanaka might have rushed blindly into renunciation, creating more conflict and widening the rift with his father. But Ven. Ñāṇavimala guided him with wisdom and restraint, offering a principle that left a lasting impression:

> “Don’t break your *sīla*<Footnote id="4">*Sīla* (moral conduct): For lay followers, *sīla* is primarily practiced by observing the Five Precepts: abstaining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants.</Footnote> for anyone—not even for your parents. But short of that, do everything you can to make them happy.”

During one solitary retreat, Shanaka gained deep insight into his *karmic* ties with his father. The years of his father pushing him—toward expected grades, a life mapped out in advance—and the frustration that came from resisting it. He saw it clearly now: not anyone's fault, but the result of cause and effect. Their relationship was a *karmic* exchange—why he had become his son and why his father had become his father. It was a *kamma* they had to reap together. He no longer blamed his father, nor did he blame himself, and a peace overflowed from within when he saw this.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala encouraged harmony, not defiance. He reminded Shanaka that parents are not random strangers; they are beings bound to us through past *kamma,* who have cared for ussince birth. Following this advice, Shanaka chose to honor hisfather’s wishes. He enrolled in business management studies in Cyprus.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala once told him:

> “You don’t overcome *kamma* by changing the environment—you remain equanimous and let it pass.”

These words shaped Shanaka’s approach to challenges. Rather than reacting, he learned to stay centered, to uphold his precepts and let old *kamma* unfold without adding new layers of conflict.

It would take nearly a decade. He never argued or pleaded with his father. He simply followed his teacher’s guidance: he meditated, studied the *Suttas,* and undertook silent retreats, both in Sri Lanka and later in India, while continuing to support his parents’ happiness.

As Shanaka continued practicing, he gradually began to understand: when the conditions truly ripen, life itself begins to push a person in that direction. Until then, there are still lessons to be learned, something still left to be understood and exhausted.

He recalled how painful it was to leave his precious stay with Ven. Ñāṇavimala. One time, after worshipping, he held his teacher’s feet before departing. He felt a deep sorrow at having to leave behind the sense of calm and Dhamma atmosphere he had found there. Seeing this, Ven. Ñāṇavimala told him:

> “You will be forced to come.”

As his practice matured, those words began to unfold of their own accord. Those who had once opposed him now began to encourage ordination. Quite unexpectedly, the entire environment around him began to change. Relatives and even monks would ask, “When is your son going to ordain?” Shanaka only wished to deepen his practice. He was not yet willing to make the decision to ordain.

Eventually, his father began asking him when he planned to ordain.

His father’s blessing gave Shanaka strength beyond anything he could have imagined. Years later, as Ven. Gnanaloka, during a grueling *cārikā* through the Knuckles mountain range, he became lost for several days. While trying to ascend a ravine adjacent to Duwili Ella by gripping a hanging root like a rope, he was batteredand injured along the journey. Stings from fever nettle lashed across his body like being whipped, and leeches clung to him in thick clusters. Exhausted, wounded, and without food or water, he wandered through the wilderness alone until he finally encountered a group of cardamom pluckers who treated his injuries and ultimately saved his life. Some later speculated that he must have possessed supernatural powers to survive. But in truth, what sustained him was something much simpler.

A quiet mantra arose in the depths of the forest:

> “My father blessed me… My father blessed me.”

That blessing, that acceptance, became his source of strength.

Looking back nearly three decades later, Ven. Gnanaloka sees just how true his teacher’s words were. Ven. Ñāṇavimala once told him:

> “The only way to truly help your parents, your relatives, and all beings… is to purify yourself. This will inspire others.”

He was right.

The seeds of Dhamma that Shanaka planted over the years bore extraordinary fruit. His father, who was once his greatest critic, became his greatest supporter, and in time, both parents were inspired to enter the holy life. His father ordained as a monk and passed away as a bhikkhu in 2024. His mother, his very first student, ordained as a nun in early 2025. It was the ultimate testimony of his teacher’s instruction. Ven. Ñāṇavimala had provided the living bridge between the Buddha’s words and the complexities of familial bonds.

The Blessed One had declared that even carrying one’s parents on each shoulder for a hundred years would not repay the debt owed to them<Footnote id="5">*Samacittavagga* (AN2.33; same-minded)</Footnote> His teacher showed him the way to honour that truth. It was not through grand gestures, but through patient conduct, unbroken *sīla,* and the steady purification of the heart. In following this counsel, Shanaka found that his ordination was not a turning away from his parents, but the highest act of gratitude.

- If there are any disturbances from outside, you remove them in a way that you have nothing to regret. Read *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta* (MN9; right view) again and again.
- **Establish yourself in the three ways of conduct so that at the end of the day, when you reflect, you have nothing to regret.**
- **Keep in mind all the time, the four factors: noble friend, reading the *Suttas* etc.**<Footnote id="23">As mentioned in the *Sotāpattiphala Sutta*, to reach the first stage of awakening (stream-entry), the “four factors” to keep in mind are noble friendship (kalyāṇa-mitta), hearing the true Dhamma (*saddhamma-savana*), wise attention (*yoniso manasikāra*), and practice in accordance with the Dhamma (*dhammānudhamma-paṭipatti*).</Footnote>—***Sotāpattiphala Sutta* (SN55.55; the fruit of stream-entry).**
- Make a firm determination to stick to the word of the Buddha always.
- Aversion arises because there is a lot of attachment.
- Memories that arise [are] connected to the sensual world. <PersonalNote>I have to go back and make a basis (exams and subsequent employment) in the world of the senses.</PersonalNote><Footnote id="24">A personal note by Shanaka—reminder of the tug between retreat and lay duty: once outside the forest monastery, he would have to resume study and, in time, work—engagements rooted in the sensory world.</Footnote>

### 7 April 1994

- *Vath Pirith Potha—Maharagama* [*The Mirror of the Dhamma* compiled by Ven. Nārada and Ven. Kassapa].
- *Sutta Nipāta*, *Vijaya Sutta* (Snp1.11; victory).
- Stick to the facts. See things as they are. Bring the mind under control. When meditating on the breath, if the mind strays to any object, know that it should be brought back to the breath. The senses delight in and search for happiness generally. In general life, we avoid unpleasant things and get attached to pleasant things, this is done even in meditational experience. So, you must always stick to *Satipaṭṭhāna* [the foundation of mindfulness].
- Generally, we see the body as pleasant, which is an illusion. The actual perception is impurities.
- Sexual urges should not be suppressed, but by constantly perceiving the impurities of the body, must make the urge subside.
- Must take all sounds, even mental sounds as distractions and must stick to things as they are: the breath. Must stick to facts.
- Must concentrate on the in breath and out breath. The whole body means the whole body of breath. Calming the body means calming the process of breath.
- We are always either living in the future or in the past.
- Must stick to the present situation and investigate and see the situation as it is or the reality of it.
- When practicing mindfulness of breathing, at that time when certain feelings arise, observe them carefully, see things as they are, and investigate how they arise; the senses. Thoroughly reflect [on] their impermanence, the nature of not belonging to you.

### 12 April 1994

- Taking part in Sinhalese New Year is only a convention. It is better to spend that time striving on the path and that merit could be transferred to relatives. For the wishes of the people who are pure and striving resolutely on the path are effective.
- Maintaining equanimity is very important, and developing and cultivating it, so that there is no more forming or constr-ucting of rise and fall, [due to] intentions, efforts and craving.
- Commentaries are useful; the *Suttas* that strike you very much, go through them again and again. But all of them have the same principle.
- Must do what your parents say so that you would not have remorse later.
- Not mentally expecting any particular vision or something. It is continuously observing the real situation—*Satipaṭṭhāna.*
- When you are in retreat, you must continuously try to keep your mind not wandering by sticking to the object of meditation or [as] second-best thing, reading the *Suttas*. Of course, you have to keep this body clean and the dwelling, the surroundings [clean]. You may attend to these things with full attention, mindfully as to what you are doing. When finished doing the job, one may come and sit down to meditate and reflect carefully of the memory of the last work done, and there is no disturbance, then one must think of something or recite which will calm oneself quickly, that one has to know, after that one can rest the mind on the object of meditation e.g. breath etc. (breath also one should not imagine but should grasp what actually is).
- In the beginning it is natural to conceptualize.

### 18 April 1994

- [*Quote from Ven. Ñāṇavimala*] I also had a migraine; it was there for some time. To overcome it, I started yoga, and became a vegetarian. Nothing worked, but when I entered the Saṅgha, when I looked at it from the point of view of Dhamma, it all vanished. There is past *kamma*. Have you read anything of Cayce? [Edgar Cayce]<Footnote id="25">Edgar Cayce (1877–1945) was a well-known American psychic who gave trance readings that purported to reveal individuals’ past lives. The book Many Mansions (1952) by Gina Cerminara documents Cayce’s work on *kamma* and reincarnation as a cause for present-life conditions.</Footnote> In a dream, it was (consciousness). I got to know the cause of my migraine. I was a soldier in the army. I had inflicted pain. Then I knew the cause<Footnote id="26">Ven. Ñāṇavimala related that, with a developed *Satipaṭṭhāna* practice, recollections of past lives began to occur. One such recollection revealed the origin of his lifelong migraines: in a former life during the time of the Roman Empire he had been a Germanic soldier fighting Romans in a Germanic village. A short Roman soldier approached, and he struck this Roman soldier on the head with a club, killing him. He noted his own unwholesome act. Following this insight on *kamma,* the migraines subsided. As an additional comment, Ven. Ñāṇavimala would stress the importance of grounding oneself in *Satipaṭṭhāna* (mindfulness), stating that the practice provides the necessary mental balance to confront often overwhelming past life recollections. ‘It was (consciousness)’ refers to Ven. Ñāṇavimala regarding these experiences as that of the working of consciousness.</Footnote>
- So don’t worry about these things. Any illness or pain, don’t try to overcome it by this action or that which is *Sīlabbata-parāmāsa* [clinging to mere rules and rituals]. Avoid that which increases it (whatever that is unpleasant). Practice of *Satipaṭṭhāna* is very effective. Maintain equanimity, let that *kamma* pass on. When that past *kamma* is finished, the pain will also be over.
- **Look at the body as if from outside—the perceptions, consciousness and feelings and see them as not yours and therefore maintain equanimity.**
- Keep the mind detached, don’t let the mind be defiled by the pain. Make sure you do not act in the present in such a way that pain will arise in the future. Make sure all unwholesome activity is avoided.
- **Try to live as minimally as possible.**
- Think how the Pacceka Buddhas lived and died at Isigili—*Isigili Sutta* (MN116; Isigili: the gullet of the seers). You will show the highest gratitude by practice.
- **Get established in *Satipaṭṭhāna*.**
- Don’t have any hopes or wishes. Try to cut down any bondage with the world of the senses. Be detached from the past, the future and the present.
- **When faced with the world, when one restrains and controls, and maintains a certain level of detachment or equanimity, that is *kamma* and it will bear its fruits in due course of time. Food should be taken as medicine.**

### 21 April 1994

- For the *sāsana* to last long and prosper, the best service you could [administer] is to yourself, properly make an endeavor to establish oneself in the proper Dhamma.
- **But if you try to change or get involved in changing the present situation, either the Bodhi pūjās [worshiping the Bodhi tree] or the conduct of the bhikkhus, even what is left will disappear. So be careful, do your own practice in accordance with the word of the Buddha, but don’t encourage or assist the wrong practices, in any circumstance. It is like swallowing red hot iron balls (using money as a bhikkhu etc.)—[***Aggikkhandhopama Sutta* (AN7.72; fire)]**
- When you go to different established places you should compromise according to their rules and regulations, but you must see that they are in accordance with the word of the Buddha.
- **You must observe this (*piṇḍapāta*) with Right View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*).**
- It is alright if you go on *piṇḍapāta* taking the 8 precepts with the white overall. It is good to go to a solitary place on a full moon day and observe [precepts]. It is good if circumstances permit, to observe 8 precepts on the other 4 *Poyas* [Uposatha days]. Failing that, choose the Sunday closest to the *Poya* or whenever there are holidays.
- **Everyday, you must do things carefully so that, when you reflect in the night, you have nothing to regret in regard to the three ways of conduct.**
- **When the mind is restless, you may read particular *Suttas* that struck you. You may read them over again and again. You may study and memorise the verses in *Dhammapada*, that particularly interest you.**
- ***Milindapañha* (Questions of King Milinda):**<Footnote id="27">Composed around the beginning of the Common Era, and of unknown authorship, the *Milindapañha* is set up as a compilation of questions posed by the Indo-Greek King Milinda (identified as King Menander of Bactria) to a revered senior monk named Nāgasena. It is widely revered for its clear analogies and probing treatment of issues like self/no-self, *kamma,* and rebirth—an early meeting of Hellenistic and Buddhist thought.</Footnote> **it is alright if you read them but accept the ones that are in accordance with the *Sutta* and *Vinaya piṭaka*.**
- **So always stick to the *Suttas*.**
- The higher rules are there to arouse the *jhāna* (meditative absorption). The minor rules are not necessary. But when one is directed to purifying the three ways of conduct, then the rules are automatically observed.
- You may continue not to eat after 12 o’clock. If it affects the health or if you are involved in a lot of work you may eat something small (milk or 2 slices of bread). But on *Poya* days, milk is also not permitted, water or tea etc. [can be consumed instead].

### 27 April 1994

- Don’t let the Dhamma seed that you have planted be destroyed. The seed that you have planted, the tree that you have grown—you must look after it, protect it, water it, and tend to it carefully so that it will grow, flourish, and come to full growth with flowers and fruit.
- Like a sailor when crossing a river, slowly, not losing the other shore, direct the boat slowly, gradually cutting the stream. Similarly, you should direct your life.
- Read *Sutta* No. 2 of *Majjhima Nikāya*—*Sabbāsava Sutta* (MN2; all the taints).

### 30 April 1994

- Always make sure to purify the mind.
- Purified mind brings purified action.
- The [Bodhisatta] left everything, settled to purify himself and [as the Buddha] served all beings in the highest.
- **Though we are separate we will be united in mind.**<Footnote id="28">Ven. Gnanaloka states that Ven. Ñāṇavimala would sometimes say this when Shanaka was leaving his kuṭi or was returning to Colombo. Ven. Ñāṇavimala used this statement to emphasize that spiritual unity is solely determined by mental purity, not physical proximity. For instance, the Buddha could see a sincere seeker practicing in the distance, whereas a person full of defilements (*kilesas*) is far away, even if they are touching the Buddha’s robe.</Footnote>
- **Avoid *akusala* [unwholesome deeds] even if you have to go through hardship.**
- Do things slowly, carefully and mindfully and always watch the three ways of conduct and just wait.
- Do *mettā bhāvanā* [loving-kindness meditation] and do a bit of *Ānāpānasati* [mindfulness of breathing].
- Be an example to others. It is a skill, the way one directs everything towards Dhamma.
- [Like] Nakulamātā and Nakulapitā [who were regarded by the Buddha as exemplary householder disciples].
- Direct the others in the highest—wish them the highest.
