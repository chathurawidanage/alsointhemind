## Reflection 01: Living as an Island Unto Oneself

Living in close association with Venerable Ñāṇavimala Mahāthera often brought to mind the Buddha’s final instructions to the Saṅgha, as recorded in the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN 16). Speaking to Venerable Ānanda shortly before his passing, the Buddha said:

> *“Tasmātihānanda attadīpā viharatha attasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā, dhammadīpā dhammasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā.”*  
>  
> “Therefore, Ānanda, be islands unto yourselves, be your own refuge, having no other; let the Dhamma be your island, let the Dhamma be your refuge, having no other refuge.”

This single instruction seemed to encapsulate the way Ven. Ñāṇavimala lived. He was beholden to no institution, title, reputation, or following. His refuge was not in monasteries or teacherhood—it was solely in the Dhamma.

One day, Shanaka—later to become Venerable Gnanaloka—saw him slowly making his way back to his kuṭi. At that time, Ven. Ñāṇavimala was already well into his eighties. Decades of walking barefoot across Sri Lanka had worn his body thin. A hip injury sustained in his youth had worsened with age. He leaned heavily on his cane, walking slowly, each movement deliberate.

Out of concern, Shanaka approached and asked, “Bhante, can I help you?”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala slowly turned, his eyes calm and bright, and replied,

> “This body is not mine—why should I give it to somebody else?”

That response left Shanaka speechless. In just a few words, Ven. Ñāṇavimala revealed what deep letting go truly looked like—not as a concept or philosophy, but as lived reality. This was the source of his unshakeable composure: when the body is no longer taken as ‘self,’ by means of wisdom its inevitable decay is no longer a personal tragedy.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala never claimed disciples and never sought to build a following. Yet those who approached him with sincerity found themselves quietly and often deeply guided.

He would often say: “If anyone is sincere, they will guide you to the *Suttas*.”

And he meant it. To him, the Buddha was not a distant figure from the past—the Buddha lived through the Dhamma. The early *Suttas* weren’t relics but living instructions meant to be practiced. He would often stress that the teachings are within reach for anyone who genuinely seeks them.

Although he never took on the title of *Ācariya* (teacher), from the moment Shanaka met him to the time of his passing, Ven. Ñāṇavimala offered steady guidance. Sometimes he pointed Shanaka to the *Suttas*; sometimes, he gave direct advice—often only fully understood years or even decades later. But more than anything, it was his presence—his silence, his unwavering commitment to the path—that deeply shaped the young aspirant’s mind and heart.

In Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s final years, Shanaka had the opportunity to serve him closely. Through those quiet, unspoken moments, he tried to express the immense gratitude he felt toward his teacher. But in time, he came to understand that true repayment doesn’t come through service or offerings—it comes only by walking the very path that Ven. Ñāṇavimala had lived so fully.

Though they first met in late 1989, it was not until 1999—after much hesitation—that Shanaka finally decided to ordain. He felt torn between two worlds: a strong pull toward ascetic life, and a lingering doubt—could he truly live a celibate life for the rest of his years?

For nearly three years, Ven. Ñāṇavimala gently encouraged him—never forcefully. He would sometimes remark, “How great it would be if you were to ordain.” On another occasion he even went as far to suggest: “for one year you can ordain, then you can disrobe again.”

One day, hearing his encouragement again, Shanaka responded with frustration: “The temples are deteriorating, the forest monasteries are deteriorating, the monks are deteriorating...”

Without missing a beat, Ven. Ñāṇavimala replied,

> “Your consciousness is deteriorating.”

The remark landed with striking clarity. Shanaka had no response—just silence.

And he was right.

Yet, confidence to take the next step did not come immediately.

Sometime later, while on a solitary retreat at Athdalagala forest monastery in Kurunegala—spent meditating, reading the *Suttas*, and reflecting quietly—something shifted. The doubts dissolved, and hesitation fell away. A deep clarity emerged: it was time.

The following morning, Shanaka boarded a bus to Colombo, following the advice of the forest monastery’s abbot to act swiftly. A part of him feared that Ven. Ñāṇavimala might pass away before he could ordain in his presence.

A quarter century has passed since that moment, and now, as Ven. Gnanaloka, he encounters others who come seeking guidance on entering monastic life. Even as he guides others, his reverence for his teacher remains absolute. He reflects that even after decades of intense practice, he has yet to match the profound wisdom and unwavering dedication of Ven. Ñāṇavimala. As he often says, “I am but a tiny speck of dust compared to my teacher.”

Following the example of his own teacher, he does not see himself as a teacher. Instead, he simply tries to hold space—offering guidance when needed, and gently pointing them to the *Suttas*, where the true teacher still speaks.

As the Buddha instructed, the goal is not to rely on others, but to stand firm—an island unto oneself.
