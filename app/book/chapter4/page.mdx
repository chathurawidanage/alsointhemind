# Chapter 04: “More Pleasure More Danger”

## Reflection 04: The Protection of Mettā

For Ven. Ñāṇavimala, compassion was not a simple sentiment, but wisdom itself. It was a seeing that cut through illusion and a courage that met death with a pure heart. A quality forged, it seemed, across many lifetimes. As a young man in Germany, working on the farm, he battled with an internal struggle—that by ploughing the land he was killing tiny creatures and he would need to hurt the farm animals as part of his duty. Later on he would understand this sensitivity, one rooted in a deep *karmic* learning.

He sometimes spoke of past-life recollections that began to occur after his practice of mindfulness (*Satipaṭṭhāna*) deepened. These were not meditative experiences in the usual sense, but spontaneous memories appearing like dreams, though he remained conscious of them.<Footnote id="1">Refer to the Notes, Chapter 1, 18 April 1994, for more description regarding his past life recollections.</Footnote>

One such recollection concerned a past life as a soldier. In the heat of battle, he found he could not bring himself to kill. In that moment of hesitation, an enemy horseman’s lance killed him. He remembered the harrowing scene with startling clarity.

He later got to know in this life that a monument commemorating that war event stood in that very place in Germany. From his recollection he came to understand that it was because of his family at the time that he was put into that situation, and the event was a source of his deep rejection of harming in his most recent life.

This *karmic* sensitivity led him to embrace strict vegetarianism as part of his spiritual practice. Möller had been shaped by his understandings of Hinduism and yoga, and by Gandhi's example of *ahimsa*—non-harming as a way of being. It was a conviction he took on absolutely.

At Dehra Dun internment camp, this commitment nearly killed him. The rations were basic, and in wartime India, where famine gripped many regions, dietary accommodations were not made. According to accounts, the diet consisted almost solely of meat, and when vegetables were included, they were typically cooked together with the meat in dishes like goulash. Unable to eat most of what was provided, week by week, his weight dropped. His Buddhist friends finally encouraged him: as long as there was no intention for the killing to be made, the animals were already dead; eating to survive was not the same as causing their death.<Footnote id="2">For the scriptural basis of this advice, see the *Jīvaka Sutta* (MN55; to Jīvaka).</Footnote> Near death, he accepted the food.

He recovered, and from that experience came to appreciate the wisdom of the Buddha’s Middle Path.

As a monk, his perspective deepened further. “Everything that takes place in this world entails killing,” he would say. He taught that all existence in *saṁsāra*, even the act of survival, inadvertently involves harm—the growing of rice kills insects, and everyday acts such as walking kill unseen beings. Each plate of food, regardless of its contents, depends on the suffering of beings. The highest compassion, he explained, was not about perfecting one’s diet, but about ending rebirth itself—extinguishing the very ‘self’ that keeps arising and thus a body that must be sustained at the expense of other life.

What mattered was intention. Indulging in tastes was a greater harm than the act of eating meat itself. If one had an attachment to the taste of meat, he would advise, one should acknowledge that and make a purposeful effort to give it up for that reason.<Footnote id="3">The Buddha instructs that a monk should reflect on food “not for fun, indulgence, adornment, or decoration, but only to sustain this body, to avoid harm, and to support spiritual practice.”</Footnote>

This same care for intention shaped his alms round each morning, mindful not of what was offered, but placing the needs of the giver foremost. When a wealthy lay devotee once wanted to continue offering specially prepared delicacies after the initial offering, he responded, “Please give an opportunity for poor people also to offer alms.” He was especially attentive that the poor had the chance to give, seeing it through a lens of *kamma*. That their present hardship was but the unfolding of past causes, and that through the merit of generosity now, they could help shape brighter conditions to come.

His compassion remained consistent, even when health concerns tested it.

Later on, whilst at Polgasduwa Hermitage, Shanaka and other devotees, concerned for his ill health, once made a special arrangement for particular nutritious food packets to be offered to him. He refused, saying, “This is not ascetic,” and insisted on taking what was normally being given to everyone.

This deep, inner goodwill wasn’t merely philosophy; it became a tangible force. It manifested as a protective energy, a fearless heart-felt wish for the well-being of others, that could be felt by all beings, human and animal alike. This was the quality that Ven. Gnanaloka would later come to rely on in the remote jungles, where he experienced its power.

In 2007, Ven. Gnanaloka spent six months in complete silence at Bambaragasthalawa—a remote cave within Kumana National Park (Yala East). What struck him most about the place was not just its seclusion but its absolute stillness. Even the animals seemed to respect the silence. The ancient cave, once used by meditating monks during the height of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, had likely not been inhabited by humans for centuries. Now, it belonged to the bears and leopards who ruled the jungle.

The nearest village was 15 to 20 kilometres away. A few concerned devotees had arranged for a *kapakaru* (lay attendant) to offer the daily meal. But Ven. Gnanaloka made it a point never to see or speak with him. If there was an urgent message, it would be left in writing near the alms bowl. Otherwise, noble silence prevailed.

One night, while lying on his side in the cave, he heard a low, rhythmic sound. At first, it felt like a dream. Then it grew clearer—deep, heavy breathing. Without rising, he reached toward the lamp and adjusted the wick. As the light spread across the cave, a wild bear came into view, just a few feet in front of him.

Instinctively, he began chanting:

> “*Virūpakkhehi me mettaṁ, mettaṁ Erāpathehi me..*.”  
> *(from the *Khandha Paritta—Ahirāja Sutta* (AN4.67; snakes))<Footnote id="4">The *Khandha Paritta* is a protective chant taught by the Buddha to safeguard practitioners from dangerous creatures, such as snakes, by radiating thoughts of universal loving-kindness (*mettā*).</Footnote>*

The bear turned and fled, disappearing into the forest like a dissolving shadow.

Years earlier, Ven. Ñāṇavimala had shared similar encounters. While meditating in a cave at Kudumbigala, a bear once entered and stood on its hind legs, claws raised. Ven. Ñāṇavimala recited the *Khandha Paritta*. As he continued, the bear lowered its paws and walked away. On another occasion, while walking through a national park, he came face to face with a bear. He lowered his gaze, radiated *mettā*, and when he looked up again, the bear was gone. When someone asked, “Bhante, what if the bear had attacked you?” he replied:

> “Well, then I would have died with a pure heart.”

As a young man, Shanaka was advised by Ven. Ñāṇavimala:

> “Recite the *Khandha Paritta* daily and act with *mettā* to all beings.”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s instructions may have saved his life more than once.

Years later, at Bambaragasthalawa, in a monastery, Ven. Gnanaloka would have another close encounter. Deep in meditation, lying down in corpse posture, he felt something heavy on his chest. He ignored it. Only later did he discover a snake coiled on his chest, perhaps seeking warmth. As he moved, the snake raised its head and met his gaze. Ven. Ñāṇavimala's instruction had settled into instinct: “Don't get frightened or excited when faced with animals because they perceive the state of mind.” Fear was replaced by a quiet recognition, almost like meeting an old friend. The snake turned, looked back a few times, and slid silently back into the forest. It was a tangible testament to the protective power of the *mettā*-training Ven. Ñāṇavimala had so consistently emphasized.

Ven. Ñāṇavimala related an instance when, in deep meditation, he realised a snake had been coiled on his lap. As he noticed it, he had gently lowered it to the ground. The snake moved on without harming him. He also spoke of how a snake would slither around him during meditation. When he chanted the *Itipiso Gāthā* (chant on the recollections of the Buddha’s virtues) the snake calmly waited without moving. When he stopped, it continued slithering. He started chanting again, and it stopped once more. As Ven. Ñāṇavimala related this story, he commented, “He [the snake] also needs some entertainment.”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala used to say:

> “Develop this heart of *mettā* in the highest way to all beings. This will be the greatest service to all beings from you.”

Ven. Ñāṇavimala encouraged Shanaka to read Ven. Ñāṇamoli's book *The Practice of Loving-Kindness (Mettā)*. Though Ven. Ñāṇavimala didn't himself instruct how to do the *mettā* practice, glimpses of his practice came through when he gave blessings for people. He would radiate a wish for the well-being of beings, ultimately directing them to spread the wish for all to be free from greed, hate, and delusion. He would say “your wishing for greed, hatred and delusion to go away.” This in essence was a wish for their ultimate release from suffering—Nibbāna.

He often spoke of *mettā-ceto-vimutti*—reaching a state of pervasive, limitless goodwill that becomes liberated from the hindrances. It is the liberation of the heart through loving-kindness—not as a technique, but as a way of being, a state of mind free from greed and hate.

This is a state of *samādhi*—deep stillness of mind—that can reach *jhāna*.*<Footnote id="5">*Jhāna*: absorbed states of deep concentration where the hindrances are temporarily suspended and the mind becomes unified.</Footnote>* Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught that from out of this *jhāna*, one can direct contemplation toward investigation, as described in the *Suttas*, allowing insight to arise and lead to liberation.<Footnote id="6">Ven. Gnanaloka mentions *Mahāmālunkya Sutta* (MN64; the greater discourse to Mālunkyāputta).</Footnote>

What stood out to Shanaka was how *mettā* could become a path to liberation through insight itself, not merely tranquility, as described in Ven. Ñāṇamoli's book.

To speak of compassion is one thing. To embody it when death stands close is another. But this was the way Ven. Ñāṇavimala walked, not once, but again and again, with unwavering courage, turning principle into lived truth and encouraging others to do the same.

### 28 January 1995

- **To overcome these [sensual] thoughts, *asubha saññā* [perception of foulness]**
#### How to spend the week:

- Read the verses in Pāli as if the Buddha is speaking to you, then the mind will be directed to that. Always the translations would not be adequate.<Footnote id="7">To Ven. Ñāṇavimala there was no second best to knowing Pāli; that was the best way to understand the Buddha. Regardless of how good they are, translators’ views invariably get projected into their translations, distorting meaning to some degree. He encouraged Shanaka to learn Pāli. However, he directed Shanaka to the *Suttas* through English translations and Shanaka/Ven. Gnanaloka would rely on them as his primary source of meaning.</Footnote>
- You must choose the *Suttas* that would help you to get on in the lay life. Read and practice loving kindness as [outlined] by Ñāṇamoli Thera [*The Practice of Loving-Kindness*], and in *Vandana Vathpirith Potha* [*The Mirror of the Dhamma* compiled by Ven. Nārada and Ven. Kassapa] there are 4 important things to be reflect on: *mettā* (loving-kindness), *buddhānussati* (recollection of the Buddha), *asubha* (foul nature of body), *maraṇānussati* (mindfulness of death).
- **Concentrate on *sīla* and *bhāvanā* (*vipassanā*).**
- **Try to elevate the state of mind—that is the greatest service. Because what follows depends on it.**
- ***Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā).**
- **Don’t study with the intention of accumulating knowledge.**<Footnote id="8">Refers to acquiring knowledge of spiritual literature just for knowledge’s sake.</Footnote>
- You can earn a living even by sweeping.
- Learn to be content with little.
- Sensual things are the most dangerous.
- **More Pleasure More Danger.**
- **Like moths who fly into the flames. So be careful.**
- Know that the Saṅgha is in decline.
- Because when living near towns, going to *pirivenas* [monastic colleges that educate monks], there are more sense impressions arising that arouse inner worldly inclinations, so they disrobe.
- So, one should retire to the forest, follow the instructions of the teacher.
- *Satipaṭṭhāna* should be practiced with Right View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*).
- *Jhāna* (meditative absorption) and then *Satipaṭṭhāna* (foundations of mindfulness).
- For *jhāna* to arise, there needs to be a foundation of *sīla* (virtue).
#### Regarding sensual desire:

- **When elephants are in rut, or other animals, or lovers—they are fully absorbed and intoxicated by that; no sense is seen.**
- **People work just to earn money, to support their body. All the activities are centered on that. No time for the practice.**
- **When on retreat food and sleep or negligence are the hindrances one must watch, not too much effort either, but one should balance.**
- **First you should raise the mind or consciousness to higher deva worlds. Then after that you direct the mind to the Dhamma.**<Footnote id="9">Ven. Gnanaloka elaborates: With *sīla* and *saddhā* (faith) and *cāga* (generosity), you stay with good activities—you’re elevated to a deva state of existence. Then in such a state of mind you practise the Dhamma.</Footnote>
- ***Saṅkhāra* (Volitional Formations) brings about birth.**
### 31 January 1995

- *Yoniso-manasikāra* (wise attention)—is these three ways of conduct.
- *Satipaṭṭhāna* should be practiced with the right understanding.
- The five faculties (*indriya*), *saddhā, viriya*, *sati*, *samādhi (*four *jhānas*) and *paññā*.*<Footnote id="10">The five spiritual faculties (*pañcindriyāni*) are inner strengths that sustain progress on the path: *saddhā* (faith or confidence), *viriya* (energy or effort), *sati* (mindfulness), *samādhi* (concentration, including the four *jhānas*), and *paññā* (wisdom or insight into the true nature of phenomena)—*Paṭhamavibhaṅga Sutta* (SN48.9; analysis, first).</Footnote>*
- No hate, no greed should arise.
- If hate arises, you should control it with *mettā* (loving-kindness).
- When greed arises, practice *asubha* (contemplation of the foul nature of the human body), when delusion arises, read the *Suttas*.
- Refer to the *Suttas* *Majjhima* 117, 25, 50—*Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty); *Nivāpa Sutta* (MN25; the bait); *Māratajjanīya Sutta* (MN50; the rebuke to Māra).
- It is good to be with the teacher in the jungle.
- It is very difficult to practice otherwise with unnecessary impressions.
- Also read *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates) and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases).
- **This life is full of suffering: old age, sickness, and the trouble of always searching for food. Even bhikkhus are not free from that; all others are working day and night only for this. [Also], the weakness of the body due to sickness. The food you put in is only for the purpose of supporting the body, [which is] constituted of the 32 parts of impurity. If you vomit it back, it is so repulsive. When it goes [past] the throat it is all foul, especially if you keep it for some days. When you reduce the craving or attraction to these things—though there [was] initial pleasure, [but] more suffering [is] connected to it. [By] cultivat[ing] the happiness in the absence of deluded pleasure, you turn away from the cause of this suffering *(paṭicca-samuppāda)*. No illusion, no craving, no clinging, [no] birth, old age, [or] death. The causes are *lobha* (greed), *dosa* (hate), and *moha* (delusion).**
- When you are doing *caṅkamana* (walking meditation) first you must arouse a sense of urgency.
- Thinking of this endless round of rebirth. Each step towards death. Then you should exclude all other thoughts and must stick to the present.
- With thoughts of *mettā*, supporting each other, harmony in mind to achieve this one goal, deliverance from all suffering, Nibbāna.
- When practicing *mettā*-*bhāvanā*, (loving kindness meditation), not just wishing long life and happiness, but wishing the highest happiness to end this suffering [for all beings].
- You must stay with the teacher in the jungle for the practice to establish firmly, so that you can face any situation in time to come.
- When you give into food and other things, craving increases and clinging, birth, old age and death.
- *Mettā-bhāvanā* and the impermanence of feelings, perceptions and consciousness.
- *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates).
- Read the *Dhammapada* verses, recite them, and learn by heart in Pāli certain verses that strike you.
- *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā)
- Mustn’t have inner conflict. Your father would like you to have a sound education. You can support each other.
- You must stick to a few *Suttas* and put them into practice.
### 3 February 1995

#### Morning

- *Majjhima Sutta* 125—*Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed), *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates).
- You must purify yourself. In other words, you become selfish—[that is,] retire to solitude to purify oneself in order to overcome the ‘I’ conceit and thereafter serve in a selfless way.
- A teacher generally has an idea of all the *Suttas*. So, he prescribes to the pupil, according to where the pupil is and where he has to get [to] and it is a step-by-step process.
- If the pupil is a layman and wants to meditate then he should start with *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā), the three ways of conduct.
- If he is just a layman, *Sigālaka Sutta* (DN31; to Sigālaka), etc.
- You must always start with the three protections<Footnote id="11">The Three Protections refer to the recollection of refuge in the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha.</Footnote> and with arousing of spiritual urgency, when beginning on *caṅkamana* (walking meditation) or any kind of *bhāvanā (meditation)* or spiritual endeavor.
- You must always try to curb and annihilate *lobha* (greed)*, dosa* (hate) *and moha* (delusion) by a suitable *kammaṭṭhāna* (object of meditation).
- One cannot rely on anything that is from outside that is happiness—only the inner peace.
- *Caṅkamana* (walking meditation) is sticking to the present without giving into thoughts from outside, this should be done with understanding and reflection of the situation of the body, how all activity is centered around supporting the impure body that is connected with all kinds of suffering—old age, sickness, and all kinds of illusive happiness.
- **Certain things are practiced for the benefit of future generations e.g. certain Vinaya rules, Venerable Mahākassapa was staying in solitude.**
- The three ways of conduct [*tīṇi sucaritāni*] done unremittingly and immaculately.
- **May you get all the protection in *saṁsāra*.**
#### Afternoon

- *Majjhima Sutta* *53—Sekha Sutta* (MN53; the disciple in higher training).
- It is for the monks, when you become a monk, it is useful, especially regarding the [spiritual] faculties [*Indriyas*].
- *Satipaṭṭhāna* leads to *jhānas*.
- Do this *caṅkamana* practice when you feel sleepy and control this hindrance.
- **All my blessings are there for your practice.**
- Always look at *Khandhasaṁyutta* and *Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta*. Look at it as it is.
- **Always stick to the present, don’t expect anything from the future nor regret or go back to the past. What you do in the present makes the future.**
- When you go on *caṅkamana* (walking meditation), think of this body: made up of defiled actions in the past, so impure and repulsive.<Footnote id="12">Ven. Gnanaloka explains that the body's impure nature arises from past defiled thoughts—due to greed, hate and delusion. It is a result of *kamma*, that this body has taken the form it has. “Why is it repulsive?” he asks. “Because it’s created by repulsive thoughts. He (Ven. Ñāṇavimala) means that you have to picture the reality of how gross it is.” The reflection is meant for clear seeing: one cannot observe what one is attached to. By reflecting upon the body’s gross nature, a necessary distance for insight is created—“because you cannot observe something that you are attached to.” When thoughts are more refined, the resulting *kamma* is also more refined, giving rise to subtler bodies of existence. Yet, all remain bound by the same law of arising and passing away.</Footnote>
- Read the *Dhammapada* verses. Memorise some.

### 16 February 1995

- *Bahussuta* [knowledgeable/learned] is important.<Footnote id="14">*Bahussuta* (Pāli) literally means “much-heard”—i.e., well-read or extensively acquainted with the *Suttas*. When Ven. Ñāṇavimala says “*Bahussuta* is important,” he is emphasizing that close and repeated reading of the Buddha’s discourses strengthens right view, supplies the practitioner with suitable objects for reflection, and supports practice.</Footnote>
- After practicing *bhāvanā* like in Rishikesh [India]. More ability to concentrate. Therefore, successful in worldly activity. But what you build up in the sensual world also comes down very fast.<Footnote id="15">Ven. Gnanaloka clarified that this was a reference to a trend, known at the time, of business leaders using yogic meditative concentration, developed in places like Rishikesh, for the purpose of worldly manifestation. Ven. Ñāṇavimala, who had direct experience with this concentration power from his time as a corporate director in his lay life, was adding the crucial teaching that all such achievements are ultimately impermanent and can “come down very fast.”</Footnote> “*sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā*” [all conditioned things are impermanent].
- As a layman you observe verbal and physical actions. As a monk higher *sīla* is first restrained by the *Pātimokkha* [monastic discipline] and *indriya saṁvara sīla* [sense restraint] and then thoughts, *ariya sīla* (noble virtue).
- You should always reflect on the impurities of the body, and the impermanence of food. Especially [reflect] if you vomit [it] out, or [if you have] some food still in your stomach undigested, and so on. And also, when you are taking food, [reflect on] the trouble involved in meeting lay people and just to support this body, with people working day and night for a body which is rushing to old age, sickness and death. But when you know that, one is able to remove the cause of suffering or the arising of this body**—**which is craving, by clear seeing or understanding, so that ultimately the craving will diminish and extinguish. The arising of new suffering will extinguish.
- One should retire with the teacher into the forest for *jhāna* to take place. Such an environment with less sense impressions to work with is necessary.
- During the day you should work in a *sīlavanta* [virtuous] way because this affects the sleep and meditation at the end of the day.
- Writing letters and so on by monks—it is not that you should forget your relatives.<Footnote id="16">Ven. Gnanaloka recalls an occasion when a visitor from Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s own hometown in Germany arrived at Polgasduwa. Although they were strangers, they shared the same roots—yet his teacher showed no special emotion or reaction. Ven. Gnanaloka notes this unwavering restraint as an example of relinquished bonds—reducing ties with all people and places, even those of profound personal significance.</Footnote>
- Even the Brahmā world is suffering. One may re-arise even in the hell realms.
- *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty).
- Sleep—you should try to sleep generally 5 to 6 hours. It is good to reduce it. You should allocate a specific time of the day for this. And at other times you must not allow sleepiness.
- You must share the Dhamma with your relatives, not by going behind them and talking, but by your own actions.
- This solid knowledge should arise. That is insight into Dhamma.
- Today more money is needed and people’s needs are more.
- When you are in retreat, you should have Dhamma thoughts.
- You mustn’t eat very little food or sleep too little; you may fall sick. But you should try to do it with little effort. You have to maintain this body and keep it in good shape.
- *Jhāna*; then *vipassanā*.<Footnote id="17">Ven. Gnanaloka recalls this was the summary in essence of Ven. Ñāṇavimala’s talk at the time: *Jhāna* helps *Vipassanā*. Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies that it doesn't mean that it is necessary to get *jhāna* and then insight. There are *Suttas* where they get insight without mention of *jhāna*. He continues to explain, in those cases, even if *jhāna* wasn't mentioned, the mind was still in a “*jhāna*-like” state—meaning the five hindrances were absent—otherwise the truth could not penetrate. An example is the *Susimaparibbājaka Sutta* (SN12.70; Susīma), which describes attainment of liberation through wisdom (*paññā-vimutta*) without possessing formless meditative attainments. The Buddha’s explanation to Susīma confirms that *Dhamma-ṭṭhiti-ñāṇa* (knowledge of the stability of the Dhamma) is the definitive requirement for Nibbāna. Generally, however, it is good to have sitting meditation and then reading.</Footnote>
- When you have wishes, and when they are fulfilled, the craving increases more, wish after wish will follow.
- Do not wait with certain ideas for a long time, even arahants discuss from time to time.<Footnote id="18">Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies, by giving the example that someone may even think they have become enlightened—don’t assume because it may also be wrong. He recommended that it is better to have discussions, which may give different perspectives on what you are experiencing.</Footnote>
### 2 March 1995

- **Supply the causes for the things that you wish to arise.**
- **Make sure the foundation is firmly established.**
- **Read *Majjhima* 125, 117, 107, 118—*Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed)*, Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty), *Gaṇakamoggallāna Sutta* (MN107; to Gaṇaka Moggallāna), *Ānāpānasati Sutta* (MN118; mindfulness of breathing).**
- ***Saḷāyatanasaṁyutta* (SN35; connected discourses on the six sense bases), *Khandhasaṁyutta* (SN22; connected discourses on the aggregates), *Bojjhaṅgasaṁyutta* (SN46; connected discourses on the factors of enlightenment)<Footnote id="19">*Bojjhaṅgasaṁyutta* (SN46; connected discourses on the factors of enlightenment): A section of the Connected Discourses where the Buddha teaches the Seven Factors of Awakening—mindfulness, investigation (clear seeing), energy, joy, calm, concentration, and equanimity.</Footnote> all in *Saṁyutta Nikāya*.**
- **Read *Word of the Buddha* [by Venerable Nyanatiloka Mahāthera] for *sammā diṭṭhi*.**
- **When you are here now, just don’t think to abide [in] *jhāna*, but try to overcome the five hindrances, then the mind will settle easily.**
- **[Then] you should concentrate to attain *jhāna*.**
- **You must do things slowly and thoroughly.**
- **In time to come when practicing *mettā bhāvanā*, pleasant feelings may arise. Look at it with equanimity, they are just feelings. All feelings are impermanent, suffering, not mine, and conditionally arisen.**
- **Equanimity is most important—not emotional feeling.**<Footnote id="20">Ven. Gnanaloka explains how Ven. Ñāṇavimala taught that equanimity is the highest conscious experience. He would say based on that you have the formless states.</Footnote>
- **Don’t worry, it will come.**
- **Regarding *bojjhaṅga* [factors of enlightenment]—this is towards true enlightenment. Not the way of the [ātman-believing] yogi, [which is] *micchā diṭṭhi* [wrong view] way.**<Footnote id="21">Here, “the way of the yogi” refers broadly to any contemplative practice—whether inside or outside historical Buddhist circles—that utilizes exalted states of consciousness or absorptions to unite with, or abide in a permanent ‘self’ or soul.While these practices can lead to profoundly peaceful, exalted states of consciousness, they are considered *micchā diṭṭhi* (wrong view) from the standpoint of ultimate awakening because they fail to see that all phenomena are *anatta* (void of a permanent identity). The *bojjhaṅgas* (factors of enlightenment) are unique because they do not build up a meditator's identity; instead, they dismantle the subtle tendency to cling to any state as “myself.”</Footnote>
- ***Bojjhaṅga* takes place when you are doing *bhāvanā*.**
- **Don’t try to have theoretical explanations, but instead experience.**
- **Don’t stick to an attainment, go on.**
- **You must change the *kammaṭṭhāna* (object of meditation) in order to tackle each defilement.**
- **When going on *caṅkamana*, control the thoughts, so that it sticks to one place. At that time *lobha* (greed)*, dosa* (hate) and *moha* (delusion) are reduced [the] sense of ‘I’ is going away, [leading] towards Nibbāna.**
- **Sometimes one is enthusiastic, sometimes one’s strength is going down. This will be the situation throughout life. So don’t worry—as long as the conditions are good, you may work as hard as possible, but not overdoing. Always the middle way.**
### 6 March 1995

- Don’t rely on anybody or any person or anything that is external. They can separate [from you] at any moment and are all unreliable. But depend on your own mind that is developed and the All Enlightened One as your guide.
- Observing the physical and verbal actions you do as a layman is known as *sīla*; [as] a monk, you go further to observe thoughts, and to bring them into control (mental actions), that is Ariya *sīla*.
- The physical and verbal actions also derive from the mental state (first verse in *Dhammapada—*Dhp 1).
- First look after your own welfare; it is not selfish. Bringing yourself to selflessness, then you can serve others in the highest way without selfish motives.
- First Right View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*) [theoretical foundation],<Footnote id="22">The original writes ‘(theoretical grasping),’ however, the meaning is to get a theoretical foundation.</Footnote> then the five hindrances<Footnote id="23">The five hindrances are mental obstacles that cloud understanding and obstruct concentration: *kāmacchanda* (sensual desire), *vyāpāda* (ill will), *thīna–middha* (sloth and torpor), *uddhacca–kukkucca* (restlessness and worry), and *vicikicchā* (doubt).</Footnote> should be checked and overcome; then *Satipaṭṭhāna*.
- *Suttas* 117, 125, 106 [of *Majjhima Nikāya*]—*Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty); *Dantabhūmi Sutta* (MN125; the grade of the tamed); *Āneñjasappāya Sutta* (MN106; the way to the imperturbable).
- Reading all the *Suttas*, memorising them, there is no point.Practice and experience. This is important.
- When we see the 32 parts of the body, and [by] not letting any worldly thought arise, one will know that one is directing towards overcoming suffering or Nibbāna.
### 8 March 1995

- All my blessings are there for all your endeavors.
- Never let the mind be disturbed by a certain situation or circumstance.
### 9 March 1995

- You must be firmly established in the Dhamma as a bhikkhu when you go out, where you will face all sorts of situations. Must think of the *Sutta* [relating to] the Simile of the Saw—*Kakacūpama Sutta* (MN21; the simile of the saw) and reflect that our *kamma* is behind us.
- First *sīla* (virtue) and then *jhāna* (meditative absorption) and then entering the path—*sotāpanna* (Stream-Enterer), *sakadāgāmī* (Once-Returner), *anāgāmī* (Non-Returner), arahant (Fully Enlightened One).
- Until arahantship (full enlightenment) there is this “I” conceit in a very subtle way.
- All lay people are like children, they need a father figure. But when the children grow up, they do not need one anymore. So don’t think of a particular person outside. Rely always on the All Enlightened One and the Dhamma that you have already mastered as your guide. Whenever effort is made and certain bliss is connected to that, take that as a strength to go on and develop.
- Do not teach the Dhamma for any worldly gain and so on.
- **Go on with compassion for beings with less understanding.**
- First give support to people who are close to you. But don’t try to convert or go behind [them], but [do so] with your own example**—**and if they are interested, you may help them accordingly. You may send to your friend in Cyprus the *Kesamutti Sutta* (AN3.65; Kesaputtiya). If [they] are more interested, a list of BPS [Buddhist Publication Society] books may be provided, and maybe point to particular books. Not [to] suggest this is Buddhism.
- Don’t have any ties. Try to reduce ties as much as possible.
- You must understand this situation. We have not always acted in a *kusala* (wholesome) way in the past. Never [be] displeased.<Footnote id="24">Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies, the idea is not to dwell or brood over troublesome life situations; know that they are the consequences of past *kamma* and those consequences will also pass. Rather one should take care not to create new unwholesome *kamma*.</Footnote>
- When unwholesome thoughts arise, don’t react with aversion or hate or fear, if reacting that way, it means one is directing the mind in that direction, therefore it increases.
- Don’t stick to an attainment or turn back from [putting] effort.
- *Suttas* to *Anamataggasaṁyutta* (SN15; connected discourses on without discoverable beginning). *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN117; the great forty); *Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta* (MN20; the removal of distracting thoughts) [to] deal with thoughts.
- Doctors are very busy, [with] more diseases, so more activity to cope up with, and they are generally highly considered by the society, for everybody is keen about good health. But in the Dhamma it is all mentioned: the root cause for sickness—that is immorality.<Footnote id="25">Ven. Gnanaloka clarifies: as people’s virtue (*sīla*) declines, sickness increases in the world. This relates to the principle that immoral conduct (*akusala kamma*) serves as a root cause for disease. The canonical connection between the decline of morality and the increase in sickness and shortening of lifespan is detailed in the *Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta* (DN26; the lion’s roar on the turning of the wheel).</Footnote>
- It is difficult to maintain *jhāna*, so you should do the second best by reading the *Suttas* etc.
- Live in the present, every pleasant present moment will bring happiness in the future, so never let the mind be displeased.
- Look at everything as a *bubble and mirage* (Dhp 170). There is no meaning in all this phenomenon.
- There is no ‘I’, there is no ‘me’.
- Daily, make sure all activities are done in such a way [that] there hasn’t been any harm done to anybody. Go to bed early. Before sleeping practice *mettā* *bhāvanā*. Do the activity with full concentration, slowly. And on *poya* days, you may retire in a suitable place and wait. All depends on your practice.
- All external support may come to an end finally. It is our *kamma* behind us, we have not always acted in a wholesome way.
- All [your] relatives and family have a lot of *saddhā* due to past lives.
- **You may give support to your family, relatives, and all others, not by trying to convert them, but by your example. It is the best way.**
- You practice in solitude and go [out] to the world, in order to fully establish in the practice of *Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovāda Sutta* (MN61; advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā) in the lay life, so that a sound basis and a foundation is established.
- **Based on the way we look from inside; the world is formed from the outside.**
- Anyway, we cannot change things outside.
- When going on *caṅkamana* (walking meditation), see the suffering we have to go through to maintain this meaningless process: coming in contact with disturbing sense impressions and the food we [consume]. [And see the] danger in it—for it [strengthens] the cause for this process that is rushing toward old age, sickness, [and] death—this craving. Yet, one should be happy that we know the root cause of this suffering: it is this craving.
- Building houses and so forth for the body etc.
- When disturbing thoughts come, *Satipaṭṭhāna* practice will be good.
